## Background

Margery Williams was born in London and later lived in the United States. The Velveteen Rabbit, or How Toys Become Real, was published in 1922, and it has been a favourite picture book ever since.

The story is about a toy rabbit made of velveteen, a soft cloth. A boy receives him as a Christmas present, but soon forgets him for more exciting toys. In the nursery, the playroom where the toys live, the expensive mechanical toys look down on the Rabbit. The only toy who is kind to him is the old Skin Horse, a toy horse covered in real skin. In this excerpt, the Rabbit asks him a big question. As you read, think about what "Real" means in this story, and whether the Skin Horse's answer is only about toys.

## Excerpt

For a long time he lived in the toy cupboard or on the nursery floor, and no one thought very much about him. He was naturally shy, and being only made of velveteen, some of the more expensive toys quite snubbed him. The mechanical toys were very superior, and looked down upon every one else; they were full of modern ideas, and pretended they were real. The model boat, who had lived through two seasons and lost most of his paint, caught the tone from them and never missed an opportunity of referring to his rigging in technical terms. The Rabbit could not claim to be a model of anything, for he didn't know that real rabbits existed; he thought they were all stuffed with sawdust like himself, and he understood that sawdust was quite out-of-date and should never be mentioned in modern circles. Even Timothy, the jointed wooden lion, who was made by the disabled soldiers, and should have had broader views, put on airs and pretended he was connected with Government. Between them all the poor little Rabbit was made to feel himself very insignificant and commonplace, and the only person who was kind to him at all was the Skin Horse.

The Skin Horse had lived longer in the nursery than any of the others. He was so old that his brown coat was bald in patches and showed the seams underneath, and most of the hairs in his tail had been pulled out to string bead necklaces. He was wise, for he had seen a long succession of mechanical toys arrive to boast and swagger, and by-and-by break their mainsprings and pass away, and he knew that they were only toys, and would never turn into anything else. For nursery magic is very strange and wonderful, and only those playthings that are old and wise and experienced like the Skin Horse understand all about it.

"What is REAL?" asked the Rabbit one day, when they were lying side by side near the nursery fender, before Nana came to tidy the room. "Does it mean having things that buzz inside you and a stick-out handle?"

"Real isn't how you are made," said the Skin Horse. "It's a thing that happens to you. When a child loves you for a long, long time, not just to play with, but REALLY loves you, then you become Real."

"Does it hurt?" asked the Rabbit.

"Sometimes," said the Skin Horse, for he was always truthful. "When you are Real you don't mind being hurt."

"Does it happen all at once, like being wound up," he asked, "or bit by bit?"

"It doesn't happen all at once," said the Skin Horse. "You become. It takes a long time. That's why it doesn't happen often to people who break easily, or have sharp edges, or who have to be carefully kept. Generally, by the time you are Real, most of your hair has been loved off, and your eyes drop out and you get loose in the joints and very shabby. But these things don't matter at all, because once you are Real you can't be ugly, except to people who don't understand."

"I suppose you are real?" said the Rabbit. And then he wished he had not said it, for he thought the Skin Horse might be sensitive. But the Skin Horse only smiled.

## Appreciation

The first part of the excerpt is a small satire of snobbery. The mechanical toys "were full of modern ideas, and pretended they were real". The model boat talks about his rigging "in technical terms" to sound important. Even the wooden lion "put on airs". Williams describes the toy world like a society of proud people, and we laugh because we know people like this.

The Skin Horse is the opposite. He is old and worn; his coat is "bald in patches". But he is wise because he has watched many proud toys "boast and swagger" and then break. Williams gives him the voice of a calm, patient grandparent.

The famous conversation is very simple. The Rabbit asks short questions, and the Skin Horse gives short, honest answers. "Real isn't how you are made… It's a thing that happens to you." Real comes from being loved "for a long, long time".

The most moving idea is that love leaves marks: "most of your hair has been loved off". The phrase "loved off" is unusual and beautiful. Being loved makes you shabby, but "once you are Real you can't be ugly, except to people who don't understand". Many adults read this as a message about human relationships and growing old. Real love is not about looking perfect.

Notice the line about people "who break easily, or have sharp edges". These describe toys, but also people who cannot accept love. The story is short, but its ideas are deep.

## Sentences to imitate

> “Real isn't how you are made,” said the Skin Horse. “It's a thing that happens to you.”
A definition by contrast: "isn't… It's…". Good for explaining ideas.

> “When you are Real you don't mind being hurt.”
"Don't mind + -ing" means something is not a problem for you.

> “It doesn't happen all at once,” said the Skin Horse. “You become. It takes a long time.”
Very short sentences make a wise idea memorable.

> But these things don't matter at all, because once you are Real you can't be ugly, except to people who don't understand.
"Once…" means "after something happens". "Except to…" adds an important exception.

## Notes

- velveteen | A cheap cotton cloth that looks like velvet. | 棉绒布
- snubbed | Treated someone as unimportant and ignored them. | 冷落
- rigging | The ropes that hold up the sails of a boat. | 索具
- sawdust | Tiny pieces of wood made by cutting it, used for stuffing toys. | 锯末
- airs | "Put on airs" means behave as if you are more important than you are. | 装腔作势
- swagger | Walk or behave in a proud, showy way. | 大摇大摆；自吹自擂
- mainsprings | The main springs that make clockwork toys move. | 发条
- fender | Here a low metal frame in front of a fireplace. | 壁炉挡板
- wound | Here "wound up" means turned with a key to make a toy move. | 上（发条）
- shabby | Old and worn. | 破旧的
