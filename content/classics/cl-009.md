## Background

This is the end of A Tale of Two Cities (1859), so it reveals how the novel finishes. Sydney Carton is a clever English lawyer who drinks too much and believes his life is wasted. He has long loved Lucie Manette, but she married Charles Darnay, a French aristocrat who looks very much like Carton.

During the French Revolution, Darnay was sentenced to death in Paris. Carton used their likeness to change places with him in prison. Now Carton has been taken to the guillotine in Darnay's place, so that Lucie, her husband and their child can escape to England. In this final scene, Dickens imagines what Carton might have thought in his last moments. Notice the word "If": we do not hear Carton actually speak. As you read, count how many times the same two words begin a sentence.

## Excerpt

They said of him, about the city that night, that it was the peacefullest man’s face ever beheld there. Many added that he looked sublime and prophetic.

One of the most remarkable sufferers by the same axe—a woman—had asked at the foot of the same scaffold, not long before, to be allowed to write down the thoughts that were inspiring her. If he had given any utterance to his, and they were prophetic, they would have been these:

“I see Barsad, and Cly, Defarge, The Vengeance, the Juryman, the Judge, long ranks of the new oppressors who have risen on the destruction of the old, perishing by this retributive instrument, before it shall cease out of its present use. I see a beautiful city and a brilliant people rising from this abyss, and, in their struggles to be truly free, in their triumphs and defeats, through long years to come, I see the evil of this time and of the previous time of which this is the natural birth, gradually making expiation for itself and wearing out.

“I see the lives for which I lay down my life, peaceful, useful, prosperous and happy, in that England which I shall see no more. I see Her with a child upon her bosom, who bears my name. I see her father, aged and bent, but otherwise restored, and faithful to all men in his healing office, and at peace. I see the good old man, so long their friend, in ten years’ time enriching them with all he has, and passing tranquilly to his reward.

“I see that I hold a sanctuary in their hearts, and in the hearts of their descendants, generations hence. I see her, an old woman, weeping for me on the anniversary of this day. I see her and her husband, their course done, lying side by side in their last earthly bed, and I know that each was not more honoured and held sacred in the other’s soul, than I was in the souls of both.

“I see that child who lay upon her bosom and who bore my name, a man winning his way up in that path of life which once was mine. I see him winning it so well, that my name is made illustrious there by the light of his. I see the blots I threw upon it, faded away. I see him, fore-most of just judges and honoured men, bringing a boy of my name, with a forehead that I know and golden hair, to this place—then fair to look upon, with not a trace of this day’s disfigurement—and I hear him tell the child my story, with a tender and a faltering voice.

“It is a far, far better thing that I do, than I have ever done; it is a far, far better rest that I go to than I have ever known.”

## Appreciation

The structure of this ending is simple and very powerful. Almost every sentence begins with "I see". This repetition at the start of sentences is called anaphora. It turns Carton's thoughts into something like a prophecy or a prayer. Each "I see" moves further into the future: first the end of the Revolution, then Lucie's family, then their old age, and finally a grandson hearing Carton's story.

Dickens is careful to make this an imagined speech. The narrator says that if Carton had written his thoughts down, "they would have been these". This lets Dickens give Carton a voice without breaking the realism of the scene. It also links Carton to another victim, a woman who asked to write her thoughts at the scaffold.

Notice the order of the visions. They start with Carton's enemies being punished, but they soon become gentle and personal. The words become softer: "peaceful, useful, prosperous and happy". The most moving idea is that Carton's name, once full of "blots", will be made "illustrious" by the child who carries it. His life will have meaning through others.

The final sentence is one of the most famous in English. It uses a balanced pair: "a far, far better thing that I do" and "a far, far better rest that I go to". The repeated "far, far" slows the rhythm, like a last breath. "Thing that I do" refers to his action; "rest that I go to" refers to death. The book ends with peace, not horror.

## Sentences to imitate

> They said of him, about the city that night, that it was the peacefullest man’s face ever beheld there.
Reporting what other people said creates distance and respect. The unusual form "peacefullest" makes the word stand out.

> I see the lives for which I lay down my life, peaceful, useful, prosperous and happy, in that England which I shall see no more.
A list of four positive adjectives, then a sad ending. The contrast between "I see" and "I shall see no more" is moving.

> I see him winning it so well, that my name is made illustrious there by the light of his.
A "so… that" structure with an image of light. A useful pattern for showing how one thing causes another.

> It is a far, far better thing that I do, than I have ever done; it is a far, far better rest that I go to than I have ever known.
Two balanced halves joined by a semicolon. Repetition ("far, far") and parallel structure give a speech its rhythm.

## Notes

- peacefullest | Dickens's unusual form of "most peaceful". | 最安详的（most peaceful 的非常规形式）
- beheld | Past tense of "behold": seen, looked at. | 看见（behold 的过去式）
- sublime | So noble or beautiful that it fills you with wonder. | 崇高的；庄严的
- utterance | Here it means expression in words. | 说出；表达
- retributive | Given as punishment that someone deserves. | 惩罚性的；报应的
- instrument | Here it means the guillotine, a tool for killing. | 刑具（此指断头台）
- expiation | Making up for a wrong or sin. | 赎罪；抵偿
- office | Here it means a job or duty (the doctor's healing work). | 职责；职务
- hence | Here "generations hence" means many generations from now. | 从今以后
- illustrious | Famous and greatly respected. | 杰出的；著名的
- blots | Marks of shame on someone's name or reputation. | 污点
