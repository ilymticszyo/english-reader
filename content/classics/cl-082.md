## Background

Virginia Woolf was an English novelist, essayist and one of the leading figures of modernism, the movement that changed literature in the early twentieth century. Mrs. Dalloway (1925) takes place on a single day in June 1923 in London, five years after the end of the First World War.

Clarissa Dalloway is a wealthy woman in her fifties, married to a politician. She is preparing for a party she will give that evening. The novel follows her thoughts, and the thoughts of other people, especially Septimus Smith, a former soldier suffering from shell shock. This excerpt is the famous opening. Clarissa walks out into the streets of Westminster. As you read, notice that the story moves freely between the present moment and her memories of Bourton, her family's country house, where as a girl she knew Peter Walsh, who once loved her.

## Excerpt

Mrs. Dalloway said she would buy the flowers herself.

For Lucy had her work cut out for her. The doors would be taken off their hinges; Rumpelmayer’s men were coming. And then, thought Clarissa Dalloway, what a morning—fresh as if issued to children on a beach.

What a lark! What a plunge! For so it had always seemed to her, when, with a little squeak of the hinges, which she could hear now, she had burst open the French windows and plunged at Bourton into the open air. How fresh, how calm, stiller than this of course, the air was in the early morning; like the flap of a wave; the kiss of a wave; chill and sharp and yet (for a girl of eighteen as she then was) solemn, feeling as she did, standing there at the open window, that something awful was about to happen; looking at the flowers, at the trees with the smoke winding off them and the rooks rising, falling; standing and looking until Peter Walsh said, “Musing among the vegetables?”—was that it?—“I prefer men to cauliflowers”—was that it? He must have said it at breakfast one morning when she had gone out on to the terrace—Peter Walsh. He would be back from India one of these days, June or July, she forgot which, for his letters were awfully dull; it was his sayings one remembered; his eyes, his pocket-knife, his smile, his grumpiness and, when millions of things had utterly vanished—how strange it was!—a few sayings like this about cabbages.

She stiffened a little on the kerb, waiting for Durtnall’s van to pass. A charming woman, Scrope Purvis thought her (knowing her as one does know people who live next door to one in Westminster); a touch of the bird about her, of the jay, blue-green, light, vivacious, though she was over fifty, and grown very white since her illness. There she perched, never seeing him, waiting to cross, very upright.

For having lived in Westminster—how many years now? over twenty,—one feels even in the midst of the traffic, or waking at night, Clarissa was positive, a particular hush, or solemnity; an indescribable pause; a suspense (but that might be her heart, affected, they said, by influenza) before Big Ben strikes. There! Out it boomed. First a warning, musical; then the hour, irrevocable. The leaden circles dissolved in the air. Such fools we are, she thought, crossing Victoria Street. For Heaven only knows why one loves it so, how one sees it so, making it up, building it round one, tumbling it, creating it every moment afresh; but the veriest frumps, the most dejected of miseries sitting on doorsteps (drink their downfall) do the same; can’t be dealt with, she felt positive, by Acts of Parliament for that very reason: they love life. In people’s eyes, in the swing, tramp, and trudge; in the bellow and the uproar; the carriages, motor cars, omnibuses, vans, sandwich men shuffling and swinging; brass bands; barrel organs; in the triumph and the jingle and the strange high singing of some aeroplane overhead was what she loved; life; London; this moment of June.

## Appreciation

Woolf uses a technique often called "stream of consciousness", which follows the movement of a character's thoughts. Notice how quickly the first page leaves the real action. Clarissa says she will buy the flowers, and almost at once the fresh morning reminds her of another morning thirty years ago at Bourton.

The memory begins with exclamations: "What a lark! What a plunge!" These words are joyful and physical, and they also fit the image of jumping into the sea. Woolf repeats the image: the air was "like the flap of a wave; the kiss of a wave". The semicolons create a flowing rhythm, like waves or breathing.

Memory is shown as imperfect. Clarissa is not sure what Peter said: "Musing among the vegetables?"—was that it? Woolf captures the strange way that "millions of things had utterly vanished" while a few small sayings remain.

Then the point of view suddenly moves to a neighbour, Scrope Purvis, who sees Clarissa as "a touch of the bird about her, of the jay". For a moment we see her from outside: "over fifty, and grown very white since her illness". This shift reminds us that every person is seen differently by others.

Big Ben's chime is one of the novel's key images. "First a warning, musical; then the hour, irrevocable." Time passes, and nothing can bring it back. Yet the paragraph ends with a long, joyful list of London sounds, closing with "life; London; this moment of June." Woolf turns an ordinary walk into a celebration of being alive, all the more precious after the war.

## Sentences to imitate

> Mrs. Dalloway said she would buy the flowers herself.
A simple, famous opening line that leads into a complex inner world.

> What a lark! What a plunge!
Short exclamations to express sudden joy.

> There she perched, never seeing him, waiting to cross, very upright.
Inversion with "There she…" and participle phrases to create a clear image.

> First a warning, musical; then the hour, irrevocable.
A balanced pair of phrases without verbs, giving a strong rhythm.

- rumpelmayer’s | Rumpelmayer's was a fashionable London firm (the men will decorate for the party). | 伦佩尔迈尔公司的

- musing | Thinking deeply and quietly. | 沉思
- kerb | The edge of a pavement next to the road. | 路缘
- jay | A colourful, noisy bird. | 松鸦
- vivacious | Lively and attractive. | 活泼的
- irrevocable | Impossible to change or undo. | 不可挽回的

- veriest | Most complete (old, literary). | 十足的
