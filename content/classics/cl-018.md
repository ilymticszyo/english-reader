## Background

The Hound of the Baskervilles (1901–1902) is the most famous Sherlock Holmes novel, and this excerpt shows its climax. There is an old legend that a huge, devilish hound has hunted the men of the Baskerville family for centuries. Sir Charles Baskerville was found dead on the moor in Devon, with the footprints of a gigantic dog nearby. Now his heir, Sir Henry, has come to live at Baskerville Hall.

Holmes believes that a real person is using the legend to commit murder. He has let Sir Henry walk home alone across the moor at night, as bait. Holmes, Watson and Inspector Lestrade are lying hidden near the path. But a thick white fog has started to roll across the moor, and they cannot see. As you read, notice how Watson describes the hound. Is it natural or supernatural? Watch how the answer changes.

## Excerpt

“Hist!” cried Holmes, and I heard the sharp click of a cocking pistol. “Look out! It’s coming!”

There was a thin, crisp, continuous patter from somewhere in the heart of that crawling bank. The cloud was within fifty yards of where we lay, and we glared at it, all three, uncertain what horror was about to break from the heart of it. I was at Holmes’s elbow, and I glanced for an instant at his face. It was pale and exultant, his eyes shining brightly in the moonlight. But suddenly they started forward in a rigid, fixed stare, and his lips parted in amazement. At the same instant Lestrade gave a yell of terror and threw himself face downward upon the ground. I sprang to my feet, my inert hand grasping my pistol, my mind paralyzed by the dreadful shape which had sprung out upon us from the shadows of the fog. A hound it was, an enormous coal-black hound, but not such a hound as mortal eyes have ever seen. Fire burst from its open mouth, its eyes glowed with a smouldering glare, its muzzle and hackles and dewlap were outlined in flickering flame. Never in the delirious dream of a disordered brain could anything more savage, more appalling, more hellish be conceived than that dark form and savage face which broke upon us out of the wall of fog.

With long bounds the huge black creature was leaping down the track, following hard upon the footsteps of our friend. So paralyzed were we by the apparition that we allowed him to pass before we had recovered our nerve. Then Holmes and I both fired together, and the creature gave a hideous howl, which showed that one at least had hit him. He did not pause, however, but bounded onward. Far away on the path we saw Sir Henry looking back, his face white in the moonlight, his hands raised in horror, glaring helplessly at the frightful thing which was hunting him down. But that cry of pain from the hound had blown all our fears to the winds. If he was vulnerable he was mortal, and if we could wound him we could kill him. Never have I seen a man run as Holmes ran that night. I am reckoned fleet of foot, but he outpaced me as much as I outpaced the little professional. In front of us as we flew up the track we heard scream after scream from Sir Henry and the deep roar of the hound. I was in time to see the beast spring upon its victim, hurl him to the ground, and worry at his throat. But the next instant Holmes had emptied five barrels of his revolver into the creature’s flank. With a last howl of agony and a vicious snap in the air, it rolled upon its back, four feet pawing furiously, and then fell limp upon its side. I stooped, panting, and pressed my pistol to the dreadful, shimmering head, but it was useless to press the trigger. The giant hound was dead.

Sir Henry lay insensible where he had fallen. We tore away his collar, and Holmes breathed a prayer of gratitude when we saw that there was no sign of a wound and that the rescue had been in time. Already our friend’s eyelids shivered and he made a feeble effort to move. Lestrade thrust his brandy-flask between the baronet’s teeth, and two frightened eyes were looking up at us.

“My God!” he whispered. “What was it? What, in heaven’s name, was it?”

“It’s dead, whatever it is,” said Holmes. “We’ve laid the family ghost once and forever.”

In mere size and strength it was a terrible creature which was lying stretched before us. It was not a pure bloodhound and it was not a pure mastiff; but it appeared to be a combination of the two—gaunt, savage, and as large as a small lioness. Even now in the stillness of death, the huge jaws seemed to be dripping with a bluish flame and the small, deep-set, cruel eyes were ringed with fire. I placed my hand upon the glowing muzzle, and as I held them up my own fingers smouldered and gleamed in the darkness.

## Appreciation

The excerpt begins with sound, just as the danger is still hidden: "a thin, crisp, continuous patter" inside the fog. Three adjectives before a noun build tension. Then Watson looks at Holmes's face, and through Holmes's reaction we know that something terrible has appeared before we see it ourselves.

The description of the hound is the most famous passage in the book. Conan Doyle first gives a simple fact, "A hound it was", with the object placed first for emphasis. Then he adds, "but not such a hound as mortal eyes have ever seen". The details are full of fire: "fire burst from its open mouth", its eyes "glowed", its head was "outlined in flickering flame". The sentence that follows uses a list of three ("more savage, more appalling, more hellish") to show that Watson's imagination can find nothing worse.

The turning point is a single sound: the hound howls with pain. Watson immediately reasons like Holmes: "If he was vulnerable he was mortal, and if we could wound him we could kill him." This balanced sentence turns fear into action. The supernatural legend becomes a real animal that can be killed.

The action is then fast and physical, with strong verbs: "spring", "hurl", "worry", "emptied", "rolled". The last paragraph slows down again and explains the mystery in a calm, almost scientific tone. The beast is a mix of a bloodhound and a mastiff, and the "flame" is something on its muzzle that glows on Watson's fingers. The ghost story has become a detective story again.

## Sentences to imitate

> A hound it was, an enormous coal-black hound, but not such a hound as mortal eyes have ever seen.
Putting the object first ("A hound it was") gives emphasis. Then the sentence corrects itself to say it is more than a normal hound.

> Never in the delirious dream of a disordered brain could anything more savage, more appalling, more hellish be conceived than that dark form and savage face
Starting with "Never" and inverting the verb makes the sentence dramatic. The list of three comparatives builds to a climax.

> If he was vulnerable he was mortal, and if we could wound him we could kill him.
Two parallel "if" clauses show quick, logical thinking. A useful pattern for a moment of decision.

> Never have I seen a man run as Holmes ran that night.
Negative inversion ("Never have I…") is a strong way to say that something was exceptional.

## Notes

- hist | An old sound used to say "listen!" or "be quiet!". | 嘘！听！
- patter | A series of quick, light tapping sounds. | 嗒嗒声
- exultant | Very happy and excited because of success. | 狂喜的；得意的
- inert | Not moving. | 不动的；无力的
- hackles | The hairs on the back of a dog's neck. | （狗）颈背的毛
- dewlap | Loose skin hanging under an animal's throat. | 喉部垂肉
- delirious | Confused and excited, as in a high fever. | 精神错乱的
- fleet | Here "fleet of foot" means a fast runner. | 快速的
- worry | Here it means bite and shake something with the teeth, like a dog. | 撕咬
- flank | The side of an animal's body. | 侧腹
- insensible | Unconscious. | 失去知觉的
- baronet | A British title, below a lord; Sir Henry is a baronet. | 准男爵
- laid | Here "laid the ghost" means stopped a ghost from appearing again. | 驱除（鬼魂）
- mastiff | A very large, strong breed of dog. | 獒犬
