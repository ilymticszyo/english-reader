## Background

Charlotte Brontë published Jane Eyre in 1847 under a man's name, Currer Bell. The story is told by Jane herself, a poor orphan who becomes a governess at Thornfield Hall. Over time she falls in love with her employer, Mr. Rochester, a moody and powerful man. Everyone believes that he will soon marry Blanche Ingram, a rich and beautiful lady.

In this chapter Rochester has been teasing Jane, telling her that she must leave Thornfield and find a new job in Ireland. Now they are alone in the garden on a summer evening, and Jane can no longer hide her feelings. As you read, notice how Jane speaks to her master. A servant was not supposed to talk like this. Look at her questions, her repetitions and her claim to be his equal. Ask yourself why Rochester keeps saying "be still".

## Excerpt

“In the shape of Miss Ingram; a noble and beautiful woman,—your bride.”

“My bride! What bride? I have no bride!”

“But you will have.”

“Yes;—I will!—I will!” He set his teeth.

“Then I must go:—you have said it yourself.”

“No: you must stay! I swear it—and the oath shall be kept.”

“I tell you I must go!” I retorted, roused to something like passion. “Do you think I can stay to become nothing to you? Do you think I am an automaton?—a machine without feelings? and can bear to have my morsel of bread snatched from my lips, and my drop of living water dashed from my cup? Do you think, because I am poor, obscure, plain, and little, I am soulless and heartless? You think wrong!—I have as much soul as you,—and full as much heart! And if God had gifted me with some beauty and much wealth, I should have made it as hard for you to leave me, as it is now for me to leave you. I am not talking to you now through the medium of custom, conventionalities, nor even of mortal flesh;—it is my spirit that addresses your spirit; just as if both had passed through the grave, and we stood at God’s feet, equal,—as we are!”

“As we are!” repeated Mr. Rochester—“so,” he added, enclosing me in his arms, gathering me to his breast, pressing his lips on my lips: “so, Jane!”

“Yes, so, sir,” I rejoined: “and yet not so; for you are a married man—or as good as a married man, and wed to one inferior to you—to one with whom you have no sympathy—whom I do not believe you truly love; for I have seen and heard you sneer at her. I would scorn such a union: therefore I am better than you—let me go!”

“Where, Jane? To Ireland?”

“Yes—to Ireland. I have spoken my mind, and can go anywhere now.”

“Jane, be still; don’t struggle so, like a wild frantic bird that is rending its own plumage in its desperation.”

“I am no bird; and no net ensnares me; I am a free human being with an independent will, which I now exert to leave you.”

Another effort set me at liberty, and I stood erect before him.

“And your will shall decide your destiny,” he said: “I offer you my hand, my heart, and a share of all my possessions.”

“You play a farce, which I merely laugh at.”

“I ask you to pass through life at my side—to be my second self, and best earthly companion.”

“For that fate you have already made your choice, and must abide by it.”

“Jane, be still a few moments: you are over-excited: I will be still too.”

A waft of wind came sweeping down the laurel-walk, and trembled through the boughs of the chestnut: it wandered away—away—to an indefinite distance—it died. The nightingale’s song was then the only voice of the hour: in listening to it, I again wept. Mr. Rochester sat quiet, looking at me gently and seriously. Some time passed before he spoke; he at last said—

“Come to my side, Jane, and let us explain and understand one another.”

“I will never again come to your side: I am torn away now, and cannot return.”

“But, Jane, I summon you as my wife: it is you only I intend to marry.”

I was silent: I thought he mocked me.

“Come, Jane—come hither.”

“Your bride stands between us.”

## Appreciation

This scene is famous because a woman in a low position speaks to a powerful man as his equal. Jane's long speech is the heart of the excerpt. It is built on questions: "Do you think I can stay to become nothing to you? Do you think I am an automaton?" Repeating the same opening ("Do you think…") is called anaphora, and it gives her words the rhythm of a speech. Each question pushes harder than the one before.

The most powerful question uses a list: "because I am poor, obscure, plain, and little". These are four ways in which society thinks she is worth less. Then she answers sharply: "You think wrong!" The short sentence after the long list feels like a door slamming.

Her argument is not only about love; it is about the soul. She says that her "spirit" speaks to his, as if they were both dead and standing before God, "equal,—as we are!" For a Victorian novel, this was a bold claim about class and gender.

Then comes the line everyone remembers: "I am no bird; and no net ensnares me". Rochester has just compared her to a wild bird, and she takes his image and turns it against him. This is a good model for an argument: use your opponent's own words. Notice also the calm description of the wind and the nightingale. After all the shouting, Brontë slows down and lets nature fill the silence before Rochester speaks again.

## Sentences to imitate

> Do you think, because I am poor, obscure, plain, and little, I am soulless and heartless?
A list of four adjectives answered by two strong opposites. A powerful pattern for a persuasive question.

> I have as much soul as you,—and full as much heart!
A comparison with "as much… as" repeated for emphasis. The second half adds force without a new idea.

> I am no bird; and no net ensnares me; I am a free human being with an independent will
Taking someone else's metaphor and turning it around. The three parts joined by semicolons build to the main idea.

> A waft of wind came sweeping down the laurel-walk, and trembled through the boughs of the chestnut: it wandered away—away—to an indefinite distance—it died.
After a dramatic scene, a quiet description slows the rhythm. The dashes make the wind seem to fade as you read.
