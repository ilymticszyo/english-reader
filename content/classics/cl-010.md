## Background

Charles Dickens published Great Expectations as a weekly serial in 1860 and 1861. It is told by Philip Pirrip, called Pip, who looks back on his life as an adult. He grew up as an orphan in a village in the Kent marshes, living with his bad-tempered older sister and her kind husband, Joe, the village blacksmith.

This excerpt comes from the first chapter. The young Pip is alone in the churchyard, looking at the graves of his parents and his five baby brothers. Then something terrible happens. As you read, notice the two voices in the text. One is the frightened child. The other is the grown-up narrator, who sometimes uses long, humorous sentences to describe what he felt. Look also at the stranger's way of speaking. His English is rough and shows that he is not an educated man.

## Excerpt

Ours was the marsh country, down by the river, within, as the river wound, twenty miles of the sea. My first most vivid and broad impression of the identity of things seems to me to have been gained on a memorable raw afternoon towards evening. At such a time I found out for certain that this bleak place overgrown with nettles was the churchyard; and that Philip Pirrip, late of this parish, and also Georgiana wife of the above, were dead and buried; and that Alexander, Bartholomew, Abraham, Tobias, and Roger, infant children of the aforesaid, were also dead and buried; and that the dark flat wilderness beyond the churchyard, intersected with dikes and mounds and gates, with scattered cattle feeding on it, was the marshes; and that the low leaden line beyond was the river; and that the distant savage lair from which the wind was rushing was the sea; and that the small bundle of shivers growing afraid of it all and beginning to cry, was Pip.

“Hold your noise!” cried a terrible voice, as a man started up from among the graves at the side of the church porch. “Keep still, you little devil, or I’ll cut your throat!”

A fearful man, all in coarse grey, with a great iron on his leg. A man with no hat, and with broken shoes, and with an old rag tied round his head. A man who had been soaked in water, and smothered in mud, and lamed by stones, and cut by flints, and stung by nettles, and torn by briars; who limped, and shivered, and glared, and growled; and whose teeth chattered in his head as he seized me by the chin.

“Oh! Don’t cut my throat, sir,” I pleaded in terror. “Pray don’t do it, sir.”

“Tell us your name!” said the man. “Quick!”

“Pip, sir.”

“Once more,” said the man, staring at me. “Give it mouth!”

“Pip. Pip, sir.”

“Show us where you live,” said the man. “Pint out the place!”

I pointed to where our village lay, on the flat in-shore among the alder-trees and pollards, a mile or more from the church.

The man, after looking at me for a moment, turned me upside down, and emptied my pockets. There was nothing in them but a piece of bread. When the church came to itself,—for he was so sudden and strong that he made it go head over heels before me, and I saw the steeple under my feet,—when the church came to itself, I say, I was seated on a high tombstone, trembling while he ate the bread ravenously.

“You young dog,” said the man, licking his lips, “what fat cheeks you ha’ got.”

I believe they were fat, though I was at that time undersized for my years, and not strong.

“Darn me if I couldn’t eat ’em,” said the man, with a threatening shake of his head, “and if I han’t half a mind to’t!”

I earnestly expressed my hope that he wouldn’t, and held tighter to the tombstone on which he had put me; partly, to keep myself upon it; partly, to keep myself from crying.

“Now lookee here!” said the man. “Where’s your mother?”

“There, sir!” said I.

He started, made a short run, and stopped and looked over his shoulder.

“There, sir!” I timidly explained. “Also Georgiana. That’s my mother.”

“Oh!” said he, coming back. “And is that your father alonger your mother?”

“Yes, sir,” said I; “him too; late of this parish.”

“Ha!” he muttered then, considering. “Who d’ye live with,—supposin’ you’re kindly let to live, which I han’t made up my mind about?”

“My sister, sir,—Mrs. Joe Gargery,—wife of Joe Gargery, the blacksmith, sir.”

“Blacksmith, eh?” said he. And looked down at his leg.

## Appreciation

The first paragraph is a single long sentence for most of its length, joined by "and that… and that… and that". The adult narrator lists everything the child "found out for certain" that afternoon: the churchyard, his dead family, the marshes, the river and the sea. The list grows wider, like a camera moving away. Then, at the very end, it comes back to a tiny figure: "the small bundle of shivers… was Pip." This sudden change of size makes the child seem very small and alone in a huge, empty world.

Then the convict appears, and the style changes completely. "Hold your noise!" is a shock after the long, calm sentence. Dickens describes the man in sentences without main verbs: "A fearful man, all in coarse grey, with a great iron on his leg." This technique feels like a series of quick pictures. The next sentence uses many passive verbs: he had been "soaked", "smothered", "lamed", "cut", "stung" and "torn". Everything in nature has hurt him, which helps us feel a little sorry for him, even though he is frightening.

Dickens also mixes fear with comedy. The church turns "head over heels" because the man has turned Pip upside down. The convict says he could eat Pip's fat cheeks. The adult narrator calmly reports that Pip "earnestly expressed" his hope that he wouldn't. The polite language for a ridiculous situation is typical of Dickens's humour, and it makes the scene unforgettable.

## Sentences to imitate

> and that the small bundle of shivers growing afraid of it all and beginning to cry, was Pip.
After a long list of big places, the sentence ends on a small human figure. A great way to show how alone someone feels.

> A fearful man, all in coarse grey, with a great iron on his leg.
A sentence without a main verb, like a photograph. Useful for a sudden, strong first impression.

> who limped, and shivered, and glared, and growled; and whose teeth chattered in his head as he seized me by the chin.
A row of verbs joined by "and" gives the feeling of continuous movement and danger.

> I earnestly expressed my hope that he wouldn’t, and held tighter to the tombstone on which he had put me
Formal, polite language used for a funny, frightening moment. The contrast creates gentle humour.

## Notes

- raw | Here it means cold and wet (weather), not "uncooked". | 阴冷潮湿的
- late | Here "late of this parish" means "who used to live here and is now dead". | 已故的
- aforesaid | Mentioned before (a legal word). | 上述的
- dikes | Ditches or banks built to control water on low land. | 堤坝；沟渠
- leaden | Dull grey, like the metal lead. | 铅灰色的
- lair | The home of a wild animal; here, the place the wind comes from. | 兽穴；巢穴
- iron | Here an iron ring and chain fixed to a prisoner's leg. | 脚镣
- pint => point | The convict's way of saying "point". | = point（方言发音）
- pollards | Trees whose tops have been cut so that they grow thick and short. | 截去树冠的树
- ha | In "you ha' got" it is the convict's short form of "have"; "Ha!" alone is a cry of surprise. | （ha'）= have（口语）；Ha! 为感叹词
- darn | A mild swear word, like "damn". | 该死（较温和的咒骂）
- han't => have | Spoken short form of "haven't" or "hasn't". | = haven't（口语）
- to't => to | Spoken short form of "to it". | = to it（口语）
- lookee => look | Old spoken form of "look you" (listen, look). | = look you，听着（口语）
- alonger => along | Dialect for "along with, together with". | = along with（方言）
- d'ye => do | Spoken short form of "do you". | = do you（口语）
- supposin => suppose | Spoken form of "supposing". | = supposing（口语）
