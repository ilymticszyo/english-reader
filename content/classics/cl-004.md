## Background

Jane Austen's Emma (1815) is about Emma Woodhouse, a clever, rich and confident young woman who likes to manage other people's lives. Her old friend and neighbour, Mr. Knightley, is the only person who often tells her when she is wrong.

In this chapter, a group of friends go on a picnic to Box Hill, a famous viewpoint in Surrey. The day is not going well. Everyone is bored and a little cross. Frank Churchill, a charming young man, starts flirting openly with Emma and invents a party game. One of the guests is Miss Bates, a kind, poor, older woman who talks far too much. She is a favourite target for jokes, but she has always been good to everyone. Read carefully: the most important moment in this excerpt is very short. Watch who laughs, who does not, and how Miss Bates responds.

## Excerpt

“I say nothing of which I am ashamed,” replied he, with lively impudence. “I saw you first in February. Let every body on the Hill hear me if they can. Let my accents swell to Mickleham on one side, and Dorking on the other. I saw you first in February.” And then whispering—“Our companions are excessively stupid. What shall we do to rouse them? Any nonsense will serve. They shall talk. Ladies and gentlemen, I am ordered by Miss Woodhouse (who, wherever she is, presides) to say, that she desires to know what you are all thinking of?”

Some laughed, and answered good-humouredly. Miss Bates said a great deal; Mrs. Elton swelled at the idea of Miss Woodhouse’s presiding; Mr. Knightley’s answer was the most distinct.

“Is Miss Woodhouse sure that she would like to hear what we are all thinking of?”

“Oh! no, no”—cried Emma, laughing as carelessly as she could—“Upon no account in the world. It is the very last thing I would stand the brunt of just now. Let me hear any thing rather than what you are all thinking of. I will not say quite all. There are one or two, perhaps, (glancing at Mr. Weston and Harriet,) whose thoughts I might not be afraid of knowing.”

“It is a sort of thing,” cried Mrs. Elton emphatically, “which I should not have thought myself privileged to inquire into. Though, perhaps, as the Chaperon of the party—I never was in any circle—exploring parties—young ladies—married women—”

Her mutterings were chiefly to her husband; and he murmured, in reply,

“Very true, my love, very true. Exactly so, indeed—quite unheard of—but some ladies say any thing. Better pass it off as a joke. Every body knows what is due to you.”

“It will not do,” whispered Frank to Emma; “they are most of them affronted. I will attack them with more address. Ladies and gentlemen—I am ordered by Miss Woodhouse to say, that she waives her right of knowing exactly what you may all be thinking of, and only requires something very entertaining from each of you, in a general way. Here are seven of you, besides myself, (who, she is pleased to say, am very entertaining already,) and she only demands from each of you either one thing very clever, be it prose or verse, original or repeated—or two things moderately clever—or three things very dull indeed, and she engages to laugh heartily at them all.”

“Oh! very well,” exclaimed Miss Bates, “then I need not be uneasy. ‘Three things very dull indeed.’ That will just do for me, you know. I shall be sure to say three dull things as soon as ever I open my mouth, shan’t I? (looking round with the most good-humoured dependence on every body’s assent)—Do not you all think I shall?”

Emma could not resist.

“Ah! ma’am, but there may be a difficulty. Pardon me—but you will be limited as to number—only three at once.”

Miss Bates, deceived by the mock ceremony of her manner, did not immediately catch her meaning; but, when it burst on her, it could not anger, though a slight blush shewed that it could pain her.

“Ah!—well—to be sure. Yes, I see what she means, (turning to Mr. Knightley,) and I will try to hold my tongue. I must make myself very disagreeable, or she would not have said such a thing to an old friend.”

## Appreciation

This is a scene about a joke that goes wrong, and Austen builds it slowly. First, she shows us a group of unhappy people. Frank Churchill tries to wake them up with a game, but his first attempt fails; even the narrator says the guests are "affronted". He tries again with a more playful rule: each person must say "one thing very clever", or "two things moderately clever", or "three things very dull indeed". The list of three is funny, and it gives Miss Bates an opening for her own joke about herself.

Miss Bates is humble and good-humoured. She laughs at her own habit of talking and looks round for everyone's approval. And then: "Emma could not resist." This four-word paragraph is a turning point. It is short because the decision happens in a moment.

Emma's line is witty, but it is also cruel. She uses Miss Bates's own words against her, in front of everybody. Austen describes the effect with great care: Miss Bates does not understand at first, and when she does, "it could not anger, though a slight blush shewed that it could pain her." The contrast between "anger" and "pain" is important. A proud person would be angry; a gentle person is simply hurt.

The final speech is painful to read. Miss Bates does not attack Emma. Instead, she blames herself: "I must make myself very disagreeable". Her kindness makes Emma's joke look even worse. Soon after this, Mr. Knightley will tell Emma how badly she has behaved, and this moment will change her.

## Sentences to imitate

> Emma could not resist.
A one-sentence paragraph marks a turning point. Use this technique when a character makes a quick decision that changes everything.

> and she only demands from each of you either one thing very clever, be it prose or verse, original or repeated—or two things moderately clever—or three things very dull indeed
A list that gets funnier as it goes. The third item turns the scale upside down.

> it could not anger, though a slight blush shewed that it could pain her.
A careful contrast between two feelings. "Could not… though… could" lets you describe a reaction exactly.

> I must make myself very disagreeable, or she would not have said such a thing to an old friend.
A gentle character blames herself instead of attacking. The phrase "an old friend" quietly shows how much Emma has hurt her.

## Notes

- address | Here it means skill and cleverness in dealing with people, not a home address. | 手腕；机敏
- affronted | Offended and a little insulted. | 被冒犯的
- presides | Is in charge of a meeting or a group, like a chairperson. | 主持
- brunt | "Stand the brunt of" means to suffer the worst part of something. | 首当其冲；承受冲击
- waives | Gives up a right on purpose. | 放弃（权利）
- engages | Here it means promises. | 保证；承诺
- dependence | Here it means trust that others will agree, not needing help. | 信赖
- mock | Pretended, not real (a mock ceremony). | 假装的；戏谑的
