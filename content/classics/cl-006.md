## Background

This excerpt gives away the biggest secret in Jane Eyre (1847). Jane and Mr. Rochester have just tried to get married. In the church, a lawyer stood up and said that the wedding could not go on, because Rochester already has a living wife. Rochester admitted that it is true. Now, angry and desperate, he takes everyone back to Thornfield Hall to show them what he has hidden.

For months Jane has heard strange laughter and noises from the third floor, and she has been told that a servant, Grace Poole, is to blame. In this scene, she learns the truth. Be ready for a dark, frightening passage, close to a horror story. As you read, notice the animal words used for the woman in the room. Many modern readers find this description troubling, and it is worth asking why Brontë describes her this way.

## Excerpt

He passed on and ascended the stairs, still holding my hand, and still beckoning the gentlemen to follow him, which they did. We mounted the first staircase, passed up the gallery, proceeded to the third storey: the low, black door, opened by Mr. Rochester’s master-key, admitted us to the tapestried room, with its great bed and its pictorial cabinet.

“You know this place, Mason,” said our guide; “she bit and stabbed you here.”

He lifted the hangings from the wall, uncovering the second door: this, too, he opened. In a room without a window, there burnt a fire guarded by a high and strong fender, and a lamp suspended from the ceiling by a chain. Grace Poole bent over the fire, apparently cooking something in a saucepan. In the deep shade, at the farther end of the room, a figure ran backwards and forwards. What it was, whether beast or human being, one could not, at first sight, tell: it grovelled, seemingly, on all fours; it snatched and growled like some strange wild animal: but it was covered with clothing, and a quantity of dark, grizzled hair, wild as a mane, hid its head and face.

“Good-morrow, Mrs. Poole!” said Mr. Rochester. “How are you? and how is your charge to-day?”

“We’re tolerable, sir, I thank you,” replied Grace, lifting the boiling mess carefully on to the hob: “rather snappish, but not ’rageous.”

A fierce cry seemed to give the lie to her favourable report: the clothed hyena rose up, and stood tall on its hind-feet.

“Ah! sir, she sees you!” exclaimed Grace: “you’d better not stay.”

“Only a few moments, Grace: you must allow me a few moments.”

“Take care then, sir!—for God’s sake, take care!”

The maniac bellowed: she parted her shaggy locks from her visage, and gazed wildly at her visitors. I recognised well that purple face,—those bloated features. Mrs. Poole advanced.

“Keep out of the way,” said Mr. Rochester, thrusting her aside: “she has no knife now, I suppose, and I’m on my guard.”

“One never knows what she has, sir: she is so cunning: it is not in mortal discretion to fathom her craft.”

“We had better leave her,” whispered Mason.

“Go to the devil!” was his brother-in-law’s recommendation.

“’Ware!” cried Grace. The three gentlemen retreated simultaneously. Mr. Rochester flung me behind him: the lunatic sprang and grappled his throat viciously, and laid her teeth to his cheek: they struggled. She was a big woman, in stature almost equalling her husband, and corpulent besides: she showed virile force in the contest—more than once she almost throttled him, athletic as he was. He could have settled her with a well-planted blow; but he would not strike: he would only wrestle. At last he mastered her arms; Grace Poole gave him a cord, and he pinioned them behind her: with more rope, which was at hand, he bound her to a chair. The operation was performed amidst the fiercest yells and the most convulsive plunges. Mr. Rochester then turned to the spectators: he looked at them with a smile both acrid and desolate.

“That is my wife,” said he. “Such is the sole conjugal embrace I am ever to know—such are the endearments which are to solace my leisure hours! And this is what I wished to have” (laying his hand on my shoulder): “this young girl, who stands so grave and quiet at the mouth of hell, looking collectedly at the gambols of a demon, I wanted her just as a change after that fierce ragout. Wood and Briggs, look at the difference! Compare these clear eyes with the red balls yonder—this face with that mask—this form with that bulk; then judge me, priest of the gospel and man of the law, and remember with what judgment ye judge ye shall be judged! Off with you now. I must shut up my prize.”

## Appreciation

Brontë builds this scene like a journey into a secret place. The first paragraph is full of movement: Rochester "ascended", they "mounted", "passed up", "proceeded". Each verb takes us one step higher in the house and closer to the mystery. Then a hidden door is opened, and the description slows down.

The first view of Bertha Mason is deliberately unclear: "What it was, whether beast or human being, one could not, at first sight, tell". Jane uses "it", not "she". Brontë then describes the figure with animal verbs: it "grovelled", "snatched and growled", and later "the clothed hyena" stands on its "hind-feet". This makes the scene frightening. It also shows how the people in the house, including Jane, refuse to see Bertha as a person. Many critics have written about this, and later writers, like Jean Rhys, gave Bertha her own story.

Notice Grace Poole's calm, practical speech in the middle of the horror. She says her patient is "rather snappish, but not 'rageous", as if she were talking about the weather. This contrast makes the scene even stranger.

Rochester's final speech is bitter and theatrical. He uses irony when he calls the fight "the sole conjugal embrace" he will ever know, and he sets up a series of contrasts: "these clear eyes with the red balls yonder—this face with that mask—this form with that bulk". He wants the lawyer and the priest to judge him with pity. Whether we accept his excuse is one of the big questions of the novel.

## Sentences to imitate

> He passed on and ascended the stairs, still holding my hand, and still beckoning the gentlemen to follow him, which they did.
Repeating "still" shows two actions happening at once and keeps the tension high.

> What it was, whether beast or human being, one could not, at first sight, tell
Delaying the main verb ("tell") to the end creates suspense. The reader, like Jane, has to wait to understand.

> He could have settled her with a well-planted blow; but he would not strike: he would only wrestle.
A clear contrast between what he could do and what he chooses to do. The colon introduces the explanation.

> Compare these clear eyes with the red balls yonder—this face with that mask—this form with that bulk
A series of paired contrasts. Using "this… that" again and again is a strong way to compare two things.

## Notes

- storey | A floor or level of a building (British spelling). | 楼层
- tapestried | Covered with tapestries, heavy cloths with woven pictures. | 挂着挂毯的
- fender | Here, a metal frame in front of a fireplace, not part of a car. | 壁炉围栏
- grovelled | Moved on hands and knees, close to the ground. | 匍匐爬行
- good-morrow | An old greeting meaning "good morning". | 早上好（古）
- charge | Here it means a person you are responsible for looking after. | 被照看的人
- tolerable | Fairly well, all right. | 还过得去
- rageous => outrageous | Grace Poole's short spoken form of "outrageous", here meaning violent and wild. | = outrageous（口语省略），狂暴的
- hob | A shelf beside a fireplace for keeping pots warm. | 炉边搁架
- visage | An old or literary word for face. | 面容
- ware => beware | Short spoken form of "beware!" (watch out), not "goods for sale". | = beware，当心！
- conjugal | Of marriage, between husband and wife. | 婚姻的；夫妻间的
- ragout | A rich, spicy stew; here Rochester's insulting image for his wife. | 浓味炖菜（此处为比喻）
