## Background

Kipling first told the Just So Stories to his own daughter, Josephine, at bedtime. She wanted each story told "just so", in exactly the same words every time, and that gave the book its name. The stories were published in 1902.

Each story explains, in a funny and impossible way, how an animal got its special feature: how the elephant got its trunk, how the leopard got its spots, and so on. This one explains the camel's hump. The Camel refuses to work and only says "Humph!", while the Horse, the Dog and the Ox work hard for Man. Finally a Djinn, a magic spirit from Arabian stories, arrives. As you read, notice the strong patterns of repetition, and try reading parts of it out loud. Kipling wrote it to be heard.

## Excerpt

In the beginning of years, when the world was so new and all, and the Animals were just beginning to work for Man, there was a Camel, and he lived in the middle of a Howling Desert because he did not want to work; and besides, he was a Howler himself. So he ate sticks and thorns and tamarisks and milkweed and prickles, most ‘scruciating idle; and when anybody spoke to him he said ‘Humph!’ Just ‘Humph!’ and no more.

Presently the Horse came to him on Monday morning, with a saddle on his back and a bit in his mouth, and said, ‘Camel, O Camel, come out and trot like the rest of us.’

‘Humph!’ said the Camel; and the Horse went away and told the Man.

Presently the Dog came to him, with a stick in his mouth, and said, ‘Camel, O Camel, come and fetch and carry like the rest of us.’

‘Humph!’ said the Camel; and the Dog went away and told the Man.

Presently the Ox came to him, with the yoke on his neck and said, ‘Camel, O Camel, come and plough like the rest of us.’

‘Humph!’ said the Camel; and the Ox went away and told the Man.

At the end of the day the Man called the Horse and the Dog and the Ox together, and said, ‘Three, O Three, I’m very sorry for you (with the world so new-and-all); but that Humph-thing in the Desert can’t work, or he would have been here by now, so I am going to leave him alone, and you must work double-time to make up for it.’

That made the Three very angry (with the world so new-and-all), and they held a palaver, and an indaba, and a punchayet, and a pow-wow on the edge of the Desert; and the Camel came chewing on milkweed most ‘scruciating idle, and laughed at them. Then he said ‘Humph!’ and went away again.

Presently there came along the Djinn in charge of All Deserts, rolling in a cloud of dust (Djinns always travel that way because it is Magic), and he stopped to palaver and pow-pow with the Three.

‘Djinn of All Deserts,’ said the Horse, ‘is it right for any one to be idle, with the world so new-and-all?’

‘Certainly not,’ said the Djinn.

‘Well,’ said the Horse, ‘there’s a thing in the middle of your Howling Desert (and he’s a Howler himself) with a long neck and long legs, and he hasn’t done a stroke of work since Monday morning. He won’t trot.’

‘Whew!’ said the Djinn, whistling, ‘that’s my Camel, for all the gold in Arabia! What does he say about it?’

‘He says “Humph!”’ said the Dog; ‘and he won’t fetch and carry.’

‘Does he say anything else?’

‘Only “Humph!”; and he won’t plough,’ said the Ox.

‘Very good,’ said the Djinn. ‘I’ll humph him if you will kindly wait a minute.’

The Djinn rolled himself up in his dust-cloak, and took a bearing across the desert, and found the Camel most ‘scruciatingly idle, looking at his own reflection in a pool of water.

‘My long and bubbling friend,’ said the Djinn, ‘what’s this I hear of your doing no work, with the world so new-and-all?’

‘Humph!’ said the Camel.

The Djinn sat down, with his chin in his hand, and began to think a Great Magic, while the Camel looked at his own reflection in the pool of water.

‘You’ve given the Three extra work ever since Monday morning, all on account of your ‘scruciating idleness,’ said the Djinn; and he went on thinking Magics, with his chin in his hand.

‘Humph!’ said the Camel.

‘I shouldn’t say that again if I were you,’ said the Djinn; you might say it once too often. Bubbles, I want you to work.’

And the Camel said ‘Humph!’ again; but no sooner had he said it than he saw his back, that he was so proud of, puffing up and puffing up into a great big lolloping humph.

‘Do you see that?’ said the Djinn. ‘That’s your very own humph that you’ve brought upon your very own self by not working. To-day is Thursday, and you’ve done no work since Monday, when the work began. Now you are going to work.’

‘How can I,’ said the Camel, ‘with this humph on my back?’

## Appreciation

This story is built like a song. The same structure is repeated three times: an animal comes to the Camel, says "Camel, O Camel, come and…", the Camel answers "Humph!", and the animal goes away "and told the Man". Children love this kind of pattern because they can guess what comes next and join in.

Kipling also repeats phrases as a kind of chorus. "With the world so new-and-all" appears again and again, like a refrain in music. "Most 'scruciating idle" is the Camel's special phrase. The word is a childish short form of "excruciating", which means extremely painful. Kipling uses it the way a child might, as a funny, strong word.

There is also wordplay. The Camel says "Humph!", a sound people make when they are rude and grumpy. In the end, the Djinn turns that word into a real "humph" on his back. The punishment fits the crime: his own lazy word becomes his burden. Notice the Djinn's dry warning: "you might say it once too often." This sentence is useful in real life too.

Look at the list "a palaver, and an indaba, and a punchayet, and a pow-wow". All four words mean a meeting or discussion, taken from different languages and cultures. Kipling enjoyed the sounds of words, and this list is a joke for the ear.

Although it is funny, the story has a simple moral about work and fairness. The Camel's idleness creates "extra work" for others, and that is why he is punished.

## Sentences to imitate

> ‘Humph!’ said the Camel; and the Horse went away and told the Man.
A repeated pattern. Use repetition to make a story easy to follow and fun to read aloud.

> ‘I shouldn’t say that again if I were you,’ said the Djinn
"I shouldn't… if I were you" is a polite but serious warning.

> but no sooner had he said it than he saw his back, that he was so proud of, puffing up and puffing up into a great big lolloping humph.
"No sooner had… than…" shows that one thing happens immediately after another.

> That’s your very own humph that you’ve brought upon your very own self by not working.
Repeating "your very own" stresses responsibility.

## Notes

- tamarisks | Small desert trees or bushes. | 柽柳
- scruciating | A childish short form of "excruciating", meaning extremely (bad). | 极其（excruciating 的儿语式缩略）
- scruciatingly | Childish form of "excruciatingly", meaning extremely. | 极其地
- bit | Here it means the metal bar put in a horse's mouth. | 马嚼子
- yoke | A wooden bar put across the necks of two animals pulling a plough. | 轭
- palaver | A long discussion (old informal word). | 冗长的商谈
- indaba | A meeting for discussion (a word from southern Africa). | （南部非洲）会议
- punchayet | A village council (a word from India). | （印度）村务会议
- pow-wow | A meeting (from Native American languages). | 会议（源自美洲原住民语）
- pow-pow | Kipling's playful form of "pow-wow", a meeting. | 商议（玩笑式说法）
- djinn | A magic spirit in Arabian stories (also "genie"). | 精灵（神怪）
- lolloping | Big, heavy and moving in an awkward way. | 笨重晃动的
