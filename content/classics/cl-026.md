## Background

This excerpt is the end of The Great Gatsby (1925), so it reveals what happens to Gatsby. Over the summer, Nick Carraway learned that his neighbour Jay Gatsby gave his huge parties for one reason: to meet Daisy Buchanan again. Five years earlier, before the war, the poor young officer Gatsby loved Daisy, a rich girl, but she married the wealthy Tom Buchanan. Gatsby made a fortune, partly through crime, and bought a house across the bay from Daisy's, where he could see the green light at the end of her dock.

The story ended in tragedy: after a car accident, Gatsby was shot, and almost nobody came to his funeral. Nick is now leaving the East to go home to the Midwest. On his last night he walks over to Gatsby's empty house. As you read, follow how Nick's thoughts move from one man's dream to the history of a whole nation.

## Excerpt

Gatsby’s house was still empty when I left—the grass on his lawn had grown as long as mine. One of the taxi drivers in the village never took a fare past the entrance gate without stopping for a minute and pointing inside; perhaps it was he who drove Daisy and Gatsby over to East Egg the night of the accident, and perhaps he had made a story about it all his own. I didn’t want to hear it and I avoided him when I got off the train.

I spent my Saturday nights in New York because those gleaming, dazzling parties of his were with me so vividly that I could still hear the music and the laughter, faint and incessant, from his garden, and the cars going up and down his drive. One night I did hear a material car there, and saw its lights stop at his front steps. But I didn’t investigate. Probably it was some final guest who had been away at the ends of the earth and didn’t know that the party was over.

On the last night, with my trunk packed and my car sold to the grocer, I went over and looked at that huge incoherent failure of a house once more. On the white steps an obscene word, scrawled by some boy with a piece of brick, stood out clearly in the moonlight, and I erased it, drawing my shoe raspingly along the stone. Then I wandered down to the beach and sprawled out on the sand.

Most of the big shore places were closed now and there were hardly any lights except the shadowy, moving glow of a ferryboat across the Sound. And as the moon rose higher the inessential houses began to melt away until gradually I became aware of the old island here that flowered once for Dutch sailors’ eyes—a fresh, green breast of the new world. Its vanished trees, the trees that had made way for Gatsby’s house, had once pandered in whispers to the last and greatest of all human dreams; for a transitory enchanted moment man must have held his breath in the presence of this continent, compelled into an aesthetic contemplation he neither understood nor desired, face to face for the last time in history with something commensurate to his capacity for wonder.

And as I sat there brooding on the old, unknown world, I thought of Gatsby’s wonder when he first picked out the green light at the end of Daisy’s dock. He had come a long way to this blue lawn, and his dream must have seemed so close that he could hardly fail to grasp it. He did not know that it was already behind him, somewhere back in that vast obscurity beyond the city, where the dark fields of the republic rolled on under the night.

Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgiastic future that year by year recedes before us. It eluded us then, but that’s no matter—tomorrow we will run faster, stretch out our arms further … And one fine morning—

So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past.

## Appreciation

The ending of The Great Gatsby is famous for its poetic language. Fitzgerald moves from small, concrete details to big ideas. The excerpt begins with ordinary signs of loss: Gatsby's grass has grown long, a taxi driver tells stories about the house, and someone has written "an obscene word" on the steps. Nick quietly rubs it out. This small action shows his loyalty to Gatsby.

Then the scene opens up. As the moon rises, the modern houses "melt away", and Nick imagines the island as the first Dutch sailors saw it: "a fresh, green breast of the new world". The colour green connects this image with Gatsby's green light. Fitzgerald suggests that Gatsby's dream of Daisy is like the old American dream: a hope for a perfect new beginning.

The long sentence about the sailors is difficult but beautiful. It says that, for one moment in history, human beings stood "face to face… with something commensurate to his capacity for wonder", that is, something as big as their ability to dream. After that, nothing could be so wonderful again.

The tone becomes sad when Nick says that Gatsby's dream "was already behind him". Gatsby was reaching for a future that was really the past. Then Fitzgerald changes from "he" to "we": "tomorrow we will run faster, stretch out our arms further". Now the dream belongs to all of us. The broken sentence, "And one fine morning—", shows that the hope never ends and never arrives.

The last line uses a powerful metaphor. We are "boats against the current", rowing forward but always pushed back into the past.

## Sentences to imitate

> Gatsby’s house was still empty when I left—the grass on his lawn had grown as long as mine.
A small, concrete detail shows a big change. Good writing often shows loss through simple objects.

> He had come a long way to this blue lawn, and his dream must have seemed so close that he could hardly fail to grasp it.
A "so… that" structure describes a hope that seemed certain. The colour "blue" adds a dreamlike feeling.

> It eluded us then, but that’s no matter—tomorrow we will run faster, stretch out our arms further
A move from "he" to "we" makes one person's story universal. The parallel verbs show endless effort.

> So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past.
A final metaphor that sums up the whole book. The rhythm is slow and heavy, like rowing against water.
