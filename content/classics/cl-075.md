## Background

John Buchan was a Scottish writer, politician and later Governor General of Canada. The Thirty-Nine Steps (1915) is one of the first modern spy thrillers. Its hero, Richard Hannay, is an ordinary man pulled into a dangerous adventure, and the book inspired many later thrillers and films, including Alfred Hitchcock's 1935 film.

The story is set in the spring of 1914, just before the First World War. Hannay is a Scottish mining engineer who has made his money in southern Africa and has just come to London. He expected to enjoy his new life, but he is bored. This excerpt is the opening of the novel. That night, a stranger will knock at his door with a story about a secret plot, and his life will change completely. As you read, notice Hannay's voice: relaxed, informal and confident, with many colloquial expressions.

## Excerpt

I returned from the City about three o’clock on that May afternoon pretty well disgusted with life. I had been three months in the Old Country, and was fed up with it. If anyone had told me a year ago that I would have been feeling like that I should have laughed at him; but there was the fact. The weather made me liverish, the talk of the ordinary Englishman made me sick. I couldn’t get enough exercise, and the amusements of London seemed as flat as soda-water that has been standing in the sun. “Richard Hannay,” I kept telling myself, “you have got into the wrong ditch, my friend, and you had better climb out.”

It made me bite my lips to think of the plans I had been building up those last years in Buluwayo. I had got my pile—not one of the big ones, but good enough for me; and I had figured out all kinds of ways of enjoying myself. My father had brought me out from Scotland at the age of six, and I had never been home since; so England was a sort of Arabian Nights to me, and I counted on stopping there for the rest of my days.

But from the first I was disappointed with it. In about a week I was tired of seeing sights, and in less than a month I had had enough of restaurants and theatres and race-meetings. I had no real pal to go about with, which probably explains things. Plenty of people invited me to their houses, but they didn’t seem much interested in me. They would fling me a question or two about South Africa, and then get on to their own affairs. A lot of Imperialist ladies asked me to tea to meet schoolmasters from New Zealand and editors from Vancouver, and that was the dismalest business of all. Here was I, thirty-seven years old, sound in wind and limb, with enough money to have a good time, yawning my head off all day. I had just about settled to clear out and get back to the veld, for I was the best bored man in the United Kingdom.

That afternoon I had been worrying my brokers about investments to give my mind something to work on, and on my way home I turned into my club—rather a pot-house, which took in Colonial members. I had a long drink, and read the evening papers. They were full of the row in the Near East, and there was an article about Karolides, the Greek Premier. I rather fancied the chap. From all accounts he seemed the one big man in the show; and he played a straight game too, which was more than could be said for most of them. I gathered that they hated him pretty blackly in Berlin and Vienna, but that we were going to stick by him, and one paper said that he was the only barrier between Europe and Armageddon. I remember wondering if I could get a job in those parts. It struck me that Albania was the sort of place that might keep a man from yawning.

About six o’clock I went home, dressed, dined at the Café Royal, and turned into a music-hall. It was a silly show, all capering women and monkey-faced men, and I did not stay long. The night was fine and clear as I walked back to the flat I had hired near Portland Place. The crowd surged past me on the pavements, busy and chattering, and I envied the people for having something to do. These shop-girls and clerks and dandies and policemen had some interest in life that kept them going. I gave half-a-crown to a beggar because I saw him yawn; he was a fellow-sufferer. At Oxford Circus I looked up into the spring sky and I made a vow. I would give the Old Country another day to fit me into something; if nothing happened, I would take the next boat for the Cape.

## Appreciation

Buchan's narrator speaks to us like a friend over a drink. His language is colloquial and lively: "fed up with it", "I had got my pile", "pal", "yawning my head off". This first-person voice makes the reader trust Hannay immediately. He is not a professional spy; he is an ordinary, honest man.

The opening is clever because it begins with boredom, not action. Hannay is "pretty well disgusted with life". London's amusements seem "as flat as soda-water that has been standing in the sun", a fresh and funny simile. His self-description, "the best bored man in the United Kingdom", is a joke that also shows his energy: he needs excitement.

Buchan also includes political background. The newspapers talk about "the row in the Near East" and about Karolides, the Greek Premier, who is "the only barrier between Europe and Armageddon". Readers in 1915, during the war, knew how real this danger was. The fictional politician will be important in the plot, so Buchan plants information early.

The final paragraph moves through London at night: the Café Royal, a music-hall, Portland Place, Oxford Circus. The real place names make the story feel solid. Hannay envies ordinary people "for having something to do", and gives money to a beggar because "he was a fellow-sufferer". This is a gentle joke.

Then comes the vow: one more day, or he will leave. Every reader knows that something will happen. Buchan uses the hero's boredom to build suspense, and the reader is as ready for adventure as Hannay.

## Sentences to imitate

> If anyone had told me a year ago that I would have been feeling like that I should have laughed at him; but there was the fact.
A third conditional to show a surprising change.

> the amusements of London seemed as flat as soda-water that has been standing in the sun.
A fresh, humorous simile.

> It struck me that Albania was the sort of place that might keep a man from yawning.
"It struck me that…" means "the idea came to me that…".

> I would give the Old Country another day to fit me into something; if nothing happened, I would take the next boat for the Cape.
A decision with a deadline that creates suspense.
