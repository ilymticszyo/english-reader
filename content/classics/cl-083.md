## Background

James Joyce was an Irish writer who lived most of his adult life outside Ireland, yet wrote almost entirely about Dublin. He is famous for Ulysses (1922), one of the most important novels of the twentieth century. Dubliners (1914) is a collection of fifteen short stories about ordinary people in the city.

"Araby" is told by an adult remembering his boyhood. The boy lives with his aunt and uncle in a quiet street. He has fallen in love with his friend Mangan's older sister, although he has hardly spoken to her. One day she mentions a bazaar, a large charity fair called Araby, which she cannot attend. He promises to bring her a gift from it. He waits all day for his uncle to come home and give him money. This excerpt is the ending, so it is marked as a spoiler. As you read, notice the contrast between the boy's dreams and what he actually finds.

## Excerpt

“I’m afraid you may put off your bazaar for this night of Our Lord.”

At nine o’clock I heard my uncle’s latchkey in the halldoor. I heard him talking to himself and heard the hallstand rocking when it had received the weight of his overcoat. I could interpret these signs. When he was midway through his dinner I asked him to give me the money to go to the bazaar. He had forgotten.

“The people are in bed and after their first sleep now,” he said.

I did not smile. My aunt said to him energetically:

“Can’t you give him the money and let him go? You’ve kept him late enough as it is.”

My uncle said he was very sorry he had forgotten. He said he believed in the old saying: “All work and no play makes Jack a dull boy.” He asked me where I was going and, when I had told him a second time he asked me did I know The Arab’s Farewell to his Steed. When I left the kitchen he was about to recite the opening lines of the piece to my aunt.

I held a florin tightly in my hand as I strode down Buckingham Street towards the station. The sight of the streets thronged with buyers and glaring with gas recalled to me the purpose of my journey. I took my seat in a third-class carriage of a deserted train. After an intolerable delay the train moved out of the station slowly. It crept onward among ruinous houses and over the twinkling river. At Westland Row Station a crowd of people pressed to the carriage doors; but the porters moved them back, saying that it was a special train for the bazaar. I remained alone in the bare carriage. In a few minutes the train drew up beside an improvised wooden platform. I passed out on to the road and saw by the lighted dial of a clock that it was ten minutes to ten. In front of me was a large building which displayed the magical name.

I could not find any sixpenny entrance and, fearing that the bazaar would be closed, I passed in quickly through a turnstile, handing a shilling to a weary-looking man. I found myself in a big hall girdled at half its height by a gallery. Nearly all the stalls were closed and the greater part of the hall was in darkness. I recognised a silence like that which pervades a church after a service. I walked into the centre of the bazaar timidly. A few people were gathered about the stalls which were still open. Before a curtain, over which the words Café Chantant were written in coloured lamps, two men were counting money on a salver. I listened to the fall of the coins.

Remembering with difficulty why I had come I went over to one of the stalls and examined porcelain vases and flowered tea-sets. At the door of the stall a young lady was talking and laughing with two young gentlemen. I remarked their English accents and listened vaguely to their conversation.

“O, I never said such a thing!”

“O, but you did!”

“O, but I didn’t!”

“Didn’t she say that?”

“Yes. I heard her.”

“O, there’s a ... fib!”

Observing me the young lady came over and asked me did I wish to buy anything. The tone of her voice was not encouraging; she seemed to have spoken to me out of a sense of duty. I looked humbly at the great jars that stood like eastern guards at either side of the dark entrance to the stall and murmured:

“No, thank you.”

The young lady changed the position of one of the vases and went back to the two young men. They began to talk of the same subject. Once or twice the young lady glanced at me over her shoulder.

I lingered before her stall, though I knew my stay was useless, to make my interest in her wares seem the more real. Then I turned away slowly and walked down the middle of the bazaar. I allowed the two pennies to fall against the sixpence in my pocket. I heard a voice call from one end of the gallery that the light was out. The upper part of the hall was now completely dark.

Gazing up into the darkness I saw myself as a creature driven and derided by vanity; and my eyes burned with anguish and anger.

## Appreciation

Joyce's language here is plain and controlled, but every detail carries meaning. The uncle's late return is told through sounds: the latchkey, the hallstand "rocking" under his coat. The boy says, "I could interpret these signs", which suggests that his uncle has been drinking. The uncle's cheerful, worn-out sayings, "All work and no play makes Jack a dull boy", show that he does not understand the boy's urgency at all.

The journey is described as a slow, lonely passage: a "deserted train", "an intolerable delay", "ruinous houses". The boy is alone in the carriage, as he is alone in his feelings. He has imagined Araby as a magical, eastern place, but he arrives at ten to ten, and "Nearly all the stalls were closed".

Joyce compares the hall to a church: "a silence like that which pervades a church after a service". The men counting money on a tray suggest a darker idea, as if commerce has replaced holiness.

The conversation of the young lady and two Englishmen is a key moment. It is empty, flirtatious and silly: "O, I never said such a thing!" This ordinary flirting shows the boy what romance really looks like, and it is very different from his dreams. The young lady speaks to him only "out of a sense of duty".

The final sentence is one of the most famous in modern short fiction: "I saw myself as a creature driven and derided by vanity; and my eyes burned with anguish and anger." This is an epiphany, a sudden moment of understanding. The light goes out, and the boy sees himself clearly for the first time.

## Sentences to imitate

> I could interpret these signs.
A short sentence that hints at something without saying it directly.

> The sight of the streets thronged with buyers and glaring with gas recalled to me the purpose of my journey.
A long noun phrase as the subject, followed by a clear verb.

> I recognised a silence like that which pervades a church after a service.
"Like that which…" for a precise comparison.

> Gazing up into the darkness I saw myself as a creature driven and derided by vanity; and my eyes burned with anguish and anger.
A final sentence of self-recognition. Note the alliteration of "anguish and anger".
