## Background

F. Scott Fitzgerald published The Great Gatsby in 1925. It is set in the summer of 1922, during the "Jazz Age", a time of parties, new money and big dreams in the United States. The story is told by Nick Carraway, a young man from the Midwest who moves to New York to work in finance. He rents a small house on Long Island, next to the huge mansion of a mysterious millionaire called Jay Gatsby.

This excerpt is the opening of the novel. Nick does not start with the story. Instead, he starts with himself: his father's advice and his habit of not judging people. As you read, ask whether you trust Nick. He says he is tolerant, but notice the words he uses about other people. Notice also how he introduces Gatsby, with both scorn and admiration in the same paragraph. This tells us a lot about the book.

## Excerpt

In my younger and more vulnerable years my father gave me some advice that I’ve been turning over in my mind ever since.

“Whenever you feel like criticizing anyone,” he told me, “just remember that all the people in this world haven’t had the advantages that you’ve had.”

He didn’t say any more, but we’ve always been unusually communicative in a reserved way, and I understood that he meant a great deal more than that. In consequence, I’m inclined to reserve all judgements, a habit that has opened up many curious natures to me and also made me the victim of not a few veteran bores. The abnormal mind is quick to detect and attach itself to this quality when it appears in a normal person, and so it came about that in college I was unjustly accused of being a politician, because I was privy to the secret griefs of wild, unknown men. Most of the confidences were unsought—frequently I have feigned sleep, preoccupation, or a hostile levity when I realized by some unmistakable sign that an intimate revelation was quivering on the horizon; for the intimate revelations of young men, or at least the terms in which they express them, are usually plagiaristic and marred by obvious suppressions. Reserving judgements is a matter of infinite hope. I am still a little afraid of missing something if I forget that, as my father snobbishly suggested, and I snobbishly repeat, a sense of the fundamental decencies is parcelled out unequally at birth.

And, after boasting this way of my tolerance, I come to the admission that it has a limit. Conduct may be founded on the hard rock or the wet marshes, but after a certain point I don’t care what it’s founded on. When I came back from the East last autumn I felt that I wanted the world to be in uniform and at a sort of moral attention forever; I wanted no more riotous excursions with privileged glimpses into the human heart. Only Gatsby, the man who gives his name to this book, was exempt from my reaction—Gatsby, who represented everything for which I have an unaffected scorn. If personality is an unbroken series of successful gestures, then there was something gorgeous about him, some heightened sensitivity to the promises of life, as if he were related to one of those intricate machines that register earthquakes ten thousand miles away. This responsiveness had nothing to do with that flabby impressionability which is dignified under the name of the “creative temperament”—it was an extraordinary gift for hope, a romantic readiness such as I have never found in any other person and which it is not likely I shall ever find again. No—Gatsby turned out all right at the end; it is what preyed on Gatsby, what foul dust floated in the wake of his dreams that temporarily closed out my interest in the abortive sorrows and short-winded elations of men.

## Appreciation

The first sentence sounds quiet and personal, but it is very carefully built. "In my younger and more vulnerable years" makes us feel that the narrator is older and wiser now. The advice from his father seems kind: other people "haven't had the advantages that you've had". But it also reminds us that Nick comes from a privileged family.

Fitzgerald's narrator likes to contradict himself, and this creates gentle irony. Nick says he reserves all judgements, but in the same paragraph he calls people "veteran bores" and "wild, unknown men". He admits that his father "snobbishly suggested" and that he "snobbishly" repeats the advice. A narrator who knows his faults is honest; but we should also be careful, because he will judge everyone in the story.

Look at the beautiful balance of some phrases. "Reserving judgements is a matter of infinite hope" is short and wise. "Conduct may be founded on the hard rock or the wet marshes" uses a contrast of images to talk about morals. Fitzgerald often turns abstract ideas into pictures.

The introduction of Gatsby is the most important part. Nick says Gatsby "represented everything for which I have an unaffected scorn", but then praises his "extraordinary gift for hope". This mixture of criticism and admiration is the feeling of the whole novel. The image of Gatsby as one of "those intricate machines that register earthquakes ten thousand miles away" shows how sensitive he is to the promise of life. The final sentence hints at a sad ending without telling us anything clearly, which makes us want to read on.

## Sentences to imitate

> In my younger and more vulnerable years my father gave me some advice that I’ve been turning over in my mind ever since.
An opening that looks back from the present. It creates a reflective, personal tone at once.

> Reserving judgements is a matter of infinite hope.
A short, general statement after long sentences. This contrast makes the idea stand out.

> And, after boasting this way of my tolerance, I come to the admission that it has a limit.
A narrator who corrects himself. Admitting a limit can make a writer sound honest.

> it was an extraordinary gift for hope, a romantic readiness such as I have never found in any other person and which it is not likely I shall ever find again.
A key quality is named, then explained again in more detail. Repeating an idea in new words makes it richer.

## Notes

- vulnerable | Easily hurt, physically or emotionally. | 脆弱的；易受伤害的
- reserved | Not showing your feelings openly. | 含蓄的；矜持的
- bores | People who are boring. | 令人厌烦的人
- privy | "Privy to" means allowed to know something secret. | 知晓（秘密）的
- feigned | Pretended. | 假装
- levity | A light, joking attitude when a serious one is expected. | 轻浮
- plagiaristic | Copied from someone else's words or ideas. | 抄袭的
- marred | Spoiled, damaged. | 损坏的
- parcelled | "Parcelled out" means divided and given out in parts. | 分配
- exempt | Free from something that affects others. | 免除的；不受影响的
- unaffected | Here it means real, sincere (not pretended). | 真诚的；不做作的
- flabby | Soft and weak (here, of a personality). | 软弱无力的
- abortive | Failed, ended too early. | 失败的；夭折的
