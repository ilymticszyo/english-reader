## Background

Mary Shelley began Frankenstein when she was eighteen, during a stormy summer in Switzerland in 1816, when her friends challenged each other to write ghost stories. The novel was first published in 1818. (Shelley revised it in 1831; this excerpt follows the 1818 text in Project Gutenberg #84, where this scene is Chapter 5.)

Victor Frankenstein is a young Swiss scientist studying at the university of Ingolstadt in Germany. He has discovered the secret of giving life to dead matter, and for two years he has worked alone, building a human body from parts. He has hardly slept or seen his family. Now, on a cold, rainy night, his experiment is finally complete. Victor is telling his story many years later, so he already knows how terribly it will end. As you read, compare what Victor hoped for with what he actually feels when his dream comes true.

## Excerpt

It was on a dreary night of November that I beheld the accomplishment of my toils. With an anxiety that almost amounted to agony, I collected the instruments of life around me, that I might infuse a spark of being into the lifeless thing that lay at my feet. It was already one in the morning; the rain pattered dismally against the panes, and my candle was nearly burnt out, when, by the glimmer of the half-extinguished light, I saw the dull yellow eye of the creature open; it breathed hard, and a convulsive motion agitated its limbs.

How can I describe my emotions at this catastrophe, or how delineate the wretch whom with such infinite pains and care I had endeavoured to form? His limbs were in proportion, and I had selected his features as beautiful. Beautiful! Great God! His yellow skin scarcely covered the work of muscles and arteries beneath; his hair was of a lustrous black, and flowing; his teeth of a pearly whiteness; but these luxuriances only formed a more horrid contrast with his watery eyes, that seemed almost of the same colour as the dun-white sockets in which they were set, his shrivelled complexion and straight black lips.

The different accidents of life are not so changeable as the feelings of human nature. I had worked hard for nearly two years, for the sole purpose of infusing life into an inanimate body. For this I had deprived myself of rest and health. I had desired it with an ardour that far exceeded moderation; but now that I had finished, the beauty of the dream vanished, and breathless horror and disgust filled my heart. Unable to endure the aspect of the being I had created, I rushed out of the room and continued a long time traversing my bed-chamber, unable to compose my mind to sleep. At length lassitude succeeded to the tumult I had before endured, and I threw myself on the bed in my clothes, endeavouring to seek a few moments of forgetfulness. But it was in vain; I slept, indeed, but I was disturbed by the wildest dreams. I thought I saw Elizabeth, in the bloom of health, walking in the streets of Ingolstadt. Delighted and surprised, I embraced her, but as I imprinted the first kiss on her lips, they became livid with the hue of death; her features appeared to change, and I thought that I held the corpse of my dead mother in my arms; a shroud enveloped her form, and I saw the grave-worms crawling in the folds of the flannel. I started from my sleep with horror; a cold dew covered my forehead, my teeth chattered, and every limb became convulsed; when, by the dim and yellow light of the moon, as it forced its way through the window shutters, I beheld the wretch—the miserable monster whom I had created. He held up the curtain of the bed; and his eyes, if eyes they may be called, were fixed on me. His jaws opened, and he muttered some inarticulate sounds, while a grin wrinkled his cheeks. He might have spoken, but I did not hear; one hand was stretched out, seemingly to detain me, but I escaped and rushed downstairs. I took refuge in the courtyard belonging to the house which I inhabited, where I remained during the rest of the night, walking up and down in the greatest agitation, listening attentively, catching and fearing each sound as if it were to announce the approach of the demoniacal corpse to which I had so miserably given life.

Oh! No mortal could support the horror of that countenance. A mummy again endued with animation could not be so hideous as that wretch. I had gazed on him while unfinished; he was ugly then, but when those muscles and joints were rendered capable of motion, it became a thing such as even Dante could not have conceived.

## Appreciation

This is one of the most famous scenes in all of horror fiction, but Shelley tells it in a surprisingly quiet way. There is no lightning in the novel. The first paragraph gives us small, gloomy details: a "dreary night of November", rain on the windows, a candle that is "nearly burnt out". Then, in the middle of a long sentence, comes the key image: "the dull yellow eye of the creature open". The moment of creation is just one tired eye.

Victor's language shows his shock. He asks a question he cannot answer: "How can I describe my emotions at this catastrophe…?" Then he repeats his own word with horror: "Beautiful! Great God!" He had chosen beautiful features, but together they look terrible. Shelley uses contrast here. The "lustrous black" hair and "pearly" teeth only make the "watery eyes" and "straight black lips" more horrible.

The paragraph that follows explains the deeper tragedy. Victor worked "with an ardour that far exceeded moderation", but "now that I had finished, the beauty of the dream vanished". This is a warning about wanting something too much, especially knowledge and power.

His nightmare is full of symbols. He kisses Elizabeth, the woman he loves, and she turns into his dead mother. Life and death mix together, just as they did in his experiment. When he wakes, the creature is standing by his bed, smiling and reaching out a hand. Victor sees a monster, but a careful reader may see a newborn being looking for its parent. Victor runs away, and that choice is the beginning of all the disasters to come.

## Sentences to imitate

> It was on a dreary night of November that I beheld the accomplishment of my toils.
"It was… that…" puts the time and mood first. A strong way to open a dramatic scene.

> I saw the dull yellow eye of the creature open; it breathed hard, and a convulsive motion agitated its limbs.
A huge event is shown through small physical details. Small details are often more frightening than big descriptions.

> I had desired it with an ardour that far exceeded moderation; but now that I had finished, the beauty of the dream vanished, and breathless horror and disgust filled my heart.
A sentence that turns on "but now that". Useful for showing how a feeling changes after a goal is reached.

> He might have spoken, but I did not hear; one hand was stretched out, seemingly to detain me, but I escaped and rushed downstairs.
Two "but" clauses show a missed chance. The narrator tells us more than he understands.

- toils | Long, hard work. | 辛劳；苦干
- infuse | Put a quality or feeling into something. | 注入；灌输
- convulsive | Moving suddenly and violently without control. | 痉挛的；抽搐的
- delineate | Describe or draw something exactly. | 描绘；描述
- wretch | An unhappy or unlucky person; also a bad person. | 可怜的人；坏人
- luxuriances | Rich, full, beautiful qualities. | 繁茂；华美之处
- dun-white | Dull, dirty white (dun = dull greyish-brown). | 灰白的；暗白色的
- inanimate | Not alive. | 无生命的
- ardour | Strong feeling, great enthusiasm. | 热情；狂热
- traversing | Moving across, back and forth. | 来回走过
- lassitude | Tiredness, lack of energy. | 倦怠；乏力
- ingolstadt | A university town in Bavaria, Germany. | 因戈尔施塔特（德国大学城）
- livid | Here, grey-blue in colour, like a dead body. | 青灰色的
- shroud | A cloth used to wrap a dead body. | 裹尸布
- inarticulate | Not clear enough to be understood as words. | 口齿不清的
- countenance | Here it means face or appearance, not "approval". | 面容；相貌
- endued | Given (a quality). | 赋予
- dante | Dante Alighieri, the Italian poet who described Hell in the Inferno. | 但丁（意大利诗人，著《地狱篇》）
