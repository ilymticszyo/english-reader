## Background

Mark Twain published The Adventures of Tom Sawyer in 1876. It is set in St. Petersburg, a small town on the Mississippi River, based on Hannibal, Missouri, where Twain grew up in the 1840s. Tom is a clever, lazy and very imaginative boy who lives with his Aunt Polly.

On a beautiful Saturday morning, when all the other boys are free to play, Aunt Polly punishes Tom. He must whitewash, or paint white, a long wooden fence. Tom is miserable. Then his friend Ben Rogers comes along the street eating an apple and pretending to be a steamboat. Tom has an idea. The characters speak in the informal English of a Missouri village, so some words and spellings are unusual; the notes will help. As you read, watch how Tom makes Ben want to do his work, without ever asking him.

## Excerpt

Tom went on whitewashing—paid no attention to the steamboat. Ben stared a moment and then said: “Hi-Yi! You’re up a stump, ain’t you!”

No answer. Tom surveyed his last touch with the eye of an artist, then he gave his brush another gentle sweep and surveyed the result, as before. Ben ranged up alongside of him. Tom’s mouth watered for the apple, but he stuck to his work. Ben said:

“Hello, old chap, you got to work, hey?”

Tom wheeled suddenly and said:

“Why, it’s you, Ben! I warn’t noticing.”

“Say—I’m going in a-swimming, I am. Don’t you wish you could? But of course you’d druther work—wouldn’t you? Course you would!”

Tom contemplated the boy a bit, and said:

“What do you call work?”

“Why, ain’t that work?”

Tom resumed his whitewashing, and answered carelessly:

“Well, maybe it is, and maybe it ain’t. All I know, is, it suits Tom Sawyer.”

“Oh come, now, you don’t mean to let on that you like it?”

The brush continued to move.

“Like it? Well, I don’t see why I oughtn’t to like it. Does a boy get a chance to whitewash a fence every day?”

That put the thing in a new light. Ben stopped nibbling his apple. Tom swept his brush daintily back and forth—stepped back to note the effect—added a touch here and there—criticised the effect again—Ben watching every move and getting more and more interested, more and more absorbed. Presently he said:

“Say, Tom, let me whitewash a little.”

Tom considered, was about to consent; but he altered his mind:

“No—no—I reckon it wouldn’t hardly do, Ben. You see, Aunt Polly’s awful particular about this fence—right here on the street, you know—but if it was the back fence I wouldn’t mind and she wouldn’t. Yes, she’s awful particular about this fence; it’s got to be done very careful; I reckon there ain’t one boy in a thousand, maybe two thousand, that can do it the way it’s got to be done.”

“No—is that so? Oh come, now—lemme just try. Only just a little—I’d let you, if you was me, Tom.”

“Ben, I’d like to, honest injun; but Aunt Polly—well, Jim wanted to do it, but she wouldn’t let him; Sid wanted to do it, and she wouldn’t let Sid. Now don’t you see how I’m fixed? If you was to tackle this fence and anything was to happen to it—”

“Oh, shucks, I’ll be just as careful. Now lemme try. Say—I’ll give you the core of my apple.”

“Well, here—No, Ben, now don’t. I’m afeard—”

“I’ll give you all of it!”

Tom gave up the brush with reluctance in his face, but alacrity in his heart. And while the late steamer Big Missouri worked and sweated in the sun, the retired artist sat on a barrel in the shade close by, dangled his legs, munched his apple, and planned the slaughter of more innocents. There was no lack of material; boys happened along every little while; they came to jeer, but remained to whitewash. By the time Ben was fagged out, Tom had traded the next chance to Billy Fisher for a kite, in good repair; and when he played out, Johnny Miller bought in for a dead rat and a string to swing it with—and so on, and so on, hour after hour. And when the middle of the afternoon came, from being a poor poverty-stricken boy in the morning, Tom was literally rolling in wealth. He had besides the things before mentioned, twelve marbles, part of a jews-harp, a piece of blue bottle-glass to look through, a spool cannon, a key that wouldn’t unlock anything, a fragment of chalk, a glass stopper of a decanter, a tin soldier, a couple of tadpoles, six fire-crackers, a kitten with only one eye, a brass door-knob, a dog-collar—but no dog—the handle of a knife, four pieces of orange-peel, and a dilapidated old window sash.

He had had a nice, good, idle time all the while—plenty of company—and the fence had three coats of whitewash on it! If he hadn’t run out of whitewash he would have bankrupted every boy in the village.

## Appreciation

This is one of the most famous scenes in American literature because it is a perfect little lesson in psychology. Tom never asks Ben for help. Instead, he acts as if whitewashing is a rare and special art. He looks at his work "with the eye of an artist", steps back to check the effect, and adds "a touch here and there". Twain lists Tom's actions with dashes, so we can see each small movement, just as Ben does.

The key line is a question: "Does a boy get a chance to whitewash a fence every day?" Tom turns a punishment into a privilege. The narrator tells us simply, "That put the thing in a new light." Then Tom refuses when Ben asks to try. This is the cleverest move of all. The more Tom says no, the more Ben wants it, until he offers his whole apple.

Twain's narrator uses big, formal words for small, childish events, which is a classic comic technique. Tom gives up the brush "with reluctance in his face, but alacrity in his heart". The other boys come "to jeer, but remained to whitewash". At the end of the day, Tom is "literally rolling in wealth", and Twain lists his treasures, from a dead rat to "a key that wouldn't unlock anything". The list is funny because the things are worthless, but to the boys they are riches.

The dialogue shows the boys' real speech: "ain't", "warn't", "lemme". Twain was one of the first writers to put everyday American speech into a serious novel, and this gave his writing great life.

## Sentences to imitate

> Tom gave up the brush with reluctance in his face, but alacrity in his heart.
A balanced contrast between what someone shows and what they feel. A useful pattern for describing hidden feelings.

> they came to jeer, but remained to whitewash.
A short sentence with two parallel verbs. The surprise in the second half makes it funny and memorable.

> That put the thing in a new light.
A simple phrase that means "made people see it differently". You can use it in your own writing.

> Tom swept his brush daintily back and forth—stepped back to note the effect—added a touch here and there—criticised the effect again
Dashes separate a series of small actions. This slows the scene down and lets the reader watch closely.

## Notes

- whitewash | A white liquid used to paint walls and fences; also, to paint with it. | 刷白；石灰水
- stump | "Up a stump" is old American slang for "in trouble, unable to escape". | （up a stump）陷入困境
- ain't | Informal or non-standard form of "am not / is not / are not / has not". | = am/is/are not（非标准口语）
- warn't => be | Dialect form of "wasn't" or "weren't". | = wasn't / weren't（方言）
- druther => rather | Dialect for "would rather". | = would rather（方言）
- let | "Let on" means "say or show that something is true", often falsely. | 假装；透露
- oughtn't | Short form of "ought not". | = ought not
- daintily | In a delicate, careful way. | 优雅地；细致地
- reckon | Informal: think, suppose. | 认为；猜想
- lemme => let | Spoken short form of "let me". | = let me（口语）
- injun | "Honest injun" was a children's phrase meaning "honestly, I promise". "Injun" is an offensive old word for Native Americans. | （honest injun）真的，说实话；“Injun”是对印第安人的冒犯性旧称
- fixed | Here "how I'm fixed" means "what situation I am in". | 处境
- tackle | Try to deal with a difficult task. | 着手处理
- shucks | An old American word showing mild disappointment, like "oh, come on". | 哎呀（表示不以为然）
- afeard => afraid | Dialect for "afraid". | = afraid（方言）
- alacrity | Quick and willing eagerness. | 欣然；乐意
- fagged | "Fagged out" is old slang for "very tired". | 累坏的
- jews-harp | A small musical instrument held between the teeth and played with a finger. | 口簧琴
- sash | Here, a window frame that holds the glass. | 窗框
