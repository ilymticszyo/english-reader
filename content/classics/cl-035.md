## Background

J. M. Barrie was a Scottish writer. Peter Pan first appeared in his play of 1904, and he turned the story into a novel, Peter and Wendy, in 1911. Peter is a boy who never grows up. He lives in the Neverland with the Lost Boys and his jealous little fairy, Tinker Bell.

In this chapter, Wendy and the boys have been captured by the pirate Captain Hook. Hook has also secretly put poison into Peter's medicine while Peter was asleep. Peter does not know this. Tinker Bell does. This excerpt is a famous, emotional moment, so it is marked as a spoiler. As you read, notice how the narrator suddenly speaks to you, the reader, and even asks you to take part in the story.

## Excerpt

“I’ll rescue her!” he cried, leaping at his weapons. As he leapt he thought of something he could do to please her. He could take his medicine.

His hand closed on the fatal draught.

“No!” shrieked Tinker Bell, who had heard Hook mutter about his deed as he sped through the forest.

“Why not?”

“It is poisoned.”

“Poisoned? Who could have poisoned it?”

“Hook.”

“Don’t be silly. How could Hook have got down here?”

Alas, Tinker Bell could not explain this, for even she did not know the dark secret of Slightly’s tree. Nevertheless Hook’s words had left no room for doubt. The cup was poisoned.

“Besides,” said Peter, quite believing himself, “I never fell asleep.”

He raised the cup. No time for words now; time for deeds; and with one of her lightning movements Tink got between his lips and the draught, and drained it to the dregs.

“Why, Tink, how dare you drink my medicine?”

But she did not answer. Already she was reeling in the air.

“What is the matter with you?” cried Peter, suddenly afraid.

“It was poisoned, Peter,” she told him softly; “and now I am going to be dead.”

“O Tink, did you drink it to save me?”

“Yes.”

“But why, Tink?”

Her wings would scarcely carry her now, but in reply she alighted on his shoulder and gave his nose a loving bite. She whispered in his ear “You silly ass,” and then, tottering to her chamber, lay down on the bed.

His head almost filled the fourth wall of her little room as he knelt near her in distress. Every moment her light was growing fainter; and he knew that if it went out she would be no more. She liked his tears so much that she put out her beautiful finger and let them run over it.

Her voice was so low that at first he could not make out what she said. Then he made it out. She was saying that she thought she could get well again if children believed in fairies.

Peter flung out his arms. There were no children there, and it was night time; but he addressed all who might be dreaming of the Neverland, and who were therefore nearer to him than you think: boys and girls in their nighties, and naked papooses in their baskets hung from trees.

“Do you believe?” he cried.

Tink sat up in bed almost briskly to listen to her fate.

She fancied she heard answers in the affirmative, and then again she wasn’t sure.

“What do you think?” she asked Peter.

“If you believe,” he shouted to them, “clap your hands; don’t let Tink die.”

Many clapped.

Some didn’t.

A few beasts hissed.

The clapping stopped suddenly; as if countless mothers had rushed to their nurseries to see what on earth was happening; but already Tink was saved. First her voice grew strong, then she popped out of bed, then she was flashing through the room more merry and impudent than ever. She never thought of thanking those who believed, but she would have liked to get at the ones who had hissed.

“And now to rescue Wendy!”

## Appreciation

The scene begins with fast, short lines of dialogue: "It is poisoned." "Poisoned?" "Hook." These quick exchanges build tension. Then Tink acts "with one of her lightning movements" and drinks the poison herself. Barrie shows her love through actions, not long speeches.

Her last words to Peter are wonderfully typical of her: she gives his nose "a loving bite" and whispers "You silly ass". Even when she is dying, she is cheeky. This mix of sadness and humour is what makes Barrie's writing special.

The most famous part comes next. Tink's light is fading, and she can only live "if children believed in fairies". Peter turns away from the story and speaks to all children who are dreaming, "nearer to him than you think". In the theatre, the actor playing Peter asks the real audience to clap. In the book, the narrator does the same to us. This breaking of the "fourth wall" (the invisible wall between a story and its audience) was very unusual, and it is still exciting.

Barrie is also funny and honest about the result: "Many clapped. Some didn't. A few beasts hissed." Three very short sentences, each one smaller. In the end Tink is saved, and she is "more merry and impudent than ever". She does not thank the believers, but she wants revenge on the ones who hissed. Barrie's fairies are not sweet angels; they have strong, human feelings.

## Sentences to imitate

> No time for words now; time for deeds
Two short balanced parts. A strong way to say that action is needed.

> “If you believe,” he shouted to them, “clap your hands; don’t let Tink die.”
A direct request to the audience. A conditional ("If you believe") followed by an order.

> Many clapped. Some didn’t. A few beasts hissed.
Three very short sentences that get smaller. Good for humour and surprise.

> First her voice grew strong, then she popped out of bed, then she was flashing through the room more merry and impudent than ever.
"First… then… then…" shows quick steps of recovery.

## Notes

- draught | Here it means a drink, especially of medicine; not cold air. | （药）饮剂
- dregs | The last drops at the bottom of a cup. | 渣滓；最后一点
- ass | Here it means a silly person (British, mild); in "You silly ass" it is affectionate. | 傻瓜
- papooses | An old word for Native American babies; today it is considered offensive. | 印第安婴儿（旧词，今含冒犯意）
- nighties | Night clothes, nightdresses. | 睡衣
- impudent | Rude and cheeky, with no respect. | 无礼的；冒失的
- affirmative | Meaning "yes". | 肯定的
