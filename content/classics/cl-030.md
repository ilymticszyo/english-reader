## Background

Herman Melville published Moby-Dick in 1851. It is a huge and ambitious novel about a whaling ship and its captain, Ahab, who wants revenge on the white whale that took his leg. At first the book was not a success; only in the twentieth century did readers begin to call it one of the greatest American novels.

This excerpt is the opening of the book. The narrator is a young man who asks us to call him Ishmael. Before he tells us about ships or whales, he explains why he goes to sea: whenever he feels low and gloomy, the ocean is his cure. Then he looks at New York City and notices that everyone seems drawn to the water. The style is playful, poetic and full of long sentences. Read it as a conversation: Ishmael often talks directly to "you", the reader.

## Excerpt

Call me Ishmael. Some years ago—never mind how long precisely—having little or no money in my purse, and nothing particular to interest me on shore, I thought I would sail about a little and see the watery part of the world. It is a way I have of driving off the spleen and regulating the circulation. Whenever I find myself growing grim about the mouth; whenever it is a damp, drizzly November in my soul; whenever I find myself involuntarily pausing before coffin warehouses, and bringing up the rear of every funeral I meet; and especially whenever my hypos get such an upper hand of me, that it requires a strong moral principle to prevent me from deliberately stepping into the street, and methodically knocking people’s hats off—then, I account it high time to get to sea as soon as I can. This is my substitute for pistol and ball. With a philosophical flourish Cato throws himself upon his sword; I quietly take to the ship. There is nothing surprising in this. If they but knew it, almost all men in their degree, some time or other, cherish very nearly the same feelings towards the ocean with me.

There now is your insular city of the Manhattoes, belted round by wharves as Indian isles by coral reefs—commerce surrounds it with her surf. Right and left, the streets take you waterward. Its extreme downtown is the battery, where that noble mole is washed by waves, and cooled by breezes, which a few hours previous were out of sight of land. Look at the crowds of water-gazers there.

Circumambulate the city of a dreamy Sabbath afternoon. Go from Corlears Hook to Coenties Slip, and from thence, by Whitehall, northward. What do you see?—Posted like silent sentinels all around the town, stand thousands upon thousands of mortal men fixed in ocean reveries. Some leaning against the spiles; some seated upon the pier-heads; some looking over the bulwarks of ships from China; some high aloft in the rigging, as if striving to get a still better seaward peep. But these are all landsmen; of week days pent up in lath and plaster—tied to counters, nailed to benches, clinched to desks. How then is this? Are the green fields gone? What do they here?

But look! here come more crowds, pacing straight for the water, and seemingly bound for a dive. Strange! Nothing will content them but the extremest limit of the land; loitering under the shady lee of yonder warehouses will not suffice. No. They must get just as nigh the water as they possibly can without falling in. And there they stand—miles of them—leagues. Inlanders all, they come from lanes and alleys, streets and avenues—north, east, south, and west. Yet here they all unite. Tell me, does the magnetic virtue of the needles of the compasses of all those ships attract them thither?

Once more. Say you are in the country; in some high land of lakes. Take almost any path you please, and ten to one it carries you down in a dale, and leaves you there by a pool in the stream. There is magic in it. Let the most absent-minded of men be plunged in his deepest reveries—stand that man on his legs, set his feet a-going, and he will infallibly lead you to water, if water there be in all that region. Should you ever be athirst in the great American desert, try this experiment, if your caravan happen to be supplied with a metaphysical professor. Yes, as every one knows, meditation and water are wedded for ever.

## Appreciation

"Call me Ishmael" is one of the most famous first lines in literature. It is only three words, but it raises questions. Is Ishmael his real name? In the Bible, Ishmael was an outcast, a person sent away from his family. The short sentence feels friendly and mysterious at the same time.

The rest of the first paragraph shows Melville's mix of humour and darkness. Ishmael describes his depression with funny images: "a damp, drizzly November in my soul", or the wish to go into the street and knock "people's hats off". Notice the repeated "whenever" at the start of each part. This build-up of clauses (anaphora) leads to the decision: "then, I account it high time to get to sea". He even calls the sea his "substitute for pistol and ball", a quiet, shocking hint at suicide, which he turns into a joke.

In the next paragraphs Ishmael becomes a guide. He uses commands ("Circumambulate the city", "look!") and questions ("What do you see?"). We walk with him through Manhattan and see "thousands upon thousands" of people staring at the sea. He describes office workers as "tied to counters, nailed to benches, clinched to desks". The three verbs suggest that ordinary jobs are a kind of prison.

The last paragraph widens the idea: any path in the countryside will lead you to water. Melville ends with a general truth: "meditation and water are wedded for ever." In this way, a simple wish to go sailing becomes a question about human nature. The ocean will be a place to think about the biggest mysteries of life.

## Sentences to imitate

> Call me Ishmael.
A three-word opening that is direct and mysterious. A short first line can be more powerful than a long one.

> Whenever I find myself growing grim about the mouth; whenever it is a damp, drizzly November in my soul
Repeating "whenever" builds a list of feelings. The metaphor "November in my soul" turns a mood into weather.

> With a philosophical flourish Cato throws himself upon his sword; I quietly take to the ship.
A contrast between a grand, famous action and a quiet personal one. The semicolon sets them side by side.

> But these are all landsmen; of week days pent up in lath and plaster—tied to counters, nailed to benches, clinched to desks.
Three parallel phrases with strong verbs of being stuck. Useful for describing a dull, trapped life.

> Yes, as every one knows, meditation and water are wedded for ever.
A confident general truth to finish a paragraph, with a gentle metaphor of marriage.

## Notes

- spleen | Here, bad temper and low spirits (an old idea about the body). | 忧郁；坏脾气
- hypos | Short for "hypochondria": here, gloomy, depressed feelings. | 忧郁症（hypochondria 的缩写）
- account | Here it means consider, think. | 认为
- ball | Here a round bullet ("pistol and ball"). | 弹丸
- cato | Cato the Younger, a Roman politician who killed himself with his sword rather than surrender. | 小加图（古罗马政治家）
- insular | Of an island. | 岛屿的
- manhattoes | An old name for the island of Manhattan and its people. | 曼哈顿（旧称）
- battery | Here, the Battery, a park at the southern tip of Manhattan. | （纽约）炮台公园
- mole | Here, a stone wall built into the sea, not an animal. | 防波堤
- circumambulate | Walk all the way around. | 绕行
- sabbath | The day of rest; for most Christians, Sunday. | 安息日
- sentinels | Soldiers who stand on guard. | 哨兵
- reveries | Pleasant daydreams. | 幻想；遐想
- spiles | Wooden posts driven into the ground to support a pier. | 桩子
- bulwarks | The sides of a ship above the deck. | 舷墙
- landsmen | People who live on land and do not know the sea. | 旱鸭子；陆地上的人
- lath | Thin strips of wood used in walls under plaster. | 板条
- clinched | Fastened firmly. | 钉牢；固定
- lee | The side of something that is sheltered from the wind. | 背风处
- nigh | Near (old word). | 近（古）
- leagues | Old units of distance, about three miles each. | 里格（旧长度单位）
- virtue | Here it means power or force, not "goodness". | 效力；力量
- dale | A valley. | 山谷
- infallibly | Without ever failing. | 必定；万无一失地
- athirst | Thirsty (old word). | 口渴的（古）
