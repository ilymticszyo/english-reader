## Background

Charles Dickens published A Tale of Two Cities in 1859. The story moves between London and Paris in the years before and during the French Revolution, which began in 1789. Dickens was writing about events about seventy years earlier, but he also wanted his readers to think about their own time.

This excerpt is the opening of the novel. The first sentence is one of the most quoted in English, and it is extremely long. Do not worry if you cannot follow every detail in the paragraphs after it. Dickens mentions real people and events from 1775, such as Joanna Southcott, a woman who claimed to be a prophet, and the "Cock-lane ghost", a famous fake. As you read, look for the pairs of opposite ideas, and notice the strong, dark images at the end of the excerpt. They give a warning of what is coming.

## Excerpt

It was the best of times, it was the worst of times, it was the age of wisdom, it was the age of foolishness, it was the epoch of belief, it was the epoch of incredulity, it was the season of Light, it was the season of Darkness, it was the spring of hope, it was the winter of despair, we had everything before us, we had nothing before us, we were all going direct to Heaven, we were all going direct the other way—in short, the period was so far like the present period, that some of its noisiest authorities insisted on its being received, for good or for evil, in the superlative degree of comparison only.

There were a king with a large jaw and a queen with a plain face, on the throne of England; there were a king with a large jaw and a queen with a fair face, on the throne of France. In both countries it was clearer than crystal to the lords of the State preserves of loaves and fishes, that things in general were settled for ever.

It was the year of Our Lord one thousand seven hundred and seventy-five. Spiritual revelations were conceded to England at that favoured period, as at this. Mrs. Southcott had recently attained her five-and-twentieth blessed birthday, of whom a prophetic private in the Life Guards had heralded the sublime appearance by announcing that arrangements were made for the swallowing up of London and Westminster. Even the Cock-lane ghost had been laid only a round dozen of years, after rapping out its messages, as the spirits of this very year last past (supernaturally deficient in originality) rapped out theirs. Mere messages in the earthly order of events had lately come to the English Crown and People, from a congress of British subjects in America: which, strange to relate, have proved more important to the human race than any communications yet received through any of the chickens of the Cock-lane brood.

France, less favoured on the whole as to matters spiritual than her sister of the shield and trident, rolled with exceeding smoothness down hill, making paper money and spending it. Under the guidance of her Christian pastors, she entertained herself, besides, with such humane achievements as sentencing a youth to have his hands cut off, his tongue torn out with pincers, and his body burned alive, because he had not kneeled down in the rain to do honour to a dirty procession of monks which passed within his view, at a distance of some fifty or sixty yards. It is likely enough that, rooted in the woods of France and Norway, there were growing trees, when that sufferer was put to death, already marked by the Woodman, Fate, to come down and be sawn into boards, to make a certain movable framework with a sack and a knife in it, terrible in history. It is likely enough that in the rough outhouses of some tillers of the heavy lands adjacent to Paris, there were sheltered from the weather that very day, rude carts, bespattered with rustic mire, snuffed about by pigs, and roosted in by poultry, which the Farmer, Death, had already set apart to be his tumbrils of the Revolution. But that Woodman and that Farmer, though they work unceasingly, work silently, and no one heard them as they went about with muffled tread: the rather, forasmuch as to entertain any suspicion that they were awake, was to be atheistical and traitorous.

## Appreciation

The famous first sentence is built from pairs of opposites: best and worst, wisdom and foolishness, light and darkness, hope and despair. This technique is called antithesis, putting two opposite ideas side by side. Dickens repeats the same pattern ("it was the… of…") again and again. The repetition creates a strong rhythm, almost like a song or a speech. Then the sentence suddenly changes direction with "in short", and we discover that this period was very like "the present period". The joke is that people always think their own time is either the best or the worst ever.

The second paragraph continues the pattern with England and France. The two kings are the same, and the queens are only slightly different: one has "a plain face", the other "a fair face". Dickens is saying that the rulers of both countries are equally comfortable and equally blind.

The tone changes in the last paragraph. Dickens describes a terrible punishment in very calm, polite language, calling it a "humane achievement". This is bitter irony. Then he turns the future into two characters, "the Woodman, Fate" and "the Farmer, Death". Giving a human form to an idea is called personification. The Woodman is choosing trees for the guillotine, and the Farmer is keeping the carts that will carry people to their deaths. Dickens never names the guillotine directly. He calls it "a certain movable framework with a sack and a knife in it", which is more frightening because we have to imagine it ourselves.

## Sentences to imitate

> It was the best of times, it was the worst of times
The classic example of antithesis: the same structure with opposite words. A memorable way to describe a period full of contrasts.

> In both countries it was clearer than crystal to the lords of the State preserves of loaves and fishes, that things in general were settled for ever.
Irony through confidence: the more certain the rulers are, the more we expect change. "Clearer than crystal" is a strong, simple comparison.

> already marked by the Woodman, Fate, to come down and be sawn into boards, to make a certain movable framework with a sack and a knife in it, terrible in history.
Personification and indirect description. Describing something by its parts can be more powerful than naming it.

> But that Woodman and that Farmer, though they work unceasingly, work silently, and no one heard them as they went about with muffled tread
Repeating "work" with two different adverbs creates a quiet, threatening rhythm.

## Notes

- epoch | A long period of time in history. | 时代；纪元
- incredulity | The feeling of not being able to believe something. | 怀疑；不相信
- superlative | The form of an adjective that means "the most" (best, worst). | 最高级
- preserves | Here, areas kept for the use of a few people, like private hunting land; the rulers' privileges. | 专属领地；特权
- southcott | Joanna Southcott (1750–1814), an Englishwoman who said she was a prophet. | 乔安娜·索思科特（自称先知）
- heralded | Announced that something was coming. | 预告；宣布
- cock-lane | A London street where a famous fake ghost was reported in 1762. | 考克巷（1762 年“闹鬼”骗局所在地）
- trident | A spear with three points; Britain is shown holding a shield and trident. | 三叉戟
- tumbrils | Carts used to carry prisoners to the guillotine during the French Revolution. | （法国大革命时）押送死囚的囚车
- forasmuch | An old formal word meaning "because, since". | 因为；鉴于（古）
