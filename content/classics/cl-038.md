## Background

Louisa May Alcott grew up in Massachusetts in a family with four daughters, and she used her own family as the model for Little Women (1868). The book follows the four March sisters as they grow up during the American Civil War (1861–1865).

This is the opening scene. Meg is the oldest and wants pretty things. Jo is a tomboy who loves books and writing. Beth is shy, gentle and musical. Amy, the youngest, wants to be elegant and uses big words, often wrongly. Their father is away with the army, and the family has little money. As you read, try to tell the sisters apart only by what they say, without looking back at this list. Alcott shows each girl's character through her complaints.

## Excerpt

“Christmas won’t be Christmas without any presents,” grumbled Jo, lying on the rug.

“It’s so dreadful to be poor!” sighed Meg, looking down at her old dress.

“I don’t think it’s fair for some girls to have plenty of pretty things, and other girls nothing at all,” added little Amy, with an injured sniff.

“We’ve got Father and Mother, and each other,” said Beth contentedly from her corner.

The four young faces on which the firelight shone brightened at the cheerful words, but darkened again as Jo said sadly, “We haven’t got Father, and shall not have him for a long time.” She didn’t say “perhaps never,” but each silently added it, thinking of Father far away, where the fighting was.

Nobody spoke for a minute; then Meg said in an altered tone, “You know the reason Mother proposed not having any presents this Christmas was because it is going to be a hard winter for everyone; and she thinks we ought not to spend money for pleasure, when our men are suffering so in the army. We can’t do much, but we can make our little sacrifices, and ought to do it gladly. But I am afraid I don’t,” and Meg shook her head, as she thought regretfully of all the pretty things she wanted.

“But I don’t think the little we should spend would do any good. We’ve each got a dollar, and the army wouldn’t be much helped by our giving that. I agree not to expect anything from Mother or you, but I do want to buy Undine and Sintran for myself. I’ve wanted it so long,” said Jo, who was a bookworm.

“I planned to spend mine in new music,” said Beth, with a little sigh, which no one heard but the hearth brush and kettle-holder.

“I shall get a nice box of Faber’s drawing pencils; I really need them,” said Amy decidedly.

“Mother didn’t say anything about our money, and she won’t wish us to give up everything. Let’s each buy what we want, and have a little fun; I’m sure we work hard enough to earn it,” cried Jo, examining the heels of her shoes in a gentlemanly manner.

“I know I do—teaching those tiresome children nearly all day, when I’m longing to enjoy myself at home,” began Meg, in the complaining tone again.

“You don’t have half such a hard time as I do,” said Jo. “How would you like to be shut up for hours with a nervous, fussy old lady, who keeps you trotting, is never satisfied, and worries you till you’re ready to fly out the window or cry?”

“It’s naughty to fret, but I do think washing dishes and keeping things tidy is the worst work in the world. It makes me cross, and my hands get so stiff, I can’t practice well at all.” And Beth looked at her rough hands with a sigh that any one could hear that time.

“I don’t believe any of you suffer as I do,” cried Amy, “for you don’t have to go to school with impertinent girls, who plague you if you don’t know your lessons, and laugh at your dresses, and label your father if he isn’t rich, and insult you when your nose isn’t nice.”

“If you mean libel, I’d say so, and not talk about labels, as if Papa was a pickle bottle,” advised Jo, laughing.

“I know what I mean, and you needn’t be statirical about it. It’s proper to use good words, and improve your vocabilary,” returned Amy, with dignity.

“Don’t peck at one another, children. Don’t you wish we had the money Papa lost when we were little, Jo? Dear me! How happy and good we’d be, if we had no worries!” said Meg, who could remember better times.

“You said the other day you thought we were a deal happier than the King children, for they were fighting and fretting all the time, in spite of their money.”

“So I did, Beth. Well, I think we are. For though we do have to work, we make fun of ourselves, and are a pretty jolly set, as Jo would say.”

“Jo does use such slang words!” observed Amy, with a reproving look at the long figure stretched on the rug.

Jo immediately sat up, put her hands in her pockets, and began to whistle.

“Don’t, Jo. It’s so boyish!”

“That’s why I do it.”

“I detest rude, unladylike girls!”

“I hate affected, niminy-piminy chits!”

“Birds in their little nests agree,” sang Beth, the peacemaker, with such a funny face that both sharp voices softened to a laugh, and the “pecking” ended for that time.

## Appreciation

Alcott begins with dialogue, not description. The first four lines come from the four sisters, one after another, and each line shows a personality. Jo grumbles, Meg sighs about being poor, Amy feels it is unfair, and Beth answers "contentedly" that they have each other. In a few seconds we know them all.

The mood changes quickly. When Jo mentions Father, the narrator tells us: "She didn't say 'perhaps never,' but each silently added it". This quiet sentence brings the danger of the war into a cosy room by the fire.

Much of the humour comes from Amy. She means "libel" but says "label", and Jo jokes that Papa is not "a pickle bottle". Amy then mixes up "satirical" and "vocabulary", saying "statirical" and "vocabilary" while trying to sound educated. Writers often use mistakes like these (called malapropisms) to make a character funny and real.

Jo is a new kind of heroine for the 1860s. She whistles, puts her hands in her pockets, and says she likes being "boyish". Readers loved her because she did not want to be a perfect young lady. Notice how the argument between Jo and Amy grows with short, sharp lines, until Beth stops it with a song. Beth is "the peacemaker", and the chapter shows her role in the family through one small action. The whole scene feels like a real family evening, and that is the secret of the book's long success.

## Sentences to imitate

> “Christmas won’t be Christmas without any presents,” grumbled Jo, lying on the rug.
A famous first line: start with a complaint that shows a character and a situation.

> “We’ve got Father and Mother, and each other,” said Beth contentedly from her corner.
A short, positive answer that changes the mood.

> She didn’t say “perhaps never,” but each silently added it
Showing what people think but do not say. A quiet way to add sadness.

> “I don’t think it’s fair for some girls to have plenty of pretty things, and other girls nothing at all,”
"Some… and others…" is a simple pattern for contrast.

## Notes

- injured | Here it means offended or hurt in feelings. | 委屈的
- bookworm | A person who loves reading. | 书呆子；爱读书的人
- impertinent | Rude, not showing respect. | 无礼的
- plague | Here it means annoy or trouble again and again. | 烦扰
- libel | A false written statement that damages someone's good name. | 诽谤
- statirical | Amy's mistake for "satirical", meaning making fun of someone. | （艾米把 satirical 说错了）讽刺的
- vocabilary | Amy's mistake for "vocabulary". | （艾米把 vocabulary 说错了）词汇
- niminy-piminy | Too delicate and fussy (old informal word). | 娇滴滴的
- chits | An old, rude word for silly young girls. | 黄毛丫头
- affected | Here it means not natural, trying to impress. | 做作的
