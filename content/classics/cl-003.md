## Background

This scene comes near the end of Pride and Prejudice, so it gives away how the main story ends. Months have passed since Elizabeth refused Darcy. Since then she has read his long letter, changed her opinion of him, and learned that he secretly saved her family from a scandal. Her youngest sister Lydia had run away with Mr. Wickham, and Darcy paid to make them marry.

Now Darcy has come back to the Bennets' neighbourhood. Elizabeth and Darcy are walking together, a little behind the others, and she finally decides to thank him. Compare this scene with his first proposal in Chapter 34. Both people speak more simply now. Pay attention to how Austen shows Elizabeth's answer: she does not give us Elizabeth's exact words at all. Why do you think she chooses to tell, rather than show, the most important moment?

## Excerpt

“Mr. Darcy, I am a very selfish creature, and for the sake of giving relief to my own feelings care not how much I may be wounding yours. I can no longer help thanking you for your unexampled kindness to my poor sister. Ever since I have known it I have been most anxious to acknowledge to you how gratefully I feel it. Were it known to the rest of my family I should not have merely my own gratitude to express.”

“I am sorry, exceedingly sorry,” replied Darcy, in a tone of surprise and emotion, “that you have ever been informed of what may, in a mistaken light, have given you uneasiness. I did not think Mrs. Gardiner was so little to be trusted.”

“You must not blame my aunt. Lydia’s thoughtlessness first betrayed to me that you had been concerned in the matter; and, of course, I could not rest till I knew the particulars. Let me thank you again and again, in the name of all my family, for that generous compassion which induced you to take so much trouble, and bear so many mortifications, for the sake of discovering them.”

“If you will thank me,” he replied, “let it be for yourself alone. That the wish of giving happiness to you might add force to the other inducements which led me on, I shall not attempt to deny. But your family owe me nothing. Much as I respect them, I believe I thought only of you.”

Elizabeth was too much embarrassed to say a word. After a short pause, her companion added, “You are too generous to trifle with me. If your feelings are still what they were last April, tell me so at once. My affections and wishes are unchanged; but one word from you will silence me on this subject for ever.”

Elizabeth, feeling all the more than common awkwardness and anxiety of his situation, now forced herself to speak; and immediately, though not very fluently, gave him to understand that her sentiments had undergone so material a change since the period to which he alluded, as to make her receive with gratitude and pleasure his present assurances. The happiness which this reply produced was such as he had probably never felt before; and he expressed himself on the occasion as sensibly and as warmly as a man violently in love can be supposed to do. Had Elizabeth been able to encounter his eyes, she might have seen how well the expression of heartfelt delight diffused over his face became him: but though she could not look she could listen; and he told her of feelings which, in proving of what importance she was to him, made his affection every moment more valuable.

They walked on without knowing in what direction. There was too much to be thought, and felt, and said, for attention to any other objects. She soon learnt that they were indebted for their present good understanding to the efforts of his aunt, who did call on him in her return through London, and there relate her journey to Longbourn, its motive, and the substance of her conversation with Elizabeth; dwelling emphatically on every expression of the latter, which, in her Ladyship’s apprehension, peculiarly denoted her perverseness and assurance, in the belief that such a relation must assist her endeavours to obtain that promise from her nephew which she had refused to give. But, unluckily for her Ladyship, its effect had been exactly contrariwise.

## Appreciation

The first proposal was full of long speeches and sharp attacks. This second scene is quieter, and the difference is the point. Elizabeth begins by calling herself "a very selfish creature". She is honest about her own feelings, which is a big change from the proud young woman who judged Darcy so quickly.

Darcy's language has changed too. He no longer talks about her family being below him. Instead he says, simply, "I believe I thought only of you." Austen keeps his sentence short, and the plain words feel more sincere than all his earlier eloquence.

His most important line is a careful, generous offer: "If your feelings are still what they were last April, tell me so at once." He gives her an easy way to say no. Then comes a balanced sentence: "My affections and wishes are unchanged; but one word from you will silence me on this subject for ever." The semicolon holds two ideas in balance: his strong love and his respect for her choice.

Then Austen does something surprising. She reports Elizabeth's answer in indirect speech and in rather formal language: her feelings had "undergone so material a change". Some readers find this funny; others find it moving. Austen seems to step back and give the couple some privacy. The joy is shown through Darcy instead: he expresses himself "as warmly as a man violently in love can be supposed to do". The small ironic phrase "can be supposed to" keeps the narrator's gentle humour alive, even at the happiest moment.

## Sentences to imitate

> Mr. Darcy, I am a very selfish creature, and for the sake of giving relief to my own feelings care not how much I may be wounding yours.
An honest, self-critical opening. Starting a difficult conversation by admitting your own fault can make it more sincere.

> Much as I respect them, I believe I thought only of you.
"Much as I…" means "Although I…". It is an elegant way to show contrast in a short sentence.

> If your feelings are still what they were last April, tell me so at once.
A clear, direct condition. Notice how simple and polite it is compared with his first proposal.

> They walked on without knowing in what direction.
A short sentence that shows deep feeling through a physical detail. They are so happy that they forget where they are going.

## Notes

- creature | Here it simply means a person; it is not an insult. | 人（旧时常用说法）
- unexampled | Never seen before; without any example. | 前所未有的
- mortifications | Feelings of shame and embarrassment. | 屈辱；难堪
- material | Here it means "important, large", not "substance". | 重大的；实质性的
- became | Here "became him" means "looked good on him, suited him". | 适合；与……相称
- relation | Here it means "a telling, an account" of events. | 叙述；讲述
- assurance | Here it means rude confidence, too much boldness. | 放肆；过分自信
- contrariwise | In the opposite way. | 相反地
