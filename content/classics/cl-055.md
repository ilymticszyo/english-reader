## Background

Susan Coolidge was the pen name of Sarah Chauncey Woolsey, an American writer. What Katy Did (1872) was one of the most popular American books for girls in the nineteenth century, and it was followed by several sequels.

Katy Carr is twelve years old and the oldest of six children. She is clever, wild, untidy and full of big plans, but she is often careless and gets into trouble. Her mother has died, and her father, a busy doctor, leaves the children in the care of his strict sister, Aunt Izzie. Later in the book Katy has a serious accident, and the story becomes about how she learns patience. This excerpt is the opening, which introduces the family and their home. As you read, notice how the narrator shows two very different ideas of what a "good" child is.

## Excerpt

Katy's name was Katy Carr. She lived in the town of Burnet, which wasn't a very big town, but was growing as fast as it knew how. The house she lived in stood on the edge of the town. It was a large square house, white, with green blinds, and had a porch in front, over which roses and clematis made a thick bower. Four tall locust trees shaded the gravel path which led to the front gate. On one side of the house was an orchard; on the other side were wood piles and barns, and an ice-house. Behind was a kitchen garden sloping to the south; and behind that a pasture with a brook in it, and butternut trees, and four cows—two red ones, a yellow one with sharp horns tipped with tin, and a dear little white one named Daisy.

There were six of the Carr children—four girls and two boys. Katy, the oldest, was twelve years old; little Phil, the youngest, was four, and the rest fitted in between.

Dr. Carr, their Papa, was a dear, kind, busy man, who was away from home all day, and sometimes all night, too, taking care of sick people. The children hadn't any Mamma. She had died when Phil was a baby, four years before my story began. Katy could remember her pretty well; to the rest she was but a sad, sweet name, spoken on Sunday, and at prayer-times, or when Papa was especially gentle and solemn.

In place of this Mamma, whom they recollected so dimly, there was Aunt Izzie, Papa's sister, who came to take care of them when Mamma went away on that long journey, from which, for so many months, the little ones kept hoping she might return. Aunt Izzie was a small woman, sharp-faced and thin, rather old-looking, and very neat and particular about everything. She meant to be kind to the children, but they puzzled her much, because they were not a bit like herself when she was a child. Aunt Izzie had been a gentle, tidy little thing, who loved to sit as Curly Locks did, sewing long seams in the parlor, and to have her head patted by older people, and be told that she was a good girl; whereas Katy tore her dress every day, hated sewing, and didn't care a button about being called "good," while Clover and Elsie shied off like restless ponies when any one tried to pat their heads. It was very perplexing to Aunt Izzie, and she found it hard to quite forgive the children for being so "unaccountable," and so little like the good boys and girls in Sunday-school memoirs, who were the young people she liked best, and understood most about.

Then Dr. Carr was another person who worried her. He wished to have the children hardy and bold, and encouraged climbing and rough plays, in spite of the bumps and ragged clothes which resulted. In fact, there was just one half-hour of the day when Aunt Izzie was really satisfied about her charges, and that was the half-hour before breakfast, when she had made a law that they were all to sit in their little chairs and learn the Bible verse for the day. At this time she looked at them with pleased eyes, they were all so spick and span, with such nicely-brushed jackets and such neatly-combed hair. But the moment the bell rang her comfort was over. From that time on, they were what she called "not fit to be seen." The neighbors pitied her very much. They used to count the sixty stiff white pantalette legs hung out to dry every Monday morning, and say to each other what a sight of washing those children made, and what a chore it must be for poor Miss Carr to keep them so nice. But poor Miss Carr didn't think them at all nice; that was the worst of it.

## Appreciation

The opening line is simple and direct: "Katy's name was Katy Carr." Then the narrator adds a small joke about the town, which "was growing as fast as it knew how". The town seems almost alive, like a child itself.

The description of the house is full of concrete details: the roses, the trees, the orchard, the brook, and four cows. The last cow is "a dear little white one named Daisy". The narrator describes the cows like a child would, by colour and character. This is a world seen at a child's height.

The sad fact about the mother is told very gently. For the younger children, she is "but a sad, sweet name". The phrase "that long journey" is how the family describes her death to the small children. The writer shows grief without heavy words.

The most interesting part is the contrast between Aunt Izzie and the children. As a girl, Aunt Izzie loved sewing and being called "good". Katy is the opposite: she "didn't care a button about being called 'good'". The narrator is clearly on the children's side. Aunt Izzie prefers the children in "Sunday-school memoirs", books about perfectly good children, which were common at the time. Coolidge is gently criticising those books.

The ending has a nice comic touch. The neighbours count "sixty stiff white pantalette legs" on the washing line. But "poor Miss Carr didn't think them at all nice; that was the worst of it." The sentence makes us smile at Aunt Izzie, and also feel a little sorry for her.

## Sentences to imitate

> She lived in the town of Burnet, which wasn't a very big town, but was growing as fast as it knew how.
A relative clause with a small joke to describe a place.

> She meant to be kind to the children, but they puzzled her much, because they were not a bit like herself when she was a child.
"Meant to… but…" shows good intentions that do not succeed.

> But the moment the bell rang her comfort was over.
"The moment…" means "as soon as".

> But poor Miss Carr didn't think them at all nice; that was the worst of it.
"That was the worst of it" adds a final, funny or sad point.

- bower | A shady place under trees or climbing plants. | 凉亭；树荫处
- locust | Here a kind of tree (the locust tree), not an insect. | 刺槐
- butternut | A kind of walnut tree. | 灰胡桃树
- recollected | Remembered. | 记得
- parlor | A sitting room in an old house (US spelling). | 客厅
- perplexing | Confusing and difficult to understand. | 令人困惑的
- unaccountable | Impossible to explain. | 无法解释的
- memoirs | Here books about the lives of (very good) children. | 传记
- spick | "Spick and span" means very clean and tidy. | 整洁的
