Few emotions are as universal, or as painful, as regret. We regret the words we said in anger, the opportunities we let pass, the career we did not pursue, and the person we did not call before it was too late. Regret seems to be a distinctly human feeling, because it depends on the ability to imagine a different past in which we acted otherwise. Yet despite its unpleasantness, psychologists increasingly argue that regret is not a flaw in our mental machinery but a useful part of it.
 
The key to understanding regret is that it involves comparison. We feel it when we measure what happened against what might have happened, and conclude that the alternative was better. For this reason, regret is closely related to responsibility. We rarely regret events that were entirely beyond our control, but we feel it keenly when we believe that a different choice was available. This helps to explain why decisions that turn out badly tend to cause more regret when they involved an action than when they involved doing nothing, at least in the short term. A person who changes jobs and fails feels sharper pain than one who stays and stagnates.
 
In the long run, however, the pattern is reversed. Researchers who have asked people to look back over their lives find that the most lasting regrets concern things not done: the journey not taken, the apology not offered, the talent not developed. Mistakes that we made can be explained, forgiven, or even turned into a good story. The paths we never tried remain open to endless imagining, and we cannot know that they would have disappointed us.
 
What function could such a painful emotion serve? One answer is that regret is a mechanism for learning. By making us feel the cost of poor decisions, it encourages us to avoid repeating them. A student who regrets not having studied is likely to prepare better for the next exam. In this sense, regret works like a teacher whose lessons are unpleasant but memorable. Experiments suggest that people who experience regret about a decision often make better choices later, especially if they are encouraged to reflect on what went wrong.
 
Regret can also strengthen social bonds. When we feel sorry for hurting someone, we are motivated to apologise and repair the relationship. The visible sign of remorse tells others that we recognise our fault and are unlikely to repeat it. A society in which no one ever regretted anything would be a cold and unreliable place.
 
But regret can also become destructive. Some people dwell on past errors for years, replaying them in their minds, and this rumination is associated with depression and anxiety. Others become so afraid of future regret that they cannot make decisions at all. Studies of shoppers show that those who always seek the best possible option are often less satisfied with their purchases than those who accept a good one, since they continually wonder whether something better existed.
 
How, then, should we live with regret? Therapists often recommend a few practices. The first is to separate what we could reasonably have known at the time from what we know now, since hindsight makes the past look more predictable than it was. The second is to treat regret as information, asking what it reveals about our values. If we regret not spending time with family, that tells us something that we can act on today. The third is self-compassion: treating ourselves with the same understanding that we would offer to a friend.
 
A life without regret is probably neither possible nor desirable. It would imply either that we never took risks or that we never cared about the consequences. The aim, perhaps, is not to eliminate regret but to listen to it, to learn what it teaches, and then to let it go.
