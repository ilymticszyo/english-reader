The word "nostalgia" was coined in 1688 by a Swiss medical student, who combined two Greek words meaning "return home" and "pain". He was describing a condition observed among Swiss soldiers serving abroad, who, it was believed, could fall seriously ill from homesickness; some physicians even recommended leeches or a trip back to the mountains as treatment. For two centuries, nostalgia was regarded as a disease. Today, it is more often regarded as a feeling, and a rather pleasant one, though the question of whether it is healthy or harmful remains surprisingly contested.

Psychologists who study nostalgia describe it as a bittersweet emotion, a warm recollection of the past mixed with the awareness that it cannot be recovered. Contrary to what one might expect, experiments suggest that people usually turn to nostalgic memories when they feel lonely, anxious, or uncertain about the future. Recalling a happy family gathering or a favourite song from adolescence seems to restore a sense of belonging and meaning. In one study, participants who were asked to write about a nostalgic episode reported feeling more optimistic and more connected to other people than those who wrote about an ordinary event. In this light, nostalgia is less a flight from reality than a resource that helps us to cope with difficulty.

Yet the feeling has a darker side when it moves from the personal to the political. Collective nostalgia, the longing of a whole group for a supposedly better age, has proved a powerful tool for politicians. Slogans that promise to restore past greatness appeal to people who feel that the world has become unfamiliar and threatening. The difficulty is that the golden age they invoke usually never existed in the form that is remembered. Selective memory removes the poverty, the discrimination, and the hardship that ordinary people experienced, leaving only a gentle glow. Historians sometimes call this "restorative nostalgia", and warn that it can be used to justify hostility towards those who are blamed for the supposed decline.

Commercial interests have also learned to exploit the emotion. Television networks revive old programmes, film studios produce endless sequels, and fashion cycles return to styles that were popular a generation earlier. The appeal is easy to understand. Familiar products carry a promise of comfort, and a person who feels uneasy about the present may be willing to pay for a reminder of a time when life seemed simpler. Even the music industry has noticed that listeners aged over thirty tend to stop discovering new songs and return, again and again, to the music of their teenage years.

A curious feature of nostalgia is that it can attach itself to experiences that we did not have ourselves. Young people today sometimes express an affection for the clothes, technology, and music of decades before their birth, and a few have become collectors of cassette tapes and film cameras. Researchers have called this "anemoia", a word invented to describe a longing for a time one has never known, though the term has not been widely adopted by scholars. It suggests that what we miss may not be a particular period at all, but a quality that we imagine that period possessed: slowness, simplicity, or a closer sense of community.

How, then, should we regard this complicated emotion? Perhaps the most sensible answer is to distinguish between two kinds. A nostalgia that acknowledges the past with affection, while recognising its flaws, can provide comfort and continuity, and there is little reason to resist it. A nostalgia that insists on the superiority of an invented past, and demands that the present be reshaped to match it, deserves far more scepticism. The difference lies, as so often, in honesty. To remember fondly is human; to forget selectively, and to build a politics on that forgetting, is dangerous.
