Few features of education provoke as much controversy as the standardised test. Every year, millions of students across the world sit examinations that will influence the schools they attend, the jobs they obtain, and even the way they think about themselves. Supporters see such tests as the fairest available way of measuring achievement. Critics regard them as a narrow and distorting instrument that shapes teaching in unhealthy ways. Both sides have a point, and understanding why requires a closer look at what tests can and cannot do.
 
The case for testing begins with comparability. If every student answers the same questions under the same conditions, and the papers are marked by the same criteria, then the results can be compared across schools and regions. Without such measures, supporters argue, it would be impossible to tell whether a particular school was succeeding or failing, and the temptation to rely on personal connections or the reputation of a family would be much greater. Tests have, in the past, opened doors for talented students from modest backgrounds, who might otherwise have been overlooked. Moreover, a regular check on what children have learned allows teachers to identify those who are falling behind.
 
Critics begin by pointing out what is not measured. Examinations are best at testing facts, procedures, and the ability to answer questions quickly, but much less effective at assessing creativity, persistence, collaboration, or the ability to formulate good questions, qualities that employers often say they value. A student may have a deep understanding of a subject yet perform poorly because of anxiety, while another may do well by learning tricks for the exam. Test scores also reflect advantages unrelated to ability: children from wealthier families often benefit from private tutors and from environments that prepare them well.
 
A further difficulty is that tests change the behaviour of those who are measured. When the results determine a school's reputation, funding, or a teacher's pay, there is a strong incentive to concentrate on what will be tested. Subjects that are not examined, such as art, music, and physical education, may be neglected. Lessons may turn into practice for the test, and some schools have been caught encouraging weaker students to stay at home on examination day. The economist Charles Goodhart expressed the general problem in a well-known saying: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.
 
Researchers who have studied the effects of high-stakes testing have found mixed results. In some cases, the pressure to improve has raised scores in the tested subjects, particularly in reading and mathematics, but gains often prove smaller on other measures of learning, suggesting that some improvement reflects familiarity with the test rather than genuine understanding. There is also evidence of rising stress among pupils.
 
Many education systems are now looking for compromise. Some have reduced the frequency of national tests, relying more on teachers' assessments and on samples of pupils rather than on everyone. Others combine examinations with projects, portfolios, and oral presentations. Technology offers the possibility of tests that adapt to the level of each student, and that provide detailed feedback rather than a single grade.
 
International comparisons add a further dimension. Governments pay close attention to rankings that compare the performance of fifteen-year-olds in different countries, and a poor result can trigger national debate and rapid reform. Yet researchers warn that such rankings hide differences in culture, wealth, and the groups of children who take part, and that countries which climb the table are not always those whose pupils are happiest or best prepared for adult life. A single number can be useful as a prompt for questions, but it is a poor guide to the quality of an entire education system.
 
The question is not whether to measure learning, since some measurement is necessary and valuable, but how to do it wisely. A good assessment system would use several kinds of evidence, treat scores with humility, and remember that the purpose of education is far wider than the ability to pass an examination.
