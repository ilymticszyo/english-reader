Walk into any bookshop and you will find shelves devoted to memoirs: accounts of childhoods in poverty, struggles with illness, journeys across continents, escapes from difficult families, and a thousand other experiences, written by celebrities and unknown authors alike. The genre has become one of the most popular in contemporary publishing, and, in the opinion of some critics, one of the most problematic. Why has the personal story become so central to modern culture, and what does it do to the truth?
 
Autobiography, of course, is ancient. Augustine wrote his Confessions in the fourth century, and Rousseau's account of his own life, published after his death in 1782, shocked its first readers with its candour. But the traditional autobiography typically covered a whole career, and its authors were usually people who had achieved something noteworthy in public life. The modern memoir differs in focus and in ambition. It concentrates on a particular period or theme, and its subject need not be famous at all. What entitles the author to write is not achievement, but experience, and the capacity to reflect upon it in a way that others will find illuminating.
 
Several developments explain the boom. One is the growing weight that society places on personal testimony, especially from those whose voices were previously unheard. Memoirs by members of minority groups, by survivors of abuse, and by people with mental illness or disabilities have given readers access to lives that mainstream culture had overlooked, and have helped to change attitudes. Another is the influence of therapeutic culture, in which the telling of one's story is regarded as a means of healing. A third is technology: blogs and social media have taught millions of people to narrate their lives in public, and the memoir is a natural extension of the habit.
 
Yet the form raises difficult questions about accuracy. Memory is notoriously unreliable, and a writer who reconstructs conversations from decades earlier is inevitably imagining some of the detail. Everyone remembers an event differently, and the people who appear in a memoir, who cannot reply, may feel that they have been presented unfairly. Occasionally, the problems are more serious. A number of celebrated memoirs have been exposed as partly or wholly invented, and their authors accused of deceiving readers who bought the books as truthful accounts. The scandals have prompted debate about whether writers have a duty to use only what can be checked, or whether the essential truth of an experience can legitimately be conveyed through invention.
 
A related matter is ethics. A memoirist writes about other people: parents, former partners, friends, colleagues. They did not choose to appear in the book, and publication may harm their reputations or relationships. Some writers change names or ask for permission; others argue that their own story inevitably includes the people who shaped it, and that silence would amount to censorship. Where to draw the line has no simple answer, and it may be that responsible authors are those who weigh the damage and the value in each case.
 
Teachers have noticed the genre's usefulness. Writing about one's own life can be an effective exercise for students, since it demands careful observation, selection of detail, and honest reflection, skills that carry over to other forms of writing. Yet it also has a danger, which is that young writers may suppose that the only subject worth writing about is themselves.
 
At its best, a memoir is not an exercise in self-display but a work of art: a shaped, reflective account that, by being deeply particular, illuminates something universal. Readers of a good memoir do not merely learn about another person; they recognise themselves. That is the quality that explains the genre's appeal, and the reason why, whatever its faults, it is likely to endure.
