In the 1960s, a student in many wealthy countries could attend university without paying any fees, and in some places could even receive a grant to cover living costs. Today, in a number of those same countries, graduates leave with debts of tens of thousands of pounds or dollars that will take decades to clear. The shift is one of the most significant, and least debated, changes in modern social policy. It raises a question to which there is no obviously correct answer: who should pay for higher education, and why?

The case for public funding rests on the idea that education benefits society as a whole. A population with advanced skills is more productive, more innovative, and, according to many studies, healthier and more engaged in civic life. Graduates also pay higher taxes over their working lives, so that, in the long run, the state may recover its investment. If the costs fall entirely on students, the argument continues, some capable young people will be discouraged from applying, particularly those from poorer families who fear debt more than their wealthier classmates do. A system that depends on private payment may therefore waste talent and reinforce existing inequalities.

The opposing view emphasises fairness in a different sense. University graduates, on average, earn considerably more than people who do not attend, which means that they receive a substantial private benefit. Why, ask supporters of fees, should a shop assistant or a bus driver, whose taxes help to pay for universities, subsidise the education of a future lawyer or banker? In several countries, students from the richest families are in fact the most likely to attend, so a system funded entirely by taxation can be a transfer of resources from the poor to the better-off. Moreover, as the number of students has increased, governments have found it increasingly difficult to cover the costs from public budgets alone, especially when they must also pay for schools, hospitals, and pensions.

Different countries have reached different compromises. In some Nordic states, tuition remains free, financed by high taxes. Elsewhere, such as in the United States, students and their families bear much of the cost, with the result that fees at prestigious institutions have risen far faster than inflation. A third model, introduced in Australia and later adopted in England and other countries, relies on income-contingent loans. Students pay nothing at the start, and then repay a percentage of their earnings once they pass a certain threshold; any balance remaining after a number of years is cancelled. In principle, this removes the fear of an unmanageable burden, since payments are linked to ability to pay.

Even so, the loan system has its critics. They note that, because many graduates will never repay the full amount, the state still bears a large part of the cost, but in a way that is less visible and harder to plan. Others argue that the language of debt changes how students think about education, encouraging them to see it as a product that should deliver a financial return, rather than as a way of understanding the world. Choosing a course in philosophy or history, which may not lead to a high salary, could come to seem like an irresponsible luxury.

There may be no perfect arrangement, but the debate would be improved by greater honesty about its terms. Education is at once a private good and a public one, and any system must decide how to divide the burden between them. What matters is that the decision is made deliberately, with an eye to the kind of society it will create.
