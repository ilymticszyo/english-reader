A single honeybee can seem very small and unimportant. It lives for only a few weeks in summer, and during its whole life, it makes less than a teaspoon of honey. Yet a hive, with up to fifty thousand bees, behaves like a single living thing. It controls its own temperature, finds food over many kilometres, and defends itself against enemies, all without a leader who gives orders.

The queen, which is the only bee that lays eggs, is often imagined as a ruler. In fact, she does not command anyone. Her main job is to produce up to two thousand eggs a day in the warm months, and her smell tells the other bees that she is healthy. Most of the hive is made up of female workers, and their jobs change as they grow older. The youngest clean the cells and feed the young. Later, they build the wax, guard the entrance, and store the food. Only in the last weeks of their lives do they fly out to collect nectar and pollen.

How do the bees decide where to find food? The answer is one of the most famous discoveries in animal behaviour. When a worker finds a good field of flowers, she returns to the hive and performs a dance. The angle of the dance, compared with the position of the sun, shows the direction of the food, and the length of the dance tells the others how far to fly. This is called the waggle dance, and it was decoded by an Austrian scientist in the 1940s, for which he later won a Nobel Prize.

Bees also make decisions as a group. When a hive becomes too crowded, a large part of the colony leaves with the old queen to find a new home. Hundreds of scouts search the area and come back to dance for the places they have found. Other bees fly out to check, and if they agree, they dance as well. Slowly, the dances for the best site become more numerous than the rest, and when enough bees agree, the whole swarm moves. The process works like a vote, and it nearly always selects the best place.

Bees are not only fascinating but also essential. They carry pollen from flower to flower, which helps plants to produce fruit and seeds. About a third of the food that we eat depends, at least in part, on this work. Yet in many countries, bee populations are falling. Scientists blame a mixture of causes, including disease, the loss of wild flowers, and the chemicals used on farms.

There are things that ordinary people can do to help. Planting flowers on a balcony or in a garden, avoiding strong chemicals, and buying honey from local producers all make a difference. For a creature so small, the bee has a very large effect on our lives.
