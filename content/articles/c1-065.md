In 2019, an international scientific panel produced a report that began with a stark statement: around a million species of plants and animals are now threatened with extinction, many within decades. The current rate of loss, the authors said, is tens to hundreds of times higher than the average over the past ten million years. Such statistics are alarming, but they raise an awkward question. Why should most people care if some obscure beetle or flower disappears? What, in practical terms, is the value of biodiversity?
 
One answer is that nature supplies a wide range of services on which human life depends, often without our noticing. Bees and other insects pollinate crops. Wetlands filter water and absorb floods. Forests store carbon, regulate rainfall, and protect soil from erosion. Mangrove trees along the coast reduce the damage caused by storms. Economists have tried to estimate the monetary value of these services, and the figures are enormous, in some calculations exceeding the output of the global economy. Even if such numbers are rough, they show how costly it would be to replace natural processes with machines.
 
A second argument concerns resilience. Ecosystems with many species tend to be more stable, much as a diversified investment portfolio is safer than one with a single asset. If one species declines, others can take over its role. Conversely, simplified systems, such as vast fields of a single crop, are vulnerable to disease and changes in climate. Biodiversity may also hold information that we do not yet know how to use. Many medicines originated in plants and microorganisms, and a species lost before it is studied may take with it a possible cure.
 
There is, however, a third view that does not depend on usefulness at all. Many people feel that nature has value in itself, regardless of what it can do for us, and that human beings have a duty to the other living things with which they share the planet. Philosophers disagree about the basis of this claim, but it echoes in many cultures and religions, and it informs the conviction that extinction is not only a practical problem but a moral one. A world without wild animals, many people feel, would be an impoverished world, even if its economy continued to run.
 
The causes of the current loss are well understood. The main one is the change in the use of land and sea: forests converted to farms, wetlands drained, oceans overfished. Others include the direct harvesting of animals, climate change, pollution, and the spread of invasive species carried around the world by trade. These pressures often act together, and their combined effect is greater than the sum of the parts.
 
Efforts to halt the decline take several forms. Protected areas are the oldest tool, and governments recently agreed to aim to protect thirty percent of land and sea by 2030. Yet a park on paper does not guarantee protection, and many reserves lack funds for enforcement. Moreover, nature outside reserves matters too, so that sustainable farming and fishing, the restoration of damaged habitats, and wildlife corridors that link isolated areas are all important. Some projects have succeeded dramatically: species such as the humpback whale and the bald eagle have recovered after protection.
 
Local people must be involved. Conservation that excludes those who live on the land has often failed and sometimes caused injustice, while projects that give communities a stake in protecting nature, through income from tourism or sustainable harvesting, have often done better. Finance is also changing, as some governments and companies begin to pay for the services that ecosystems provide.
 
Whether the world can slow the loss in time is uncertain. But the choice is not simply between economy and environment. As the scientists emphasised, the two cannot be separated, for the health of one rests on the health of the other.
