Few subjects divide parents, teachers, and researchers as sharply as the effect of smartphones on young people. In recent years, rates of anxiety, depression, and self-harm among adolescents have risen in several wealthy countries, and the timing of the rise, which began around the time when smartphones and social media became universal, has led many to conclude that the devices are to blame. Some schools have banned phones, and a number of governments are considering restrictions on social media for children. Yet the scientific picture is less clear than the public debate would suggest, and an honest assessment requires distinguishing between what is well established and what is merely plausible.
 
The case against the phone begins with correlation. Surveys show that teenagers who spend several hours a day on social media are more likely to report symptoms of depression and low self-esteem. There are plausible reasons for this. The constant comparison with carefully edited images of other people's lives can damage self-image, particularly among girls. Online bullying follows young people into their bedrooms, where once they might have escaped it at home. Late-night scrolling reduces sleep, which is itself a powerful factor in mental health, and the notifications that interrupt every activity may undermine concentration and the ability to enjoy face-to-face company.
 
However, researchers who analyse large data sets have found that the average association between screen time and wellbeing is surprisingly small, comparable, in one influential analysis, to the association between wellbeing and eating potatoes. Critics of the alarmist view add that correlation, again, does not prove causation. Teenagers who feel unhappy may turn to their phones for distraction or company, rather than the other way round. And the rise in mental distress might have other causes, including academic pressure, economic insecurity, a greater willingness to talk about and report problems, and changes in the way that health services diagnose them.
 
The proponents of the harm hypothesis reply that average effects can conceal large effects on particular groups. A small average might hide serious damage to a minority of vulnerable adolescents, who deserve protection even if most teenagers are unaffected. They point to experiments in which volunteers who reduced their use of social media for several weeks reported modest improvements in mood. The difficulty is that these studies are short, the effects are small, and participants know which group they are in, which may influence their answers.
 
What is certain is that the way in which phones are used matters more than the amount of time they are used. Passive scrolling, in which one watches other people's content without interacting, seems to be associated with worse outcomes than active communication with close friends. For a teenager who is isolated, perhaps because of distance, disability, or a minority identity, online communities can be a lifeline, offering support that is unavailable nearby. A blanket ban would remove that benefit along with the harm.
 
Another point on which there is broad agreement concerns sleep. Teenagers who keep their phones in the bedroom at night sleep less and worse, and the removal of devices from the bedroom is a simple measure that costs nothing and has a plausible benefit. Similarly, the design of many apps, which use endless feeds, automatic video, and rewards that unpredictably appear, is intended to hold attention for as long as possible, and young people with their developing self-control are especially exposed to it.
 
For parents and educators, the evidence suggests a sensible but unspectacular course. Rather than counting hours, they might ask how a child uses a device, whether it replaces sleep, exercise, or friendships, and whether the young person seems distressed. Conversations are more productive than confiscations. Schools that have restricted phones during lessons report better concentration, although it remains to be shown whether this improves wellbeing. For governments, the argument for regulating the design of platforms, instead of simply prohibiting access, is at least worth serious consideration.
 
The honest conclusion is that we do not yet know how large the harm is, and that certainty in either direction is premature. In the meantime, humility and careful attention to individual children may serve better than moral panic.
