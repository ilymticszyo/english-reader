In the middle of Antarctica and Greenland, snow falls year after year and never melts. Each layer is pressed by the weight of the next, slowly turning into ice, and in the process it traps tiny bubbles of the air that existed when the snow fell. A sheet of ice thousands of metres thick is therefore more than frozen water. It is an archive of the atmosphere, and scientists who drill deep into it can read the history of our planet's climate across hundreds of thousands of years.

The technique is both simple in principle and enormously demanding in practice. Researchers use hollow drills to extract long cylinders of ice, called cores, which are cut into sections, labelled, and carried carefully to laboratories where they are stored at temperatures far below freezing. Some drilling projects have lasted many years, in remote camps where winter temperatures can fall below minus fifty degrees and where every piece of equipment must be flown in or hauled across the snow. The longest cores recovered so far, from a site in Antarctica, contain ice that fell as snow about eight hundred thousand years ago.

The information stored in the ice comes in several forms. The most direct is the air trapped in bubbles, which can be analysed to measure the concentration of gases such as carbon dioxide and methane. The results show a clear pattern: during the cold periods known as ice ages, these gases were at low levels, and during the warmer intervals between them, they rose. The relationship between greenhouse gases and temperature, in other words, is written into the ice. It also reveals that the present concentration of carbon dioxide is higher than at any time in the entire record, by a wide margin, and that it has risen at a speed with no precedent in those eight hundred thousand years.

The ice also holds other evidence. The chemical composition of the water molecules themselves changes slightly with temperature, so that the ratio of certain atoms provides an estimate of how warm it was when the snow fell. Layers of dust show when the climate was dry and windy. Particles from volcanic eruptions appear as sharp spikes, and historians have used them to date events whose records are uncertain. Even traces of lead from ancient smelting can be found, revealing the activity of the Roman economy in far-off northern snow. More recently, radioactive isotopes from nuclear weapons tests in the twentieth century created a marker that helps to confirm the dating of the upper layers.

Reading the record requires careful interpretation. Deeper ice is compressed and stretched by the pressure of the weight above, so the annual layers become thinner and harder to distinguish. Gases can also move slightly through the ice, and the air is younger than the surrounding ice, because bubbles close off only after the snow has been compacted over decades. Scientists spend years refining their methods, and by combining several lines of evidence, they reduce the uncertainty.

Some of the most important discoveries concern how quickly the climate can change. In the Greenland ice, researchers found evidence that, at the end of the last ice age, temperatures in the region rose by many degrees within a few decades, much faster than gradual changes would have suggested. This revealed that the climate system can behave in sudden, nonlinear ways, with thresholds beyond which change accelerates. The finding is one reason why scientists take the possibility of abrupt shifts seriously.

The ice sheets themselves are now under threat. Melting in Greenland and parts of Antarctica is adding to the sea level, and glaciers around the world, which have also preserved ancient records, are shrinking. Some scientists have therefore begun to collect cores from endangered glaciers and to store them in a freezer in Antarctica, a kind of library for future researchers who may have better techniques.

There is a certain poignancy in this work. The archive that took hundreds of thousands of years to form is disappearing within a few generations, while the information that it contains reveals how we came to be here. Ice cores tell us that the planet has changed before, and they also tell us, with unusual clarity, that it has rarely changed so fast.
