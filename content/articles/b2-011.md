Not long ago, people bought new clothes a few times a year, when the seasons changed or when something wore out. Today, many shops offer new styles every week, and prices are so low that a shirt can cost less than a sandwich. This business model is known as fast fashion, and it is popular for obvious reasons. But a growing number of experts argue that its real price is far higher than the number on the label.

Consider the environmental impact first. Making clothes uses enormous quantities of water, especially cotton, which needs a lot of water to grow. Dyeing fabric also pollutes rivers in several countries where factories are poorly regulated. In addition, most cheap clothes are now made from synthetic fibres, which come from oil. Every time such clothes are washed, they release tiny pieces of plastic that travel through the drains and eventually reach the sea.

Then there is the problem of waste. Because fast fashion encourages us to treat clothes as disposable, millions of tonnes of textiles are thrown away every year. Much of this material is burned or buried, because it is difficult to recycle fabric that mixes different fibres. Charities, which once welcomed second-hand clothes, now say they receive far more than they can sell.

The human cost is just as serious. To keep prices low, many companies order their products from countries where wages are small and working conditions can be unsafe. A factory collapse in Bangladesh in 2013, which killed more than a thousand people, shocked the world and forced some brands to promise changes. Yet campaigners say that progress has been slow, and that workers still struggle to earn enough to live on.

Defenders of the industry reply that it provides millions of jobs in poor countries and allows people on low incomes to dress well. They have a point. Simply stopping production would harm the very people we want to help. The challenge is to improve the system, not to destroy it.

So what can ordinary shoppers do? Buying fewer, better things is the most effective step. A well-made jacket that lasts ten years is cheaper in the long run than five weak ones. We can also repair clothes, swap them with friends, or buy them second-hand, which has become surprisingly fashionable. Reading labels and supporting companies that are open about their factories also puts pressure on the industry.

Governments have a role, too. Some countries are now making brands responsible for the waste they create, and others are demanding more honest information about how clothes are made.

Fashion should be a pleasure, not a burden on the planet or on the people who make it. If we start to value our clothes more, the industry will have to follow.
