About one person in ten is left-handed. This proportion has been remarkably stable across history and in most cultures. Archaeologists have found evidence of left-handed people in ancient cave paintings and tools. Yet scientists still cannot fully explain why some people favour the left hand, or why they are a minority.
 
The first thing to say is that handedness is connected to the brain. In most people, the left side of the brain controls language and also controls the right hand. In many left-handed people, the pattern is reversed or more mixed, although about seven in ten still have language centres on the left side. This suggests that the connection is not simple.
 
Genes play a role, but not a large one. If both parents are right-handed, the chance of having a left-handed child is about ten percent. If both are left-handed, it rises to about twenty-five percent. Even identical twins, who share the same genes, sometimes differ in handedness. So other factors, perhaps the environment in the womb, must also matter.
 
Why has left-handedness survived? One theory is that it provides an advantage in fighting. In sports such as tennis, boxing, and fencing, left-handed players are over-represented among the top performers. An opponent who has mostly trained against right-handed people finds the left-handed style unfamiliar. In ancient times, this surprise may have helped fighters survive. At the same time, the advantage works only if the left-handed are rare, which may explain why they never became the majority.
 
In everyday life, being left-handed has been a nuisance. Many tools, such as scissors, can openers, and desks, are designed for right-handed use. For centuries, children were forced to write with the right hand, and being left-handed was linked in many cultures with bad luck. The word "sinister" comes from the Latin word for "left". In some languages, the word for "right" also means "correct".
 
Research on health has produced mixed results. Some early studies reported that left-handed people were more likely to suffer accidents or illnesses, but later work has found no clear difference. Others have suggested that they are more creative, and the list of famous left-handed artists and thinkers is impressive. But careful studies have not confirmed any major difference in intelligence.
 
Today, attitudes are changing. Schools no longer try to change children's natural preference, and shops sell tools designed for the left hand. Scientists continue to study the brain to understand how preferences develop. For now, the origin of handedness remains a puzzle, one that reminds us that even simple differences between people can have complicated causes.
