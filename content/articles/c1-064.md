For many people in many cities, the daily journey to work is a source of frustration. Buses are late, trains are crowded, and connections are poor, so that anyone with the means chooses to drive. Yet the car, which promised freedom, has delivered congestion, pollution, and dangerous streets. As cities grow and climate targets become more demanding, planners and politicians are asking again how to persuade people to leave their cars at home. The answer, it seems, lies in making public transport not merely cheaper, but genuinely better.
 
The case for investment rests on arithmetic. A bus can carry fifty people in the space occupied by a handful of cars, and a train line can move tens of thousands of passengers an hour, far more than a motorway lane. In cities where space is limited, this efficiency is essential. Public transport also produces much lower emissions per passenger, uses less land, and is safer. For people who cannot drive, such as the young, the old, and the poor, it provides access to jobs, education, and healthcare, and in this sense, it is a basic public service.
 
Yet most systems struggle with a vicious circle. When ridership falls, services are cut, which makes the system less useful, which pushes more people away. Fares rise to cover costs, and the poorest passengers suffer most. Many systems also face an immense backlog of maintenance, as old tracks and vehicles require expensive repairs. The pandemic made all this worse, as passengers who began to work from home did not all return.
 
Experience from successful cities suggests that the key factors are frequency, reliability, and coverage. People will not plan their lives around a bus that comes once an hour, but they will use one that comes every ten minutes, because they can simply turn up. Dedicated lanes for buses, which allow them to avoid traffic, can greatly improve speed at modest cost; in several Latin American cities, such "rapid transit" systems carry as many passengers as underground railways, at a fraction of the price. Simple ticketing, with one card or app for all services, makes journeys easier, and clear information about arrival times reduces the stress of waiting.
 
Technology offers some new possibilities. Real-time data allows passengers to see exactly where their bus is, and operators to adjust services according to demand. Electric buses are quieter and cleaner than diesel ones, and several cities have committed to replace their fleets. Self-driving vehicles are often promised as a revolution, but their benefits for public transport remain uncertain, and many experts warn that, if they simply replace individual cars, they might increase traffic rather than reduce it. More modest innovations, such as on-demand minibuses in areas where regular services are not viable, show promise.
 
Money, however, remains the central question. Almost no system in the world covers its costs from fares alone, and subsidies are the norm. Some cities have tried making travel free, which can increase use, although studies suggest that many of the new passengers are people who would otherwise have walked or cycled. Others finance transport through taxes on property near new stations, on the grounds that the arrival of a line raises land values, or through charges on drivers entering congested areas. These charges have worked in several cities, reducing traffic and raising funds that are then spent on alternatives, although they are politically difficult to introduce.
 
Finally, the most effective transport policy may be about more than transport. Housing near stations, mixed neighbourhoods, and safe walking and cycling routes make it easier to live without a car, and a system that people find pleasant, safe, and dignified will attract passengers. A bus stop at which women feel unsafe after dark will not serve half of the population.
 
The vision of the future city is not one in which cars are banned, but one in which they are not necessary. Achieving it will require patience, sustained funding, and the willingness to give priority to people over vehicles.
