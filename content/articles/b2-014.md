Imagine living in a village where, after the sun goes down, the only light comes from a smoky lamp burning kerosene. Children cannot read or do their homework, shops close early, and a medical clinic cannot keep vaccines cold. For hundreds of millions of people around the world, this is still normal. Connecting these communities to a power station through long cables is often too expensive. Increasingly, though, the solution is arriving from the sky.

Solar power has become cheap enough to change the picture. Small panels that turn sunlight into electricity can now be bought for a modest price, and in many remote areas, there is plenty of sun. A single panel on a roof can charge a battery during the day, and the battery can power a few lamps, a radio, and a phone charger at night. For a family used to spending money on kerosene every week, the system can pay for itself within a couple of years.

The effects can be dramatic. With electric light, children study in the evening, and teachers report better results in school. Mothers are safer when they cook, since kerosene lamps cause fires and produce unhealthy smoke. Small businesses, such as tailors and hairdressers, can stay open later. A clinic with a solar refrigerator can store medicine and treat more patients. Even a mobile phone makes a difference, because it gives farmers access to weather forecasts and market prices.

Several organisations have developed clever ways to make the technology affordable. In some projects, families pay a small amount by mobile phone each month, similar to a mobile phone contract, until they own the equipment. In others, a whole village shares a small power network, called a mini-grid, which provides electricity to homes and workshops.

Problems remain, though. Panels and batteries break, and a village needs someone who can repair them. If a part fails and nobody knows how to replace it, the system may sit unused for years. Batteries also contain materials that must be disposed of carefully, or they will damage the environment. Finally, a few solar panels are not enough to run heavy machinery or a factory, which means that economic growth may still depend on stronger sources of power.

Even so, many experts are optimistic. Compared with burning kerosene or running a noisy generator, solar energy is clean, quiet, and increasingly reliable. It also offers a rare chance for developing regions to skip the old model of large, centralised power stations, in the same way that many people moved directly from having no telephone to owning a mobile phone.

For millions of families, the sun has always been there. The change is that they can finally keep some of its light.
