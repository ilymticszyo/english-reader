In many countries, wind and solar power have become the cheapest ways to generate electricity. Their costs have fallen dramatically over the past decade, and installations are spreading rapidly. Yet there remains a central difficulty, one that is often overlooked in public discussion. The sun does not shine at night, and the wind does not blow on demand, whereas people expect electricity at all times. The question of how to store energy, or otherwise balance supply and demand, will largely determine how far renewable sources can replace fossil fuels.
 
The problem arises because electricity networks must match production and consumption at every moment. In the past, this was managed by adjusting the output of power stations that burned coal or gas, which could be turned up or down at will. With renewables, supply is variable. On a bright, windy afternoon, there may be more electricity than needed, forcing operators to waste it or pay customers to use it. On a still, dark winter evening, there may be a serious shortage. In some regions, these gaps can last for days, a phenomenon that grid planners describe with the German word "Dunkelflaute".
 
Several solutions are being developed. The most familiar is the battery. Large installations based on the same technology as that used in electric cars can store power for a few hours, releasing it when demand peaks. Their prices have fallen sharply, and they are becoming common in places with a great deal of solar power. But batteries are best suited to short-term balancing. Storing enough energy to supply a city for several days would require an enormous number of them, at a cost that is still prohibitive.
 
An older technology is pumped hydroelectric storage, in which water is pumped uphill when electricity is plentiful and released through turbines when it is needed. It accounts for most of the storage capacity worldwide, and it is reliable and long-lasting. However, it requires particular geography, and new projects face objections from those concerned about the environment.
 
Other proposals look further ahead. One is to use surplus electricity to produce hydrogen, which can be stored in tanks or underground caverns and burned or used in fuel cells later. The idea is attractive, particularly for industries such as steel and shipping that are difficult to electrify, but the process wastes a considerable proportion of the energy and the infrastructure is not yet in place. Others are exploring heat stored in hot rocks or molten salt, compressed air, and even heavy weights lifted and lowered by cranes.
 
Storage is not the only answer. Grids can be extended so that regions with wind can supply those without, since weather rarely affects everywhere simultaneously. Consumers can be encouraged to use electricity when it is plentiful, for instance by charging cars at midday or running washing machines at night, using price signals and smart devices. Some backup from nuclear or gas plants may remain necessary, at least for the foreseeable future.
 
Policy matters as much as technology. In many countries, the rules that govern electricity markets were written for a world of large power stations, and they do not reward the flexibility that storage provides. A battery that absorbs cheap power at noon and sells it at dusk performs a valuable service to the grid, yet in some places, it is charged twice, once as a consumer and once as a producer. Reforming such rules is unglamorous work, but it can unlock investment worth billions. There is also a question of fairness, since wealthy households with solar panels and batteries may leave the network and pass its fixed costs on to those who cannot afford to do the same.
 
No single technology will solve the problem. A realistic system is likely to combine many approaches, each suited to a different timescale, from seconds to seasons. The challenge is as much about planning, regulation, and investment as it is about engineering. Countries that think about it early will be better prepared for a future in which most of our power comes from the weather.
