Surveys conducted in many democracies over recent decades tell a similar story: the proportion of citizens who say that they trust their government, the press, or the large institutions of public life has fallen, in some places dramatically. Commentators speak of a crisis of confidence, and politicians who promise to challenge a corrupt establishment have found large audiences. Yet trust is a curious thing. It is invisible when present, and we notice it chiefly when it fails. Understanding what it is, and why it erodes, is a pressing task for any society that wishes to govern itself.
 
Trust, in its simplest sense, is a willingness to depend on another person or organisation without being able to check their actions. We trust the pilot to fly safely, the pharmacist to dispense the right medicine, and the bank to hold our savings. This is not irrational; no individual could verify every claim on which his or her life depends, and a complex society could not function if everybody tried. Economists have long observed that trust reduces the costs of doing business, since contracts need not be written to cover every eventuality, and political scientists have found that societies with high levels of trust tend to have more effective governments and less corruption.
 
It is important, however, to distinguish between trust and trustworthiness. Citizens who distrust an institution may be perfectly right to do so; the loss of confidence in some banks after the financial crisis of 2008, or in certain churches after the exposure of abuse, was a rational reaction to genuine failure. Philosophers have therefore suggested that the proper aim is not maximum trust, but trust that is well placed, in institutions that deserve it, and withheld from those that do not. A society in which people believed everything they were told would be as dysfunctional as one in which nobody did.
 
What makes an institution trustworthy? Research points to three qualities: competence, meaning the ability to do the job well; integrity, meaning honesty and adherence to principles; and benevolence, meaning real concern for those it serves. Each is easily damaged. A single scandal can destroy a reputation built over many years, because people tend to give more weight to evidence of bad behaviour than of good, which is perhaps a sensible instinct. Moreover, trust is difficult to rebuild, since it requires not only that the institution behaves well but that observers notice that it does.
 
The causes of the recent decline are debated. Some historians point to a series of shocks, including unpopular wars, financial collapse, and scandals in which officials were found to have deceived the public, each of which damaged confidence. Others emphasise structural change: growing inequality may foster a feeling that the system is rigged, and the fragmentation of the media has allowed groups to inhabit separate informational worlds, in which a source that one group considers authoritative is dismissed by another. The internet has made failures far more visible, as well as providing a stage for those who profit from outrage.
 
There is, however, a danger in a simple narrative of decline. Trust in specific institutions varies greatly, and in some countries, it has remained high or even risen. People often distrust politicians in general while continuing to respect their own representative, or the local doctor and the teachers at the school. Scholars also remind us that an age of unquestioning deference was not necessarily a golden one: it was frequently one in which the powerful could behave badly without challenge.
 
If trust cannot be demanded, how can it be earned? The evidence suggests that transparency, accountability, and consistency matter more than public relations. Institutions that publish their data, admit mistakes quickly, and accept independent scrutiny tend to be believed, while those that conceal and deny, in the end, are not. For citizens, the task is to cultivate a calibrated scepticism: neither believing everything nor dismissing everything, but asking who is speaking, what evidence they offer, and whether they have anything to gain.
