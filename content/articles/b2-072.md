In an age of information, it is fair to ask why anyone should read novels. Facts are available instantly, and time is limited. A story about people who never existed, in places that may not be real, can seem like an indulgence. Yet research and experience suggest that fiction does something valuable that other kinds of writing cannot do so well.

One of its main gifts is empathy. When we read a novel, we enter the thoughts and feelings of another person, often someone very different from ourselves. We feel their fear, understand their reasons, and share their disappointment. Several studies have found that people who read fiction regularly score higher on tests that measure the ability to understand other people's emotions. The effect may be small, and it is hard to be sure which comes first: those who are already sensitive may choose to read more stories. Nevertheless, the idea has a long history. Many writers have argued that a good book makes the reader a little more tolerant and a little more humane.

Fiction also provides a safe place to practise difficult experiences. A reader can explore love, loss, war, or injustice without facing the real risks. Novels may prepare us for events that we have not yet met, and they give words to feelings that we had not been able to express. Many people say that a book arrived at the right moment and made them feel less alone.

Stories also train the mind. To follow a long and complicated plot, readers must remember characters, notice details, and predict what will happen next. Studies suggest that reading for pleasure in childhood is linked with larger vocabulary, better results at school, and greater success later in life, even after taking account of family background. For those learning a second language, simple stories are an excellent way to meet words in context.

Moreover, fiction can tell truths that facts alone do not. A statistic may tell us that millions of people have been forced to leave their homes, but a story about one family on a journey can make us feel what that means. Writers have used fiction to expose injustice and to imagine alternative societies, and some novels have changed public opinion and even laws. By inventing, a writer can sometimes reveal more than by describing.

Critics, however, point out that fiction can mislead, and that people may form wrong ideas from stories that are not accurate. They are right that readers should be careful, especially with fiction presented as history. Others worry that reading is declining, as screens take more of our time. Surveys in several countries show that fewer adults read for pleasure than a few decades ago, though audiobooks and e-readers have helped some people to read more.

What matters is not the form but the experience: the quiet, concentrated attention that a story demands. In a world of constant interruption, that attention is rare and precious. Fiction invites us to slow down, to imagine, and to see through the eyes of another.

It may be, then, that fiction is not a luxury at all. It may be one of the ways in which we learn to be human.
