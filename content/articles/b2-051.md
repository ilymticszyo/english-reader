Twenty years ago, hardly anyone had heard the word "podcast". Today, hundreds of millions of people listen to them, and there are millions of different shows, covering everything from true crime and football to philosophy and gardening. How did a simple idea, recorded talk delivered over the internet, become a major part of modern culture?

The word was invented in the early 2000s, from "iPod", the popular music player of the time, and "broadcast". The technology was simple. Anyone with a microphone and a computer could record a programme and put it on the internet, and listeners could subscribe so that new episodes arrived automatically on their devices. For the first few years, podcasts were mostly a hobby for technology enthusiasts. Then, as smartphones became common, listening became easy, and the audience grew rapidly.

One reason for their popularity is convenience. Unlike a television programme, a podcast does not demand your eyes. People listen while they commute, cook, exercise, or clean the house, using time that would otherwise be empty. In this way, podcasts fit into the gaps of a busy life.

Another reason is the feeling of closeness. A voice in your ear, often speaking in a relaxed and personal manner, can feel like a conversation with a friend. Many listeners say that they feel they know the hosts, and some devoted fans spend hours each week with the same voices. This relationship creates strong loyalty, which is valuable for advertisers, since listeners often trust the products that their favourite host recommends.

Podcasts have also opened the door to subjects and voices that traditional media often ignore. A radio station needs a large audience to survive, so it must offer programmes that please many people. A podcast, in contrast, can succeed with a small but passionate group. As a result, there are shows for every interest, from the history of a single small town to the problems of a rare illness. For people who feel that no one else shares their interests, this can be a great comfort.

Education has benefited as well. Teachers record lessons, scientists explain their research in plain language, and language learners practise their listening skills by following programmes made for them. Some of the most popular series are long, carefully researched investigations that, in earlier times, might have become books or television documentaries.

However, there are also problems. Because anyone can make a podcast, quality varies enormously, and some shows spread false information with great confidence. The market has also become crowded, and most podcasts attract very few listeners and make little money. Many are abandoned after a few episodes, once the early enthusiasm fades. At the same time, a small number of big companies and celebrities now dominate the charts, which makes it harder for new voices to be heard.

Still, the format shows no sign of disappearing. It has changed the way that we use our free time, and it has proved that, in an age of screens, many people are happy to close their eyes and listen.
