Water covers most of our planet, so it may seem strange to speak of a shortage. But only about three percent of the world's water is fresh, and most of that is locked in ice or deep underground. The part that is easy to reach, in rivers, lakes, and shallow wells, is a very small share. As the population grows and the climate changes, more and more places are facing a serious problem.

According to the United Nations, around two billion people live in countries where the water supply is not sufficient. In some regions, the problem is physical. There is simply not enough rain, or the rivers are drying up. In others, there is plenty of water, but it is polluted, or the people lack the money and the equipment to bring it to their homes. Millions of women and girls in poor countries spend hours each day walking to fetch water, which keeps them from school and from work.

The biggest user of fresh water is not the household but farming. About seventy percent of the water that people take from rivers and underground sources is used to grow crops. Industry and homes use the rest. This means that any lasting solution has to include food. Growing rice or cotton in a dry region can use huge amounts of water, and wasteful methods, such as flooding entire fields, lose much of it before the plants can use it.

Climate change adds to the pressure. Rising temperatures make water evaporate faster, and rainfall has become less predictable. Some places suffer from longer droughts, while others see violent floods that wash away the soil instead of filling the wells. In mountain regions, glaciers that provide water for millions of people downstream are shrinking.

What can be done? Technology offers some answers. Drip irrigation delivers water directly to the roots of plants and can reduce the amount used on farms by half. Many cities lose a large share of their water through broken pipes, and repairing them is cheaper than finding new sources. In dry countries such as Israel, wastewater is cleaned and used again for farming, and desalination plants turn seawater into drinking water, though this requires a great deal of energy and is expensive.

Policy matters as well. When water is free or very cheap, people have little reason to save it. Fair pricing, together with protection for the poorest households, can encourage careful use. Countries that share rivers also need to cooperate, since a dam in one country can affect millions of people in another.

Finally, every individual can help, through small changes such as fixing leaking taps, choosing efficient machines, and eating less meat, which needs a lot of water to produce.

Water is the basis of life, health, and food. Whether we manage it wisely may be one of the most important questions of this century.
