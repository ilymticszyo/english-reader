Around seven thousand languages are spoken in the world today, but their distribution is startlingly uneven. A handful of them, such as Mandarin, Spanish, and English, are used by billions of people, while nearly half of the total are spoken by fewer than ten thousand speakers each. Linguists estimate that, on average, one language falls silent every few weeks, and some predict that by the end of this century, between a third and a half of the world's languages may have disappeared. This process, unfolding largely out of sight, represents one of the greatest losses of human knowledge in our time.

Languages die for a variety of reasons, and sudden catastrophe is the least common of them. Occasionally, a community is destroyed by disease or violence. Far more often, the cause is gradual and, in a sense, voluntary. Parents who live in a society where a dominant language brings opportunities for education and employment decide, understandably, to speak that language with their children. Within a generation or two, the ancestral tongue is spoken only by the elderly, and when the last of them dies, the language dies with them. In some cases, the process has been accelerated by deliberate policy: children were punished for speaking their mother tongue at school, and they grew up associating it with shame.

Why should it matter? One answer concerns knowledge. Each language encodes the experience of the community that created it, including detailed information about local plants, animals, weather, and medicine. Researchers working with indigenous speakers have documented vocabularies for ecosystems that outsiders had barely begun to study, and some of this knowledge has practical value, for instance in the identification of plants with medicinal properties. When a language disappears without being recorded, this accumulated understanding often vanishes too.

A second answer concerns diversity of thought. The strongest version of the claim, that language determines what we are able to think, has been largely rejected by modern scholars. A weaker version, however, enjoys considerable support: that the structures of a language influence what its speakers habitually notice. Some Australian languages, for example, do not use words like "left" and "right", but refer instead to compass directions, and speakers of these languages are remarkably good at keeping track of their orientation, even in unfamiliar places. Every language, in short, offers a slightly different way of dividing up the world, and a smaller number of languages means a narrower range of ideas.

Finally, there is the matter of identity. For many communities, a language is bound up with their songs, stories, and religion, and its loss can feel like the loss of a relative. Studies suggest that indigenous groups that maintain their ancestral languages tend to report better mental health and lower rates of suicide, though it is difficult to separate cause from effect.

The situation is not entirely hopeless. Welsh, which was in steep decline a century ago, is now taught in schools across Wales and supported by television and law, and the number of speakers has stabilised. Hebrew, which had been used mainly for prayer for nearly two thousand years, was revived as an everyday language in the twentieth century. In New Zealand, so-called "language nests", in which elders speak Maori to young children, have helped to restore the language. Digital technology offers new tools, including dictionaries, apps, and recording projects that make it easier to preserve what remains.

Such efforts are not sufficient on their own. A language survives only if people want to use it and have good reasons to do so, which depends on whether it has a place in schools, workplaces, and the media. The fate of the world's languages will thus be decided less by linguists than by the choices of ordinary families, and by the willingness of governments to support them.
