It seems natural to us that children should spend their early years at play and at school, shielded from adult responsibilities, and that the period of life before adulthood should be treated as a distinct and precious stage. This conviction is so deeply rooted that it is hard to imagine anything different. Yet a number of historians have argued that childhood, as we understand it, is a relatively recent invention, and that for most of European history, young people were regarded and treated very differently.

The most famous, and most controversial, version of this argument was advanced in the 1960s by a French historian, who claimed that in the Middle Ages, "childhood did not exist". By this he did not mean that there were no children, but that the idea of a separate phase of life, with its own needs and character, had not yet developed. He pointed to medieval paintings in which children appeared as small adults, with the same faces and proportions as their parents, and to the fact that, once a child could manage without constant care, usually around the age of seven, he or she entered the adult world of work.

Later scholars have criticised this claim, and with good reason. Evidence from letters, diaries, and graves indicates that medieval parents loved their children and mourned their deaths, and that toys, games, and special foods for the young were widespread. Even so, the broader point retains considerable force. The conditions of life were such that there was little room for protracted dependence. In a farming household, even small children were expected to gather wood, mind animals, and scare birds from the crops, and in towns, boys and girls of ten or twelve were commonly sent away as apprentices or servants.

The gradual transformation of attitudes had several causes. One was the changing religious climate of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, which led many reformers to see children as souls to be guided, and to emphasise the importance of instruction. Another was the rise of a prosperous middle class, whose families had the resources to keep their sons at school and to treat their daughters as ornaments of the household instead of labourers. Philosophers contributed as well. In the eighteenth century, Jean-Jacques Rousseau argued that children were not simply imperfect adults but beings with their own ways of seeing and feeling, and that education should respect their natural development.

Industrialisation complicated the picture. In the early factories and mines of Britain, children as young as five or six worked for long hours in miserable and often lethal conditions. Public outrage at these abuses, stirred by novelists, journalists, and reformers, led to a series of laws that limited child labour and, eventually, made schooling compulsory. By the early twentieth century, in much of Europe and North America, the idea that children belonged in the classroom and not the workplace had become widely accepted, and was reinforced by the new sciences of psychology and paediatrics.

The ideal of a protected childhood is, of course, a noble one, and its spread has contributed to a dramatic decline in child mortality and exploitation. Nevertheless, historians caution against assuming that our own arrangements are universal or inevitable. Even today, hundreds of millions of children around the world work, sometimes out of necessity, and the Western image of a carefree childhood may not correspond to their circumstances or their values. Within wealthy societies, too, there are signs that the boundary is changing: children are exposed to adult content through the internet at an early age, yet they remain dependent on their families far longer than previous generations.

The history of childhood thus carries a quiet warning. What we regard as natural is often the result of particular historical circumstances, and may change again.
