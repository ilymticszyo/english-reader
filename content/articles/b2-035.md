You have an important report to write, and the deadline is on Friday. It is Monday, and you know that you should start. Instead, you tidy your desk, answer old emails, and make another cup of tea. By Thursday night, you are in a panic. Sound familiar? Almost everybody puts things off at times, and psychologists have been trying to understand why.

The first thing to know is that procrastination is not about laziness or bad time management. Researchers now believe that it is mainly about emotions. When we face a task that makes us feel anxious, bored, or unsure of ourselves, we want to escape from that feeling. Doing something easier, or more enjoyable, gives us immediate relief. The brain rewards us for avoiding the unpleasant task, even though the problem remains and gets bigger.

There is also a question of time. People tend to give more importance to the present than to the future. The pleasure of watching a film tonight feels real and close, while the stress of a missed deadline seems distant. Psychologists call this present bias. Also, we often imagine our future selves as different people who will be more energetic and disciplined than we are now. "I will do it tomorrow" really means "someone else will do it".

Perfectionism is another cause. People who want to do everything perfectly may delay starting because they are afraid that their work will not be good enough. As long as they do not begin, they cannot fail. Unfortunately, the delay increases the pressure and reduces the quality of the result, which then proves their fear right.

The consequences can be serious. Studies of university students show that those who regularly delay get lower grades and report higher levels of stress and poorer sleep. In the workplace, it can damage careers, and where health is concerned, putting off a visit to the doctor can be dangerous.

Fortunately, there are practical ways to fight it. One is to break a large task into small parts, and to decide on the first very small step, such as writing one paragraph or opening the document. Starting is usually the hardest part, and once we have begun, it becomes easier to continue. Another is to remove distractions, for example by putting the phone in another room. It also helps to make a specific plan, such as "I will write for thirty minutes at nine o'clock on Tuesday", rather than a vague intention to work soon.

Finally, be kind to yourself. Studies suggest that people who forgive themselves for delaying are less likely to delay again than people who feel guilty. Guilt adds to the bad feelings that we try to escape, while understanding helps us to move forward.

Procrastination is a very human habit. The goal is not to become perfect, but to notice what we are avoiding, and to take one small step anyway.
