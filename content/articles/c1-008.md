When people imagine archaeology, they often picture gold masks, royal tombs, and spectacular temples. Yet many researchers find the past more fully revealed by far humbler objects: broken pots, discarded bones, fragments of cloth, and heaps of ordinary rubbish. These remains rarely appear in museum posters, but they allow us to reconstruct how common people lived, what they ate, and what they worried about, subjects that official histories frequently ignore.

The reason is simple. Written records from the ancient world were usually produced by, and for, the powerful. Kings recorded their victories, priests recorded their rituals, and tax officials counted the harvest. Ordinary families, who made up the vast majority of the population, appear only occasionally, and usually as numbers. Their everyday existence has to be inferred from the things they left behind, and rubbish heaps, known by archaeologists as middens, can be extraordinarily informative.

Consider food. By analysing animal bones and plant remains in ancient waste, researchers can establish not only what people ate but how their diets changed over time. In some northern European settlements, shells piled up over centuries reveal that coastal communities relied heavily on shellfish. Tiny grains of pollen, preserved in the soil, show which crops were grown and how forests were cleared. Even the chemical composition of a skeleton can indicate whether a person ate mainly meat or vegetables, offering clues about inequality within a single community.

Sometimes the evidence is astonishingly personal. At a Roman military fort in northern England, archaeologists discovered hundreds of thin wooden tablets covered in ink. They were not official decrees. They were letters, shopping lists, and requests for supplies. One was an invitation to a birthday celebration, written by the wife of an officer, and another was a complaint from a soldier who had not received the socks, sandals, and underwear he had been promised. Such documents transformed distant imperial troops into recognisable human beings with ordinary frustrations.

In the town of Pompeii, buried under volcanic ash in 79 AD, writing was found scratched on walls: advertisements, political slogans, jokes, and insults. These casual messages, which no one thought worth preserving, give a vivid impression of a noisy, argumentative, and humorous society. They also remind us that literacy was more widespread than many scholars once assumed.

Modern archaeologists have even turned their attention to recent history. Projects that excavate twentieth-century rubbish dumps have shown, for example, that people's accounts of their own habits are often inaccurate. When researchers compared what residents claimed to throw away with what was actually found in their bins, they discovered that people consistently underestimated the amount of waste and the quantity of alcohol and processed food they consumed. The refuse, unlike the interview, did not exaggerate.

The methods themselves have become increasingly sophisticated. Scientists can now recover traces of ancient proteins from pottery, revealing whether a vessel once held milk, wine, or fish. Analysis of the tiny deposits on teeth can show which plants a person chewed, and even whether they suffered from illness during childhood. Satellite images and ground-penetrating radar identify buried walls without a single spade entering the soil, which allows researchers to study a site while leaving most of it undisturbed for future generations with better tools. Each technical advance expands the range of what a scrap of ancient rubbish can tell us.

There are, of course, limits to what rubbish can reveal. Objects survive unevenly: stone, metal, and fired clay endure for thousands of years, while wood, textiles, and paper usually vanish unless the conditions are unusually dry, frozen, or waterlogged. This means that the record is biased towards the durable, and archaeologists must take care not to assume that what remains is what mattered most. A society that built its homes, tools, and clothing mainly from perishable materials may appear poor and simple in the ground, though its daily life was rich. Interpretation therefore requires imagination as well as evidence, and good researchers are careful to explain which of their conclusions are firm and which are only plausible.

This approach carries an important lesson. Understanding any society, past or present, requires attention to the unremarkable. Grand monuments tell us what people wished to be remembered for; rubbish tells us what they really did. And the same may be true of us. Future archaeologists, digging through landfill sites, may know more about our daily lives than any of our own newspapers or social media posts could ever tell them. What they find may be less flattering than we would like, but it will certainly be honest.
