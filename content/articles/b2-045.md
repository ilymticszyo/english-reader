Imagine that you are a farmer in a village ten thousand years ago. You have too much grain, but you need a new tool. You must find a toolmaker who happens to need grain at the same time, and who agrees that the exchange is fair. This system, called barter, works in small communities, but it is slow and difficult. The invention of money solved the problem, and it changed the world.

Money is, in the simplest terms, something that everybody accepts in exchange for goods and services. The earliest forms were objects that were scarce, durable, and easy to recognise. In different parts of the world, people used shells, salt, cattle, and pieces of metal. Metal was especially useful because it did not rot, could be divided, and held its value.

The first coins appeared in the kingdom of Lydia, in what is now Turkey, about 2,600 years ago. They were small pieces of a natural mixture of gold and silver, stamped with a symbol to guarantee their weight and quality. The idea spread quickly across the Mediterranean, because coins were convenient and the stamp of a ruler gave people confidence. Rulers soon discovered another advantage: they could pay for armies and large buildings with coins, and tax the people in the same coins.

Paper money was first used in China, around a thousand years ago. Merchants who were tired of carrying heavy strings of coins began to leave them with a trusted person and take a note instead. Later, governments began to print notes themselves. When Marco Polo travelled to China in the thirteenth century, he was amazed that people were happy to accept pieces of paper, and he wrote about it at length. In Europe, banks developed the same idea in a different way. Goldsmiths who stored gold for customers gave them receipts, which people began to use as money.

For a long time, paper money was linked to precious metal, because anyone who held a note could, in theory, exchange it for gold. This link gradually disappeared during the twentieth century, and today, most money is no longer backed by anything except trust in the government that issues it. Even the notes and coins that we hold make up only a small share of the total. Most money exists only as numbers in computers.

The newest chapter is digital. Cards and phone payments have reduced the use of cash, and in some countries, it is almost possible to live without it. Cryptocurrencies, such as Bitcoin, have attempted to create money that is not controlled by any government or bank, although their prices have been very unstable. Several central banks are considering digital versions of their national currencies.

Throughout this long story, one fact remains constant. Money works only as long as people believe in it. It is not so much a thing as a shared agreement, and that may be its most remarkable feature.
