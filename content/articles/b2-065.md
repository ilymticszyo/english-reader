Imagine walking into a restaurant painted entirely in bright red, or a hospital waiting room painted black. Most of us would feel uncomfortable straight away. Colours seem to affect our moods, our choices, and even our behaviour, and companies, designers, and doctors pay a great deal of attention to them. But how much of this is science, and how much is only popular belief?

Some responses to colour appear to be built into our biology. Warm colours, such as red and orange, are associated with energy and excitement, while cool ones, such as blue and green, are connected with calm. Researchers have found evidence that these associations are common in many societies, perhaps because of what we see in nature: blue skies and green plants usually suggest safety and comfort, while red may recall fire, blood, or danger. In some animals, red is used as a warning, and in humans, the colour of a face can show anger, shame, or health.

The effects can be measured in experiments. In one well-known study, participants who saw the colour red before a test performed worse than those who saw green or grey, apparently because red signalled danger and made them anxious. In sport, researchers once reported that athletes in red were slightly more likely to win in certain competitions, though later studies have found the effect to be small and not always repeatable. Some studies suggest that people working in blue rooms are more creative, while red rooms help them to concentrate on detailed tasks. But these findings are far from universal, and results often differ between experiments.

Culture matters a great deal. In most Western countries, white is the colour of weddings and purity, while black is worn at funerals. In parts of East Asia, white is associated with mourning, and red is the colour of luck and celebration. Purple was once reserved for royalty in Europe because the dye was extraordinarily expensive. Even within a society, associations change over time. A century ago, pink was often considered a boy's colour and blue a girl's, a reversal of today's custom.

Businesses use colour deliberately. Fast-food chains often use red and yellow, which are said to stimulate appetite and suggest speed. Banks and technology companies prefer blue, which is thought to communicate trust and stability. Many shops use colours in the layout to guide customers' attention. Whether these choices truly change what people buy is difficult to prove, but companies appear to believe that they do, and they invest heavily in them.

In health and education, colour has also been explored. Some hospitals use soft greens and blues to help patients relax, and many schools choose light colours for classrooms. Yet the evidence that wall colour improves learning or recovery is mixed. What seems more important than the colour itself is whether a space is well lit, quiet, and comfortable.

In short, colour influences us, but not in a simple or predictable way. It is best understood as one among many factors that shape how we feel.
