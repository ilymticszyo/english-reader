The idea of giving every citizen a regular sum of money, with no conditions attached, has a long and distinguished history. Thinkers from different parts of the political spectrum have supported it, though for very different reasons. Some on the left see it as a way to eliminate poverty and to give workers more bargaining power. Some on the right regard it as a simpler and less intrusive alternative to the complicated system of benefits found in many countries. For decades, however, universal basic income was mostly discussed in theory. In recent years, a growing number of experiments have begun to supply actual evidence.

One of the best-known took place in Finland between 2017 and 2018. The government selected two thousand unemployed people at random and paid them a monthly sum, regardless of whether they found work. A comparison group continued to receive the standard unemployment benefits, which require recipients to look for jobs. The main question was whether guaranteed income would discourage people from working. The results were modest but interesting. Those who received the payment were employed for slightly more days than the comparison group, though the difference was small. Far more striking were the effects on well-being: recipients reported less stress, better health, and greater trust in other people and institutions.

A different kind of experiment was carried out in Stockton, California, where a group of residents received five hundred dollars a month for two years. Researchers found that the proportion of recipients who held full-time jobs increased, and the amount spent on basic needs, such as food, utility bills, and car repairs, rose accordingly. The stereotype that people would waste unconditional money on harmful products was not supported. Meanwhile, in several villages in Kenya, charity-run programmes have given large payments to entire communities for a decade or more. The studies there suggest that many recipients invest in small businesses and livestock, and that local economies benefit because spending circulates among neighbours.

These results should not be exaggerated. Most trials are small, last only a few years, and involve selected groups, so they cannot tell us what would happen if an entire country adopted such a policy permanently. A temporary payment may lead people to behave differently from a guaranteed lifetime income, and a pilot in one town cannot capture the effects on prices, taxes, and wages across a national economy. Critics also point out that paying everyone, including the rich, would be extremely expensive, and the money would have to come from higher taxes or from cuts to existing services, such as healthcare and education, which many people depend on.

Moreover, the idea touches on deeper questions about values. If work provides not only income but also purpose, routine, and social connection, can a payment alone replace what a job gives? Proponents reply that the freedom to refuse bad jobs, to care for relatives, to retrain, or to start a business is itself a form of dignity, and that much valuable work, such as looking after children, is currently unpaid. Opponents worry about fairness to those who contribute through employment and about the possibility that the amount would never be enough to live on, leaving people dependent on a political promise.

Practical administration is another problem that experiments tend to hide. A pilot can send money to a few hundred households, but a nationwide scheme would need to replace or reorganise existing benefits, decide whether to pay children and migrants, and determine how payments would interact with the tax system. These details matter a great deal. A scheme designed to simplify welfare could, if poorly constructed, leave disabled people or large families worse off than before. Designers therefore need to think not only about the principle but also about the transition, which is likely to be the most politically difficult stage.

The experiments cannot settle this argument, but they do correct some assumptions. The fear that people will simply stop working appears to be exaggerated, at least in the short term, while the hope that a small payment will transform society overnight appears equally optimistic. What the evidence suggests is more modest and perhaps more useful: security changes how people feel and plan. Whether that benefit is worth the cost remains a political decision, and the next generation of studies will need to be larger, longer, and more varied to inform it.
