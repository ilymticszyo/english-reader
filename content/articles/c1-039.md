In the 1840s, a disease swept through the potato fields of Ireland. Within a few years, a million people had died of hunger, and another million had left the country. The cause was a microscopic organism that destroyed the potato plants, but the scale of the disaster owed much to a human decision. The poorest part of the population relied on a single variety of potato, and because the plants were all closely related, none of them had any resistance to the infection. The Irish famine is often cited as a classic example of the danger of monoculture: the cultivation of a single crop, or a single variety, over a vast area.

Today, the world's food supply is, in many respects, even less diverse. Of the thousands of plant species that humans have eaten over the centuries, just three, rice, wheat, and maize, provide about half of the calories consumed worldwide. Within each crop, the number of varieties in commercial use has dropped sharply. In the course of the twentieth century, according to estimates by international agencies, some three quarters of the genetic diversity of cultivated crops was lost, as farmers abandoned traditional local varieties in favour of a few high-yielding ones. The same pattern can be observed in livestock: most of the world's milk is produced by a single breed of cow, and commercial chicken production is dominated by a handful of closely related lines.

The advantages of this concentration are plain. Uniform crops can be planted, treated, and harvested by machine, and the new varieties of the "green revolution" in the second half of the twentieth century produced extraordinary increases in yields, saving millions of people from famine. Specialisation also lowers costs, enabling food to be sold cheaply to the world's growing urban population.

The drawback is vulnerability. A genetically uniform crop gives a pest or a pathogen a vast and homogeneous supply of food, and a single mutation that overcomes the plant's defences can spread with remarkable speed. In 1970, a fungus destroyed about fifteen percent of the maize harvest in the United States, because nearly all of the plants shared a particular genetic trait. The banana provides a more recent warning. The variety exported to most of the world is a single clone, and a soil fungus that attacks it has been spreading through plantations on several continents, with no easy cure. The previous standard variety was wiped out by an earlier version of the same disease.

Monoculture also increases dependence on chemical inputs. A field of one crop needs fertilisers to replace the nutrients that it removes and pesticides to control the pests that multiply in its absence of competitors, and these chemicals impose costs on soil, water, and wildlife. Meanwhile, climate change intensifies the risk, since the conditions to which a given variety is adapted may no longer apply in thirty years' time. Genetic diversity is, in effect, an insurance policy, which preserves the traits, such as drought tolerance or resistance to a new disease, that future farmers may urgently need.

Fortunately, many people are working to protect this inheritance. Seed banks around the world store hundreds of thousands of varieties, including a vast underground vault in the Arctic that serves as a backup for the others. Scientists regularly return to these collections in search of useful genes. On farms, growing a mixture of crops, rotating them from year to year, and planting hedgerows and trees can build resilience, although such methods often require more labour and knowledge. Consumers can also play a part by choosing local and unusual varieties, which gives farmers a reason to keep growing them.

A resilient food system, in short, resembles a healthy ecosystem: varied, flexible, and capable of absorbing shocks. Efficiency is important, but a system that has no margin for error is only efficient until something goes wrong.
