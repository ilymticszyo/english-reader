Look around the room where you are sitting. How many things are made of plastic? Probably dozens: the casing of your phone, the keys on your keyboard, the bottle on your desk, the pen in your hand, perhaps even the fibres in your clothes. Since the middle of the last century, plastic has become so common that it is almost impossible to avoid. But growing concern about pollution has led some people to try to live without it. Is a plastic-free life really possible?

The case for reducing plastic is strong. Much of it is used only once, for a few minutes, and then thrown away, yet it lasts for hundreds of years. A large part ends up in landfills, in rivers, and in the sea, where it harms wildlife and breaks into tiny pieces that enter the food chain. Scientists have found these particles in places as distant as mountain tops and the deepest parts of the ocean, and even in human bodies. Making plastic also uses oil and produces greenhouse gases.

Some people have taken up the challenge. They carry their own bags, bottles, and containers. They buy loose vegetables rather than packaged ones, use soap bars rather than liquid soap in bottles, and choose products made of glass, metal, or paper. A few shops now sell goods by weight, so customers can fill their own jars. Those who have tried this often say that it has changed their attitude to shopping. They buy less, think more about what they need, and waste less food.

But it is not easy. Plastic is cheap, light, and strong, and in many cases, it is the best material for the job. In hospitals, it keeps equipment clean and safe. In food packaging, it helps to keep products fresh, and, therefore, can reduce food waste, which also harms the environment. Light plastic parts make cars and aeroplanes more efficient. Replacing it with other materials can sometimes create new problems. A glass bottle, for example, is heavier, so it uses more fuel to transport.

Practical difficulties also exist. In many places, shops that sell unpackaged goods are rare or expensive, and a plastic-free life requires time and money that not everyone has. A person working two jobs, or looking after children alone, may reasonably decide that other priorities matter more. This raises the question of fairness: should the solution depend on personal sacrifice, or should the responsibility lie elsewhere?

Many experts argue that individual action, while useful, is not enough. The most important changes must come from governments and companies. Rules can ban the most wasteful products, such as thin shopping bags, and encourage producers to design packaging that can be reused or recycled easily. Charges for plastic bags have already reduced their use dramatically in several countries.

So what is a realistic approach? Perhaps not perfection, but progress. Refusing the things that you do not need, such as straws and extra packaging, choosing reusable items, and supporting rules that cut waste are all steps in the right direction. A world with less plastic is possible, even if a world with none is not.
