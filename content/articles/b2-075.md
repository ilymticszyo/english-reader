Seen from the air, a coral reef looks like a pale blue and green patch in the dark ocean. Beneath the surface, it is one of the most colourful and crowded places on Earth. Coral reefs cover less than one percent of the sea floor, yet they are home to about a quarter of all marine species. They also protect coasts, support fishing, and provide jobs for hundreds of millions of people. But they are in serious danger.

A coral reef is built by tiny animals that live in very large groups. Each one produces a hard skeleton, and over thousands of years, these skeletons pile up to form the vast structures that we call reefs. The Great Barrier Reef, off the coast of Australia, is so large that it can be seen from space. Corals live in partnership with tiny living things inside their bodies. These partners use sunlight to make food, and in return, the coral gives them shelter. They also give corals their bright colours.

This partnership is delicate. Corals can survive only in a narrow range of temperatures, and when the water becomes too warm, the partners leave the coral. Without them, the coral turns white. A white coral is not dead, and if the water cools quickly, it can recover. But if the heat continues for weeks, the coral starves. Since the 1980s, mass events of this kind have become more frequent, and several of them have affected large areas of the Great Barrier Reef. Rising sea temperatures, caused by climate change, are the most important cause.

Other pressures add to the problem. Pollution from farms and cities carries chemicals and soil into the sea, which can harm the corals and encourage the growth of seaweed. Too much fishing removes the fish that keep the reef healthy, such as those that eat the seaweed that would otherwise cover it. Destructive methods, such as fishing with explosives, break the reef physically. The oceans also absorb carbon dioxide from the air, which changes the water and makes it harder for corals to build their skeletons.

The loss of reefs would have effects far beyond the sea. Reefs act as natural barriers, reducing the force of waves that hit the shore, and so protect coastal villages from storms. They provide food for communities, and diving and fishing attract visitors. Some chemicals from reef creatures are being used in the search for new medicines.

What can be done? Reducing greenhouse gases is the most important step, since it addresses the main cause. In local areas, protecting reefs from pollution and overfishing gives them a better chance to survive heat. Marine reserves, in which fishing is limited, have helped reefs to recover in several places. Scientists are also experimenting with growing corals that can survive heat, breeding them in laboratories, and planting them on damaged reefs. These projects are promising but cannot cover more than a small area.

Some reefs have shown unexpected strength and have recovered from damage. This offers hope, but it depends on the speed of climate change. The future of the reef will be decided on land, by the choices that people make about energy, farming, and the way we use the sea.
