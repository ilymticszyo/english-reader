Most people have, at some point, felt a pang of loss at the demolition of a building. It may not have been a famous or beautiful one: a local cinema, a school, or a block of flats where they grew up. Yet its disappearance seemed to take with it something that could not be replaced. This reaction points to something that architects and historians have long understood: buildings are not only shelters. They are also stores of memory, and they play an essential role in how communities understand who they are.

Cities are, in effect, enormous records of the past. Walking through an old town, we read its history in the width of its streets, the style of its houses, and the names carved above its doors. Buildings link the present to earlier generations in a way that books cannot, because they can be entered and touched. A cathedral that took two centuries to build reminds us of the faith and the effort of people we will never meet. A factory that has been converted into apartments preserves the memory of an industrial age, even as its function has changed.

This explains why conflicts so often involve buildings. In wars, the deliberate destruction of libraries, temples, and bridges is not an accident but a strategy, designed to erase the identity of a people. After the destruction, reconstruction becomes an act of recovery. In the 1950s, the people of Warsaw rebuilt their old town, which had been almost entirely destroyed, using paintings and photographs as guides, and it is now a World Heritage site. Critics said that it was a copy, but for the citizens, it was a way of reclaiming their city.

Preservation, however, is not simple. Cities must also change to meet the needs of the living. Old buildings may be inefficient, unsafe, or poorly suited to modern uses, and the land they occupy may be badly needed for housing. There is a risk that cities will become museums, preserved at the expense of the people who live in them. Conversely, unrestrained development has often destroyed historic districts, replacing them with buildings that could be anywhere in the world.

The question of what is worth keeping is also less obvious than it seems. Which buildings should be protected: those that are old, those that are beautiful, or those that are associated with important events? Over time, opinion changes. Many Victorian buildings that were demolished in the 1960s as ugly are now regarded as treasures, while concrete buildings from the same decade, once admired as modern, are widely disliked and threatened. Some structures also carry painful memories. Prisons, monuments to dictators, and former places of slavery pose a difficult question: should such sites be removed, or preserved as a warning?

Many architects and planners now favour adaptive reuse, in which an old building is given a new purpose while keeping its character. Railway stations have become museums, warehouses have become offices and schools, and power stations have become galleries. This approach saves materials and energy, since the greenest building is often the one that already exists, and it allows the past and present to coexist. It also acknowledges that a building's value lies not only in its appearance, but also in the stories attached to it.

Finally, memory is not only a matter of grand monuments. Ordinary places, such as the corner shop, the market, or the street where a community gathers, are often the ones most loved by residents, and most easily lost. Increasingly, local people are asked to help identify the places that matter to them. That is a sensible direction. For a building to matter, someone has to remember it.
