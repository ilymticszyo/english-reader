Every day, millions of people cross bridges without a thought. We trust that these great structures will hold. And most of the time they do. But a small number of bridges have collapsed, and each failure has taught engineers an important lesson.

In 1940, the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in the United States began to move wildly in a moderate wind. The road twisted like a ribbon, and after several hours, it broke apart and fell into the water. No one died, but the film of the collapse is still shown to students. Engineers had designed the bridge to withstand strong winds pushing against it. They had not thought enough about how a steady wind could cause a slow, growing movement. After this event, designers began to test models of new bridges in wind tunnels before building the real ones.

Other failures have come from small mistakes. In 1967, a bridge over the Ohio River collapsed during the rush hour, killing forty-six people. Investigators found that one small crack in a single metal part had grown over many years until it gave way. Because the bridge had been built in a way that depended heavily on that part, there was no backup. The disaster led to new rules requiring regular inspection of bridges across the country.

Sometimes the cause is the weather or the ground. Floods can wash away the soil around the supports of a bridge, leaving it unsteady. Earthquakes can shake the structure in ways that its designers did not expect. In each case, careful study afterward produced better methods, such as deeper foundations and flexible joints.

Age is another problem. Many bridges were built decades ago, for much lighter traffic than they carry today. Salt used on winter roads and moisture in the air slowly damage the metal inside concrete. Without good maintenance, even a strong bridge becomes weak. Unfortunately, repairing old bridges is not exciting, and politicians often prefer to spend money on new projects that people can see.

What do these stories show? First, engineering is a process of learning. Each failure reveals something that earlier calculations missed. Second, the most dangerous failures are often the result of a chain of small problems, not one big mistake. And third, safety is not a one-time achievement. It requires constant checking, honest reporting, and enough money.

Modern bridges are safer than ever, thanks to computers, better materials, and sensors that warn of damage. Yet the lesson remains the same. A bridge is not finished when it opens. It needs care for the whole of its life.
