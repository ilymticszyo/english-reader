When something breaks, what do you do? Not long ago, many people would have tried to fix it themselves or taken it to a local shop. Today, a cracked phone screen or a failing washing machine often leads to a different decision: throw it away and buy a new one. For manufacturers, this is a source of profit. For the environment, it is a growing problem. In response, a movement has appeared with a simple demand: people should have the right to repair the things they own.

The problem has several causes. Many modern devices are built in ways that make repair difficult. Batteries may be glued inside, screws may be of unusual shapes that ordinary tools cannot turn, and parts may be joined together so that one broken piece means replacing a whole unit. In addition, many companies refuse to sell spare parts or repair manuals to anyone except their own authorised workshops. Some products even use software to block the use of parts that were not approved by the maker. The result is that repair is expensive, and often costs nearly as much as a new product.

The consequences are considerable. Each year, the world produces tens of millions of tonnes of electronic waste, much of which contains valuable metals as well as dangerous chemicals. Only a small share is recycled properly. Most of the environmental damage of a smartphone occurs when it is made, so using it for longer is one of the best ways to reduce its effect. Consumers also lose money, since a repairable product could last several years more.

Manufacturers respond that there are good reasons for their practices. Thin and light devices need compact designs, and some features, such as water resistance, require parts to be tightly sealed. They also argue that inexperienced repairs may be dangerous, since batteries can catch fire, and that unauthorised repairers may damage the product or harm the company's reputation. Some say that protecting their designs and software is necessary to keep their business secure.

Supporters of repair reply that these concerns can be managed without making repair impossible. They point to examples in which companies have designed products that are both slim and easy to open, and note that independent workshops have repaired devices safely for decades. They also remind us that, in many cases, the real reason is commercial: a product that cannot be repaired must be replaced.

Governments have begun to act. In recent years, several American states and the European Union have passed laws or introduced rules that require manufacturers to provide parts, tools, and information for a number of products for a certain period. Some countries have also introduced repair scores, which tell shoppers how easy a product is to repair, and a few offer tax reductions for repairs.

At a community level, "repair cafes" have appeared in many cities. Volunteers with practical skills help neighbours to mend toasters, bicycles, and clothes, and teach others how to do it. They save money and waste and also build friendships.

Of course, not everything can or should be repaired. But the principle is difficult to argue against. When we own something, we should be allowed to keep it working.
