Since the Industrial Revolution, economic growth has been the great aim of modern societies. Governments are judged by their ability to deliver it, businesses plan around it, and citizens expect each generation to be richer than the last. The results have been extraordinary. In the past two hundred years, average incomes in many countries have risen many times over, life expectancy has doubled, and extreme poverty, once the normal condition of humanity, has fallen to a small share of the world's people. Yet there is a growing recognition that the pursuit of ever-greater output may be running into limits that cannot be ignored.
 
The most obvious limit is environmental. Economic activity depends on materials and energy taken from the planet, and it releases waste back into it. Throughout history, growth has been closely linked to the burning of fossil fuels, which has pushed the concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere to its highest level in millions of years. Other pressures are also intensifying: forests are cleared, fish stocks collapse, and species disappear at a rate far above the natural one. Scientists have suggested that humanity is already exceeding several "planetary boundaries", beyond which the risk of sudden and damaging change increases.
 
Optimists reply that growth need not mean more damage. They point to "decoupling", the process in which economic output rises while resource use or emissions fall. There is evidence that this has happened: several wealthy countries have cut their territorial emissions while their economies expanded. New technologies, from renewable energy to more efficient machines, offer the hope that we can produce more with less. In this view, the answer to environmental problems is not less growth but better growth, driven by innovation and clean investment.
 
Sceptics find this too comfortable. They note that much of the apparent decoupling is the result of moving polluting industries abroad, so that the emissions are counted in other countries, and that the reductions achieved so far are far smaller than those needed. They also observe that efficiency gains have a way of being cancelled out: when a technology becomes cheaper to use, people tend to use it more, a phenomenon known as the rebound effect. A more efficient engine may encourage more driving. If total demand keeps rising, even the best technology may not be enough.
 
This has led to a more radical proposal, known as "degrowth". Its supporters argue that wealthy countries should deliberately reduce their consumption of materials and energy, while improving the quality of life through shorter working hours, better public services, and fairer distribution. They claim that, beyond a certain income, additional wealth does little for wellbeing, so that societies could become healthier and happier with a smaller economy. Critics reply that such a policy would be politically impossible and economically painful, particularly because modern systems for pensions, healthcare, and employment depend on growth. They also say that poorer countries cannot be asked to give up the prospect of rising living standards.
 
Between these positions lies a range of ideas that try to redefine progress. Some economists have called for new measures that count the depletion of natural resources and the benefits of unpaid work and leisure, instead of focusing solely on the total value of goods and services. Others suggest a "doughnut" model, in which the aim is to meet everyone's basic needs without exceeding the planet's ecological limits. Neither the concept nor the measures are fully developed, but they signal a shift in thinking.
 
What can safely be said is that the old assumption, that growth and wellbeing always move together, is no longer obviously true, and that growth based on increasing use of fossil fuels cannot continue indefinitely. The more difficult question is how to adjust. A transition that is too slow risks environmental disaster, while one that is too abrupt could cause severe hardship, particularly for those who are already vulnerable.
 
Perhaps the sensible course is to ask not whether the economy should grow, but what it should grow for. A society that clearly defines its goals, such as health, security, and a stable climate, is better placed to decide which kinds of growth to encourage and which to leave behind.
