Reading seems natural to anyone who can do it. But unlike speaking, which children pick up simply by listening, reading is a recent invention, and it has to be taught. Scientists who study the brain have shown that learning to read requires the brain to build entirely new connections, and their discoveries have changed the way many schools teach.
 
Humans have been speaking for tens of thousands of years, so our brains have had time to become well suited to language. Writing, however, appeared only about five thousand years ago, which is far too recent for evolution to have produced special equipment. Instead, the brain adapts parts that evolved for other purposes, such as recognising faces and objects, and trains them to recognise letters. This takes time and practice.
 
The first step is to understand that spoken words are made of separate sounds. A child who can hear that "cat" begins with the sound "k" and ends with "t" is ready to link those sounds with letters. This is known as phonics, and it is the core of many teaching methods. Studies in several countries have shown that children who are taught the relation between letters and sounds directly are more likely to become good readers than those who are expected to guess.
 
There has, however, been a long argument about methods. Some teachers prefer to introduce whole words and stories from the start, in the belief that children will learn better if they are interested in the meaning. Others emphasise sounds and rules. In the end, most researchers agree that a combination works best: explicit teaching of letters and sounds, together with plenty of enjoyable books. Children need both the skill and the motivation.
 
English is especially difficult. Its spelling is irregular, with many words that do not follow the usual rules, such as "though", "through", and "cough". Studies show that children learning languages with more regular spelling, such as Finnish or Italian, usually learn to read more quickly. English-speaking children may need two or three years to reach the same level.
 
Some children struggle more than others. About one person in ten has dyslexia, a condition that makes it hard to link letters with sounds. It has nothing to do with intelligence, and with early, patient help, most dyslexic children can learn to read well. Teachers and parents should look for warning signs, such as difficulty with rhyming, and ask for support early.
 
Parents can play an important part. Talking with children, singing songs, and sharing picture books all build the foundations of reading before school begins. Children who hear many words at home usually enter school with a larger vocabulary.
 
Once reading becomes automatic, it feels effortless. But behind that ease lies years of training, and a remarkable change in the brain. Understanding this should make us more patient with those who are still learning.
