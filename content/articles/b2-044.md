For many of us, the word "museum" suggests a quiet building filled with glass cases and signs that say "Do not touch". Visitors walk slowly from room to room, read short labels, and leave an hour later, a little tired. This picture is becoming out of date. Around the world, museums are changing how they work and whom they serve.

One of the most visible changes is the move from looking to doing. Many science museums now let visitors push buttons, build machines, and carry out experiments. Art museums run workshops where people can paint or sculpt, and history museums sometimes ask children to dress in the clothes of the past. This approach is based on research which shows that people remember more when they take part than when they only watch.

Technology has also opened new possibilities. Visitors can use their phones to find extra information about an object, or to see how a broken building looked in the past. Some museums have placed their collections online, so that anyone in the world can examine a painting in great detail without paying for a ticket. During the pandemic, when buildings closed, virtual tours reached millions of people. Yet most curators insist that the internet does not replace the real object. Standing in front of an ancient statue, they argue, gives a feeling that no screen can match.

A second important change concerns the stories that museums tell. In the past, many museums presented history from the point of view of the powerful, and they displayed objects that had been taken from other countries without asking permission. Today, there is a growing debate about this. Some museums have returned objects to the places they came from, while others have invited local communities to help to explain their own culture. Curators are also paying more attention to the experience of women, workers, and minorities, who were once left out of the story.

Museums are also trying to attract people who rarely visit. They offer free entry on certain days, stay open late for evening events, and develop programmes for people with disabilities. Some have special "quiet hours" for visitors who find noise and crowds difficult. In a number of cities, museums have become places to meet friends, listen to music, or enjoy a meal, rather than simply to learn.

Not everyone approves of these changes. Critics worry that too much entertainment will make museums shallow, and that the need to earn money from tickets and shops may influence what they show. They also point out that old buildings and huge collections are expensive to maintain, and that funding is often under pressure.

Still, the best museums manage to combine fun with serious learning. They remind us that the past is not a closed book, and that every visitor, young or old, has something to ask and something to add.
