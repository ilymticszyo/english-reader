In a world that celebrates success, failure is rarely welcome. Schools reward correct answers, employers prize a record of achievement, and social media is filled with images of triumph. Yet many of the most accomplished people in history attribute their success to the lessons of earlier failures. The inventor Thomas Edison, according to a famous remark, said that he had not failed but had found ten thousand ways that did not work. Whether or not the story is accurate, it captures an idea that researchers in psychology and management have begun to study seriously: that failure, handled well, is among the most powerful tools for learning.
 
The scientific method itself is built on the value of being wrong. A hypothesis is tested, and often it fails, but the failure tells the scientist something. A negative result narrows the field of possibilities and may point toward a better explanation. Many of the great discoveries, from penicillin to the microwave oven, began with an unexpected outcome that someone was prepared to take seriously. As the philosopher Karl Popper argued, progress in science depends not on proving theories right but on testing them in ways that could show them to be wrong.
 
In psychology, the work of Carol Dweck has been influential. She distinguishes between a "fixed" mindset, in which people believe that ability is something that cannot be changed, and a "growth" mindset, in which they believe that it can be developed through effort and strategy. Those with a fixed mindset tend to avoid challenges, because failure threatens their self-image. Those with a growth mindset see setbacks as information, and are more likely to persist. Studies in schools suggest that students who are taught to think this way sometimes improve their results, although the size of the effect is debated, and the idea has been criticised for being applied too simply.
 
Organisations have also learned from failure, though not always willingly. Aviation is often cited as a model. After every accident, investigators produce detailed reports that are shared across the industry, and pilots are encouraged to report errors and near misses without fear of punishment. As a result, flying has become extraordinarily safe. In contrast, in fields where mistakes are hidden, such as some areas of medicine, the same errors may be repeated. Companies such as those in the technology sector have adopted the slogan "fail fast", encouraging teams to try new ideas quickly and to abandon them early if they do not work.
 
It would be a mistake, though, to romanticise failure. Some failures are costly and painful, and their burden is not shared equally. A wealthy entrepreneur whose business collapses can usually try again, while a poorer one may be ruined. Failure teaches only those who are in a position to reflect and to recover. Moreover, not every failure is instructive. Carelessness, repeated without thought, is simply carelessness. What matters is the willingness to examine what went wrong and to change.
 
How, then, can we make better use of our mistakes? Teachers can praise effort and strategy, not only results, and can design tasks that allow students to try again. Managers can create an environment in which people feel safe to admit errors. Individuals can keep a record of what they have learned from setbacks, and talk about them honestly with others.
 
Cultural differences matter here. In some societies, a failed business is regarded as a normal stage in an entrepreneur's career, and investors even look for those who have learned from a collapse. In others, bankruptcy carries a lasting shame, which discourages people from taking risks at all. Countries that wish to encourage innovation may therefore need to look at their laws and customs, to make second chances easier. A system that punishes every mistake severely will discover that people stop trying anything new.
 
Perhaps the greatest benefit is a change in attitude. A person who is not afraid to fail is free to take risks, to try new things, and to be creative. Seen in this light, failure is not the opposite of success but a part of the road toward it.
