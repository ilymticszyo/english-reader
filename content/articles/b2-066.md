When we think of great scientists, most of us picture men: Newton, Darwin, Einstein. Yet throughout history, women have made important discoveries, often under difficult conditions and, too often, without receiving credit. Their stories show how talent can be lost when society refuses to recognise it.

For centuries, women were simply excluded. Universities did not accept female students, scientific societies refused them membership, and many families expected daughters to marry rather than study. Those who did work in science usually did so as assistants or in private. In the eighteenth century, a French noblewoman translated Newton's great work into French, adding her own corrections and ideas, and her translation is still used today. But she was known mainly as a translator, rather than as a scientist in her own right.

Even when women made major discoveries, their contribution was sometimes hidden. In the 1840s, a young English mathematician wrote what is now considered the first computer program, for a machine that had been designed but never built. In the 1950s, a British chemist, Rosalind Franklin, took the X-ray photographs that revealed the spiral structure of DNA. Her pictures were shown to other researchers without her knowledge, and when the discovery was announced and later honoured with a Nobel Prize, she had died, and her name was hardly mentioned.

Marie Curie is the most famous exception. She was the first woman to win a Nobel Prize, and so far the only person to win in two different sciences, physics and chemistry. Her work on radioactivity changed medicine and physics. Yet she faced constant obstacles, including attempts to keep her out of the French Academy of Sciences, and she worked in a poor laboratory that was little more than a shed.

Other women were important in less visible ways. At an observatory in the United States around 1900, a group of women, who were paid much less than men, studied thousands of photographs of the stars, classified them, and made discoveries that shaped modern astronomy. 

Historians have a name for this pattern: the "Matilda effect", after a nineteenth-century American campaigner who described the way that women's scientific work is often credited to men. It shows how recognition depends on more than talent. It also depends on who has power, who writes the history, and whose voice is heard.

Today, the situation has improved, but problems remain. Women now make up about half of university students in many countries, and more than half in some fields. But they are still a minority in physics, engineering, and computing, and they are less often named as lead authors or given senior posts. Researchers have shown that, in some studies, the same application is judged less well when it carries a woman's name. Efforts to change this, from mentoring programmes to more flexible working conditions, are beginning to have an effect.

Remembering the women of the past is not only about justice. It is also a reminder that scientific progress has always depended on many hands, and that the next great discovery may come from someone who is still waiting for a chance.
