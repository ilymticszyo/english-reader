It may surprise you to learn that the first electric cars appeared more than a hundred and thirty years ago. In the early 1900s, they were quiet, clean, and easy to drive, and they were popular, especially with city drivers. Then cheap petrol and the invention of the electric starter for petrol engines made them fall out of fashion. It took almost a century for them to return.

Today, electric vehicles are growing fast. In some countries, more than half of the new cars sold are electric. Governments have encouraged this change with money for buyers and with plans to end the sale of petrol cars in the next ten to fifteen years. The reason is simple: transport produces a large share of the gases that heat the planet, and electric cars produce no exhaust from the car itself. They also reduce noise and improve air quality in crowded cities.

However, the picture is more complicated than it looks. An electric car is only as clean as the electricity that charges it. If the power comes from coal, the benefit is smaller, though studies suggest that, over its whole life, an electric car still produces fewer emissions than a petrol one. As more electricity comes from wind and sun, this advantage will grow.

The battery is another difficulty. Making one needs metals such as lithium, cobalt, and nickel, and mining them can damage the environment and, in some places, harm workers. Companies are now trying to use less of the most problematic materials, and to recycle old batteries so that the metals can be used again. Progress is real, but it is not yet fast enough.

Buyers also have practical worries. Electric cars still cost more than similar petrol cars, although prices are falling. People who live in flats often have no place to charge a car at home. Charging stations are still rare in many regions, and charging takes longer than filling a tank. Many drivers fear that the battery will run out on a long journey, which experts call range anxiety. In reality, most daily trips are short, and a modern battery can easily cover them.

Finally, there is a bigger question. Even if every car were electric, we would still have traffic jams, large roads, and cities built around cars. Some experts argue that the real answer is not to replace each petrol car with an electric one, but to build cities where people can walk, cycle, and use public transport.

The electric car is a big step forward, but it is not a magic solution. It is part of a larger change in the way we move.
