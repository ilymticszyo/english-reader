Cheese is one of the oldest foods made by people. Nobody knows exactly when it was first made, but experts believe that it was discovered by accident several thousand years ago, when milk was carried in a bag made from the stomach of an animal. The warmth and natural substances inside the bag turned the milk into soft white lumps. Ever since, people have been exploring the many ways to make it.
 
The basic process is simple, but the results are endlessly varied. Milk is first warmed, and then a substance is added that makes it separate into solid parts, called curds, and a thin liquid. The curds are cut, heated, and pressed, and the salt is added. Finally, the new cheese is left to age. This last step is where the character develops. Some cheeses are ready in a few days, while others rest in cool cellars for years.
 
Many things affect the taste. The kind of milk matters, whether it comes from cows, sheep, goats, or buffalo, and so does the food that the animals eat. A cheese made in the mountains in summer, when animals eat fresh herbs and flowers, tastes different from one made in winter. Even the air in the cellar, which contains tiny living organisms, helps to shape the flavour. This is why many famous cheeses can only be made in certain places, and why producers are so proud of their traditions.
 
In small dairies around the world, the work has hardly changed for centuries. The cheesemaker begins early in the morning, when the milk is still warm. He or she must judge by touch and smell when the curd is ready. During ageing, the wheels of cheese are turned and washed by hand, and each one is tested before it is sold. There are no shortcuts, and mistakes can spoil weeks of work.
 
Industrial cheese is a very different business. Factories produce huge quantities, with machines doing most of the tasks. The result is cheaper and more consistent, and it has made cheese available to millions of people. But many food lovers feel that something is lost. They argue that the flavour of mass-produced cheese is plainer, and that traditional producers deserve support.
 
This has led to a revival of interest in small producers. In many regions, young people have taken over family farms, or left city jobs to learn the craft. Local markets and specialist shops have also helped, by allowing customers to taste before they buy.
 
A good piece of cheese tells a story of land, animals, season, and human patience. It cannot be rushed, and perhaps that is why it is so satisfying. When you next enjoy a slice, it is worth pausing to taste how much time it contains.
