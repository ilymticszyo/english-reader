For many years, the night train seemed to belong to the past. Low-cost airlines made it cheap to fly between European cities, and travellers who had little time preferred to arrive within a couple of hours rather than spend a whole night on the rails. Several countries cancelled their sleeper services because they were losing money. Yet in recent years, a growing number of these routes have been quietly brought back.

One reason is concern about the climate. Flying produces far more carbon dioxide per passenger than travelling by train, especially on routes of moderate length. As more people have become aware of this, some have decided to avoid short flights whenever an alternative exists. Governments have noticed the change in attitude, and a few have begun to support railways with money and new regulations.

Another reason is the changing way people think about time. A night train allows you to go to sleep in one city and wake up in another, which means you do not lose a day of your trip. You also save the cost of a hotel room. For someone travelling on a tight budget, or for a business traveller who wants to arrive rested, this can be a real advantage.

However, the revival is not without difficulties. Sleeper carriages are expensive to build, and many of the old ones are worn out. Companies that want to run night services must often borrow carriages that were built decades ago, which means that comfort is sometimes disappointing. Tracks are also shared with freight trains and day services, so a delay on one line can easily spread to others. Passengers who expected to arrive at seven in the morning sometimes find themselves waiting at a small station at nine.

Prices are another problem. Although a bed on a night train can be good value, tickets bought at the last minute are sometimes more expensive than a flight. Critics argue that unless fares become more predictable, the night train will remain an attractive idea for a small group of enthusiasts rather than a real alternative for most people.

Even so, the signs are encouraging. New carriages with private bathrooms and better beds are being designed, and several companies have announced plans to connect cities that have not had a direct service for years. If these plans succeed, the night train may become less of a nostalgic curiosity and more of an ordinary choice, something people consider the way they now consider a budget flight.
