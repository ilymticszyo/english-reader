For more than two centuries, the newspaper was the main way that people learned what was happening in their town and in the world. It arrived at the door each morning, paid for by readers and by advertisers. Today, that picture has changed beyond recognition. Millions of people read the news on their phones, often for nothing, and many local papers have disappeared. Is the newspaper dying, or just changing?

The problem began with money. For a long time, newspapers earned most of their income from advertising, especially small advertisements for jobs, houses, and cars. When these moved to websites that were cheaper and better targeted, the loss was enormous. At the same time, readers became used to free news online. Since the early 2000s, the number of journalists employed by newspapers in many countries has fallen sharply, and thousands of local titles have closed, leaving some towns without any regular coverage of their council or schools.

The consequences go beyond the industry. Researchers have found that in places where a local paper closes, fewer people vote, public spending tends to rise, and officials are less likely to be questioned. Journalism has a role in a democracy, because it brings out information that powerful people would rather hide. When it declines, it becomes harder for citizens to judge what is going on.

In response, newspapers have tried several new approaches. The most important is the digital subscription. A number of well-known papers now earn more from readers than from advertisers, convincing millions of people to pay for online access. They do this by offering something that cannot be found for free: reliable reporting, deep analysis, and special features. Other titles have introduced a limited number of free articles each month, or have asked readers to give voluntary donations.

Some publishers have focused on becoming more than a newspaper. They sell events, run podcasts, produce video, and offer newsletters. Others have formed non-profit organisations, supported by foundations and donations, which are not under pressure to make a profit. Small, specialised sites devoted to a single subject or neighbourhood have also appeared, and some have been successful.

The picture is uneven. A few large national titles are doing well, while many smaller ones struggle. Some of the success depends on a wealthy audience that can afford several subscriptions, which raises worries about a divided public: well-informed paying readers on one side, and others who rely on free but less reliable sources on the other. Technology companies, which control much of the online traffic, have also become powerful intermediaries, and some governments have demanded that they pay publishers for the use of their content.

There are also new threats. Artificial intelligence can now write simple news stories, and search tools may answer questions without sending readers to the original source. At the same time, false information spreads quickly on social media, making trustworthy reporting more valuable.

The printed paper may not last forever. But the need for reliable, carefully checked information will continue, and newspapers, in whatever form, are still the best way we have found to supply it.
