It is difficult to imagine life without a shared agreement about what time it is. Trains depart at 8:15, meetings start at ten, and a video call between two continents requires only a quick calculation. Yet this convenience is surprisingly recent. For most of history, time was a local matter, and the arrival of the railways turned that arrangement into a serious problem.

Until the nineteenth century, each town set its clocks by the sun. Noon was simply the moment when the sun stood highest in the sky. Because the sun reaches this point slightly later as one travels west, neighbouring towns had slightly different times, and nobody was troubled by the gap of a few minutes. A traveller on horseback moved so slowly that the difference hardly mattered.

Railways changed everything. A train could cover in an hour a distance that previously took a day, and it ran on a timetable that had to be understood in many places at once. In Britain, the problem became apparent around the year 1840, when passengers arriving from one city found that their watches disagreed with the station clock. The railway companies solved it for their own convenience by adopting a single standard, based on the time at the Royal Observatory in Greenwich, which became known as railway time. Many towns resisted at first, regarding it as an unwelcome interference, and some kept two clocks on their town halls, showing local time and railway time side by side. Within a few decades, nearly everyone had accepted the new arrangement, and in 1880, it became the legal standard.

The situation in the United States was more chaotic. With a continent to cross, the railways operated according to dozens of different local times, and a timetable could be baffling. Stations in large cities sometimes displayed several clocks, one for each company. In 1883, the railway industry agreed to divide the country into four zones, and on a November day known as "the day of two noons", clocks were reset across the continent. The government did not make the system official for another thirty-five years.

The world as a whole required a still wider agreement. In 1884, delegates from more than two dozen countries met in Washington for an international conference, and they selected the line passing through Greenwich as the prime meridian, the zero point from which longitude is measured. Their choice was influenced by the fact that most of the world's shipping already used maps based on Greenwich. France abstained from the vote, and for years continued to define its time by reference to Paris.

The concept of time zones, in which the globe is divided into regions that keep a common hour, followed. Some countries chose odd arrangements, setting clocks half an hour or forty-five minutes apart from their neighbours, and a large country such as China uses a single time across a vast territory. Daylight saving, introduced in many countries during wartime to save fuel, added another layer of complication.

Modern technology has made precision extraordinary. Atomic clocks, which measure time by the vibrations of atoms, are so accurate that they would lose less than a second over hundreds of millions of years. They underpin the networks that guide aircraft, coordinate financial markets, and allow the phone in your pocket to find its position.

The question of whether the system will remain fixed is open to debate. Every few years, some politicians propose abolishing daylight saving, while others argue for keeping it throughout the year, and the arguments are often as much about habits and health as about economics. Scientists who study sleep warn that moving clocks twice a year disturbs our internal rhythms, and several studies have recorded small increases in accidents and heart problems in the days afterwards. There has even been a proposal to replace local time with a single universal time for the entire planet, which would make scheduling easier but would force some people to work in the middle of their night.

The history of standard time is a reminder that what seems natural is often the product of negotiation. The hour on your wrist is not a feature of the universe but an agreement, made by people who found that cooperation required them to share a clock.
