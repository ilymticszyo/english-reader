In 2023, the highest-paid footballers earned more in a single year than most people will earn in a lifetime, and the best-known athletes in basketball, tennis, and boxing received fortunes that would have been unimaginable to earlier generations. To many observers, such sums seem absurd, particularly when nurses, teachers, and firefighters earn a small fraction of them. Yet economists see nothing mysterious in the figures. They are the result of a particular structure of markets in which small differences in talent produce enormous differences in income.
 
The key idea is that of the "superstar" market, described by the economist Sherwin Rosen in the early 1980s. In many fields, a few individuals earn vastly more than the rest, even though they are only a little better. The reason is that their talent can be sold to a huge audience at almost no additional cost. A surgeon can only operate on one patient at a time, but a great footballer can be watched by hundreds of millions through television and the internet. Since fans want to see the best, and a slightly worse player is not a good substitute, the top performers receive a disproportionate share of the revenue.
 
Technology has intensified this effect. Broadcasting rights are now sold worldwide, and clubs and leagues earn income from advertisements, merchandise, and sponsors who wish to be associated with famous names. A player who attracts viewers and sells shirts is worth far more to a club than his performance on the pitch alone would suggest. The salaries paid to stars are, in a sense, a share of the profits that they bring.
 
Another factor is competition between clubs. The wealthiest ones, driven by the desire to win and by owners who may value prestige over profit, bid against each other for the few players who can make a difference. Because a single exceptional player can change the fortunes of a team, the price is driven upward. In football, a great deal of this money ends up with the players rather than the clubs, since the talent is scarce and the players can threaten to move elsewhere. Changes in the rules during the 1990s, which gave footballers more freedom to change clubs when their contracts ended, accelerated the rise in wages.
 
Critics raise concerns about fairness and the effects of this system. The gap between the top and the bottom is huge. Most professional athletes earn modest sums, have short careers, and face the risk of injury and an uncertain future after retirement. In team sports, wealthy clubs tend to win, which reduces competition and the unpredictability that makes sport exciting. Leagues therefore adopt measures to restore the balance, such as salary caps, the sharing of broadcasting income, or the draft system in the United States, in which the worst teams get the first choice of young players.
 
There is also the question of who really pays. Ultimately, the money comes from fans, through tickets, subscriptions, and the prices of products that advertisers sell. In some countries, public money has been used to build stadiums, even when the benefits to local communities are doubtful. And some argue that the huge wealth of top clubs, particularly when owned by states or investors with other interests, distorts the sport.
 
Women's sport shows the same principles at work, though at a different stage. Pay and attention have long been far lower, and campaigners argue that this is partly a result of past neglect: fewer broadcasts meant smaller audiences, which meant less money for the next season. As television coverage and attendance have grown in recent years, so have salaries, and some national teams have won agreements for equal pay. The comparison suggests that the market is not a fixed law of nature, but depends on choices about what to show and what to support.
 
Whether or not we approve, the economics are consistent. As long as audiences are global and talent is scarce, the rewards at the very top will be extraordinary. The more interesting question is not why stars are paid so much, but what we want sport to be.
