For most of history, loneliness was regarded as a private sorrow, an unfortunate feeling that people were expected to endure quietly. In the past decade, however, a growing body of research has led doctors and governments to treat it as something closer to a public health problem. In 2018, Britain appointed a minister for loneliness, and several other countries have since launched national strategies. A condition once discussed mainly by poets has become a subject for epidemiologists.

It is important to distinguish between being alone and feeling lonely. Some people live by themselves and are perfectly content, while others, surrounded by colleagues or relatives, feel painfully disconnected. Researchers define loneliness as the distressing gap between the social relationships a person has and those that person wants. Social isolation, by contrast, is an objective measure of how few contacts someone has. The two often overlap, but they are not identical, and both appear to carry health risks.

The evidence is striking, though it requires careful interpretation. Large studies that follow thousands of people over many years have found that those who report strong social ties tend to live longer than those who do not. Some analyses have suggested that the effect on mortality is comparable to that of well-known risks such as smoking or obesity. Lonely people are also more likely to experience depression, sleep poorly, and develop heart disease, and some research links chronic loneliness with faster decline in memory in later life. Scientists point out that illness can itself cause isolation, so the direction of cause and effect is difficult to establish. Nevertheless, the consistency of the findings has convinced many experts that the connection is real.

Several explanations have been proposed. Evolutionary psychologists argue that humans survived by living in groups, so the brain treats isolation as a threat and responds with stress. The body then spends more time on alert, with raised levels of stress hormones and inflammation, which over many years may damage the heart and the immune system. Lonely individuals may also take less exercise, eat less well, and have no one to notice when they become unwell or to encourage them to see a doctor.

Loneliness is not confined to the elderly, as is often assumed. Surveys in several countries have found that young adults report some of the highest levels, perhaps because they are moving between cities, changing jobs, and building relationships from scratch. Digital communication complicates the picture. For some, online contact provides a valuable lifeline, especially for people who are disabled or who live far from friends. For others, scrolling through the curated lives of acquaintances may increase the feeling of being left out, and the quality of these exchanges may not match that of a conversation in person.

What can be done? Because the causes vary, effective responses are also varied. Programmes that offer older people regular visits or telephone calls have shown some benefit. Doctors in a few places can now "prescribe" community activities, such as gardening groups or walking clubs, to patients who are isolated. Urban planners can contribute by creating libraries, parks, and cafes that encourage casual encounters, and employers can recognise that colleagues who work remotely may need deliberate opportunities to connect.

Measuring progress is itself a challenge. Because loneliness is subjective, surveys rely on people's own descriptions, and the answers may be affected by cultural attitudes. In some societies, admitting loneliness carries a stigma, particularly for men, who may be less willing to report it. Researchers are therefore experimenting with simple, neutral questions, such as how often someone feels left out or lacks companionship, and with long-term studies that follow individuals through changes in their lives. Better data will help policymakers to learn which interventions actually work, and to avoid spending money on well-intentioned programmes that fail to reach the people who need them most.

At the same time, we should be cautious about turning loneliness into another problem to be solved with a programme. Friendship cannot be ordered by a government, and well-meaning schemes may fail if they ignore what people actually want. Often the most effective step is individual and modest: contacting a neighbour, accepting an invitation, or simply listening with attention. A society that wishes to be healthier may need to rediscover the value of these small acts, and to build the time, the places, and the habits that make them possible.
