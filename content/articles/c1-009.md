When Alexander Fleming noticed in 1928 that a mould had killed the bacteria growing near it in his laboratory, he could hardly have predicted the consequences. Within two decades, the drug that came from his observation, penicillin, was saving soldiers from infected wounds. Infections that had been fatal became treatable, and surgery, childbirth, and cancer treatment grew much safer. Yet Fleming himself warned, in a speech in 1945, that careless use of the drug might teach bacteria to resist it. Today, his warning has become a central problem for medicine.

The mechanism is a straightforward example of evolution. In any large population of bacteria, a few individuals happen to carry genetic changes that allow them to survive a particular drug. When the antibiotic is used, the vulnerable bacteria die, but the resistant ones remain and multiply, passing on their advantage. The more often a drug is used, the stronger the pressure for resistance. Bacteria can also share these protective genes with unrelated species, so a trait that appears in one place can spread widely and quickly.

Human behaviour has accelerated this process considerably. In many countries, antibiotics have been prescribed for illnesses that they cannot cure, such as colds and most sore throats, which are caused by viruses. Patients sometimes stop taking a course of treatment as soon as they feel better, leaving the toughest bacteria alive. In some places, the drugs can be bought without a prescription. Meanwhile, large quantities are given to farm animals, not only to treat disease but to encourage growth and to prevent infections in crowded conditions. Residues from hospitals, farms, and factories then enter the water supply, giving bacteria even more opportunities to adapt.

The consequences are already visible. A recent major study estimated that infections resistant to treatment were directly responsible for more than a million deaths in a single year, a number higher than that for several well-known diseases. Doctors now encounter patients whose infections do not respond to the usual drugs and must resort to older, more toxic alternatives, or to medicines of last resort that are also beginning to fail. Routine procedures, such as hip replacements, could become dangerous if we can no longer prevent infection afterwards.

What makes the situation particularly troubling is the lack of new weapons. Developing a novel antibiotic is expensive and slow, and the financial rewards are poor. A drug that treats a chronic condition, such as high blood pressure, will be taken for years and therefore earns a steady income. An effective antibiotic, by contrast, is taken for a week, and doctors are encouraged to keep new ones in reserve to slow resistance. Many large pharmaceutical companies have therefore left the field, and the pipeline of new drugs has thinned.

Solutions require action on several fronts. Better diagnostic tests would help doctors to identify quickly whether an infection is bacterial, preventing needless prescriptions. Stricter rules on farm use could reduce the pressure for resistance. Basic measures, such as clean water, good sanitation, and vaccination, prevent infections before they start. Economists have also proposed new financial models, in which governments pay companies for the availability of a valuable new drug, regardless of how many doses are sold.

Public understanding is a further obstacle. Many people believe that it is the human body that becomes resistant to drugs, when in reality it is the bacteria that change. This misunderstanding can lead patients to assume that a new prescription is simply a stronger version of the old one, or that they have "used up" a medicine. Clear communication, from doctors and in schools, could help to correct such beliefs. Several countries have launched awareness campaigns, and early evidence suggests that doctors prescribe fewer unnecessary drugs when they receive regular feedback comparing their habits with those of their colleagues. Modest interventions of this kind, repeated across millions of consultations, may prove as valuable as any new laboratory discovery.

There is no dramatic moment in this crisis, no single disaster that captures the headlines. Instead, it advances quietly, one failed treatment at a time. That is precisely what makes it dangerous, and it explains why experts call for attention now, while effective drugs still exist.
