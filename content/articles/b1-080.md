We cross bridges every day, and we hardly think about them. But a bridge has a hard job. It must carry cars, trains, and people, and it must stand in wind, rain, and even earthquakes. How does it do this?

The idea is simple. A bridge has to deal with two main forces. One force pushes things together. The other pulls things apart. A good bridge uses both forces in a smart way.

The oldest kind of bridge is just a flat piece of wood or stone that lies across a river. This works for short distances. But a long, flat piece can bend and break in the middle.

A bridge with a curved shape is stronger. The weight on top pushes the stones against each other. The stones press together tightly, so the curve stays in place. The Romans built bridges like this that are still standing today.

For very long distances, engineers build a hanging bridge. The road hangs from thick steel ropes, and the ropes are fixed to tall towers. The ropes pull, and the towers push down on the ground. This allows the bridge to cross a wide river or a bay.

Engineers also think about wind and heat. Metal grows a little in hot weather, so bridges have small gaps that let them move. Some bridges are built to bend slightly in the wind. This makes them safer.

Next time you cross a bridge, look at its shape. You may see a flat road, a curve, or long ropes. Each one is a clever answer to the same question: how can we cross safely?
