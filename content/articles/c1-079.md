For most of the twentieth century, the high street was the centre of British town life. It was where people bought their bread from the baker, their meat from the butcher, and their clothes from a department store whose name had been familiar for generations. It was also where they met friends, heard the news, and felt a sense of belonging to a place. Today, in many towns, the same streets are lined with empty shops covered by boards. How did this transformation come about, and what should be done?
 
The first cause was the rise of the car and the large supermarket. From the 1960s onward, retailers discovered that they could offer lower prices and greater choice by building huge stores on the edge of town, with free parking. Customers who had once visited several shops on the high street now bought everything in a single weekly trip. Small shops, unable to compete on price, began to close. Later came out-of-town shopping centres, which brought together dozens of famous brands under one roof and added cafés and cinemas.
 
The second cause was the internet. Online retailers, with no need for expensive premises in prime locations, could sell at lower prices and deliver to the door. Books, music, electronics, and clothes moved steadily from the shop to the screen. The pandemic accelerated a trend that was already well underway, and several of the best-known high street chains collapsed. Meanwhile, rents and local taxes, which had been set in a different era, remained high, squeezing the margins of those who survived.
 
Yet it would be too simple to describe the decline as inevitable. In some places, the high street has proved surprisingly resilient. Towns with a distinct character, with independent shops, good markets, and attractive buildings, have often fared better than those dominated by identical chains. Others have adapted by offering what the internet cannot, such as personal service, expert advice, and experiences. Cafés, restaurants, hairdressers, and gyms, whose services cannot be delivered by post, have increasingly filled the gaps.
 
This points to a broader change in the role of the town centre. If its purpose is no longer mainly to sell goods, it may need to become a place where people live, work, learn, and meet. Several towns have converted empty shops into homes, offices, health clinics, and libraries. Some have created new squares and pedestrian areas, planted trees, and organised markets and festivals. The aim is to give people a reason to come, even when they have nothing to buy.
 
There are, however, obstacles. Property owners may prefer to keep a building empty in the hope of a better tenant rather than accept a lower rent. Planning rules may make it difficult to change the use of a building. And local councils, whose budgets have been cut, often lack the money to invest. Any recovery requires cooperation between councils, owners, businesses, and residents, as well as a clear vision.
 
Some countries have tried different models. In parts of continental Europe, town centres have been protected by strict limits on out-of-town shopping, and by policies that keep housing close to the centre, so that thousands of people live within walking distance of the shops. In some cities, community groups have been given the right to take over empty buildings and run them as workshops, cafés, or markets. The results vary, but the common lesson is that a lively centre rarely happens by accident. It needs people who live nearby, who care about the place, and who have a say in its future.
 
Nostalgia is not a strategy, and the high street of memory cannot be restored. But the desire for places where people meet is as strong as ever. If towns can find a new purpose for their centres, the empty windows may yet become a sign of renewal rather than decline.
