Few events in nature are as powerful as a volcanic eruption. In 1815, the explosion of a mountain in Indonesia was so loud that it was heard more than two thousand kilometres away. The ash that it threw into the sky was so thick that the following year, 1816, became known in Europe and North America as "the year without a summer", with failed harvests and widespread hunger. Yet volcanoes are not only destroyers. Without them, life on Earth might not exist in the form that we know.

To understand a volcano, we need to look beneath our feet. The surface of the Earth is made of huge plates of rock that move very slowly, by about the same speed that our fingernails grow. Most volcanoes are found where these plates meet. In some places, one plate slides under another and melts, and the hot liquid rock, called magma, rises through cracks to the surface. In others, the plates move apart, and magma rises to fill the gap. The "Ring of Fire", which surrounds the Pacific Ocean, contains about three quarters of the world's active volcanoes.

Not all eruptions are the same. Some volcanoes release their lava slowly, in rivers of fire that can be watched from a safe distance. Others explode with terrible force, because the magma contains a lot of gas that cannot escape. The most dangerous products of such eruptions are not the lava but clouds of burning gas and ash that race down the mountainside at hundreds of kilometres an hour. In 79 AD, such a cloud buried the Roman town of Pompeii in a few hours.

Today, scientists can reduce the danger. They measure small earthquakes, changes in the shape of the mountain, and the gases that escape from the ground, all of which can show that an eruption is coming. Thanks to this monitoring, thousands of people have been moved to safety in time. However, no one can say exactly when an eruption will happen, and more than eight hundred million people live within a hundred kilometres of an active volcano, often because the land around it is so good for farming.

This is the creative side of volcanoes. Ash and lava break down over time to produce rich soils, and farmers have grown crops on the sides of volcanoes for thousands of years. Volcanic activity also created many of the world's islands, including Iceland and Hawaii, and it provides heat that can be used to make electricity. Even the early atmosphere and oceans of our planet were shaped by gases from huge numbers of eruptions long ago.

Living near a volcano means accepting a risk in exchange for great rewards. As our knowledge improves, we may be able to enjoy the benefits while keeping the danger to a minimum.
