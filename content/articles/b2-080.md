Twenty years ago, a vegetarian in many countries had few choices in a restaurant. Today, supermarket shelves are full of plant-based burgers, milks made from oats and nuts, and products that look and taste like meat but contain none. What has caused this change, and will it last?

Several reasons are often mentioned. The first is health. Many doctors advise people to eat more vegetables, fruit, beans, and whole grains. Studies link such diets with a lower risk of heart disease and some other illnesses. Some people have cut down on red meat in particular, after warnings that large amounts may be harmful.

The second reason is the environment. Raising animals for food needs a lot of land, water, and feed, and it produces gases that contribute to climate change. Experts estimate that food production causes about a quarter of all gas emissions that heat the planet, and meat and dairy products account for more than half of that share. For people who worry about the climate, changing their diet seems one of the most direct ways to help.

The third reason is concern for animals. Many people are troubled by the conditions in which farm animals are kept, and they prefer not to support the system. Younger consumers in particular are more likely to say that they want to reduce their meat intake.

Companies have noticed. Food scientists now work to create products that match the taste and feel of meat, using plants, and some restaurants have added more vegetable dishes to their menus. Even fast food chains offer meat-free options. Sales of plant-based milk have grown strongly, and in some countries, it has taken a large part of the market.

Yet the trend has limits. Many plant-based products are highly processed, and critics say they may not be as healthy as simple foods like beans or lentils. Some are also expensive. Sales of meat substitutes grew quickly and then slowed in several markets, suggesting that some people tried them once and did not return. And traditional food cultures, such as those of herding communities, depend on animals in ways that cannot easily be replaced.

Most experts do not expect the world to become vegetarian. A more realistic outcome is that people will eat less meat, but not none, with more variety in what they choose. Even small changes, such as a few meat-free days each week, could make a real difference if many people join in.

Whatever the future holds, the change shows how quickly eating habits can shift when health, values, and convenience point in the same direction.
