Every year, millions of people travel to mountains. Some come to ski, some to climb, and others simply to sit with a view. Many of them cannot easily explain what they are looking for. Yet the pull of high places is very old, and it can be found in almost every culture.

In the past, mountains were often seen as the homes of gods. The ancient Greeks believed that their gods lived on Mount Olympus. In Japan, Mount Fuji has long been a sacred place, and pilgrims still climb it. In many parts of Asia, South America, and Africa, high peaks are connected with spirits and with the origins of local peoples. These beliefs are not surprising. A mountain rises above the ordinary world, and it seems closer to the sky.

For a long time, however, Europeans did not enjoy mountains. Travellers in earlier times described the Alps as ugly and frightening, and they closed the curtains of their carriages as they crossed them. The change came about two hundred years ago. Poets and painters began to describe mountains as sublime, a word for beauty mixed with fear. Scientists went up to study the rocks and the weather, and soon people were climbing for pleasure. The first climb of the highest peak in the Alps was soon followed by what is sometimes called the golden age of climbing.

Today, the reasons are varied. For some, it is the physical challenge: the effort of climbing, the cold, and the satisfaction of reaching the top. For others, it is the silence. In a world full of noise and screens, a mountain valley can feel very quiet. Studies suggest that time in natural places lowers stress, and that views of wide spaces help people think more calmly.

There is also a sense of perspective. Standing high above villages and fields, people often feel small in a positive way. Their daily worries seem less important. Many climbers say that the experience changes how they see their ordinary lives.

Yet there are costs. Popular peaks, such as Mount Everest, now suffer from crowds, rubbish, and risk. Glaciers are melting as the climate becomes warmer, and mountain villages must adapt to less snow. Visitors can help by choosing quieter times, carrying their waste away, and respecting local people.

Perhaps the real attraction of the mountains is that they remind us of something larger than ourselves. They are old, slow, and indifferent to human plans. That is humbling, but it is also a relief. For a day or two, we can leave our concerns below, and breathe thin, clear air.
