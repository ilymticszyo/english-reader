Today, nearly everyone has a phone in their pocket. But less than two hundred years ago, if you wanted to talk to someone in another town, you had to write a letter and wait for days. How did we get from letters to phones?

The telephone was invented around the year 1875 by a man called Alexander Graham Bell. Bell was a teacher of deaf students, and he was very interested in sound. He wanted to find a way to send a voice through a wire, in the same way that short signals already travelled through wires. He worked for years with his friend, Thomas Watson, in a small room full of wires and metal.

In March 1876, the two men were working in different rooms. Bell spilled something on his clothes and called out, "Mr. Watson, come here, I want to see you." Watson heard the words through the machine. They were the first words ever spoken on a telephone.

At first, many people did not believe that the telephone was useful. Some thought it was only a toy. But the idea spread quickly. Within a few years, there were thousands of phones in America, and cities began to fill with poles and wires. People had to call a worker, a person who connected their wire to the right one.

In the last century, phones became smaller and easier to use. People could dial a number by themselves, and later they could call another country. Then, about forty years ago, the first phones that you could carry became available. They were big and expensive, but they were the start of something new.

Today's phones are very different. You can send photos, watch films, find your way in a strange city, and pay for your shopping. Most people use them more for messages than for talking.

Yet the idea is the same as in 1876: a person speaks in one place, and another person hears the voice somewhere else. It is easy to forget how special that is.
