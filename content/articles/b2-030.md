Most of us have had days when work felt like too much. We were tired, bored, and impatient with our colleagues. Usually, a weekend or a holiday fixes the problem. But for some people, the tiredness never goes away. They wake up already exhausted, they stop caring about their job, and they feel that nothing they do matters. This condition has a name: burnout.

The term was first used in the 1970s by an American psychologist who noticed the problem among people who worked in health care and social services. Today, we know that it can affect anyone. In 2019, the World Health Organization described burnout as a result of stress at work that has not been well managed. It has three main signs: a feeling of energy running out, increasing distance from or negative feelings about one's job, and a drop in performance.

What causes it? It is not simply working long hours, although that can contribute. Researchers point to several other factors. One is having too much work and too few people to do it. Another is lack of control, for instance when you have responsibility for results but no power to decide how to reach them. Unfair treatment, unclear expectations, and a lack of support from managers all make things worse. Also, people who work for causes they care about, such as nurses and teachers, are at particular risk, because they find it hard to say no and often put their work before their own health.

Burnout is not only a personal matter. It affects families, whose members suffer when a person returns home empty of energy. It also costs employers a great deal, through sick days, mistakes, and the loss of talented workers who decide to leave.

So what can be done? Experts say that individual solutions, such as exercise, sleep, and meditation, can help, but they are not enough if the problem is the job itself. Real change needs to happen in the workplace. Managers can set realistic goals, share the work more fairly, and give people more choice in how they do it. Some companies have introduced rules against sending emails in the evening, so that people can rest. Above all, it helps to create a culture in which employees are able to say that they are struggling, without fear of losing their jobs.

If you recognise the signs in yourself, do not wait. Talk to someone you trust, such as a friend, a doctor, or a manager, and think about what could change. Burnout is not a sign of weakness. It is a sign that something in the way we work needs to be repaired.
