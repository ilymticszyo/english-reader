Every day, we are the targets of attempts to change our minds. Advertisers want us to buy, politicians want our votes, colleagues want our support, and even friends try to persuade us to choose a particular film. We experience most of this as a matter of reasoned argument, but psychologists have shown that our decisions are influenced by far more than logic. Understanding how persuasion works is therefore not only of professional interest to marketers; it is a form of self-defence.
 
One of the most influential researchers in the field, an American psychologist who spent years studying salespeople, charity workers, and advertisers, identified a small number of principles that appear repeatedly. The first is reciprocity: people feel obliged to return favours. A free sample in a shop, or a small gift from a charity, increases the chance that we will buy or donate, even though the gift was given in order to produce exactly that effect. The second is commitment and consistency. Once people have taken a small step, such as signing a petition, they are more likely to agree to a larger request later, because they wish to act in line with their earlier decisions.
 
The third principle is social proof. When we are uncertain, we look at what others are doing. Restaurants that display the label "most popular" sell more of that dish, and hotels that tell guests that most visitors reuse their towels achieve better results than those that simply appeal to environmental concern. In one study, a message saying that most neighbours had reduced their energy use led households to reduce theirs, whereas a message about saving money had little effect. People, it seems, are more influenced by what their peers do than by what experts say.
 
Authority is the fourth. We tend to obey, or at least trust, people who appear to be knowledgeable or powerful, and signs of authority, such as a white coat or a title, can be enough. Scarcity is the fifth: an item seems more valuable when it is rare, which is why advertisements tell us that stock is limited or that an offer will end soon. The last is liking. We are more easily persuaded by people whom we find attractive, who are similar to us, or who seem to like us.
 
These findings can be uncomfortable, because they suggest that our choices are less free than we like to think. Yet they are not simply tools for manipulation. Many of the same techniques are used for good ends. Public health campaigns use social proof to encourage vaccination, charities use commitment to build lasting support, and teachers use praise to create a positive relationship with their students. The difference lies in honesty and purpose. Persuasion that gives accurate information and respects the other person's interests is legitimate; persuasion that depends on deceiving, pressuring, or exploiting weaknesses is not.
 
There are also limits to what persuasion can do. People are less easily influenced on matters that are central to their identity, and attempts to change deeply held beliefs through facts alone often fail, sometimes even strengthening the opposition. This is one reason why political arguments so rarely result in agreement. More promising approaches involve listening, asking questions, and helping people to reach conclusions themselves, which research shows to be more effective than telling them what to think.
 
For those who wish to resist unwanted influence, a few habits are helpful. Pause before decisions that are made under pressure of time. Ask whether a message is appealing to evidence or only to emotion. Notice when you are being offered something free, and ask what is expected in return. And remember that a feeling of being certain is not the same thing as being right.
 
Persuasion is a basic part of social life, neither good nor bad in itself. Knowing how it works lets us use it more fairly, and be persuaded more wisely.
