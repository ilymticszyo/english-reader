We breathe air through our noses and mouths. Our lungs take oxygen from the air and pass it into the blood. But fish live in water. They cannot use lungs like ours. So how do they breathe?

The answer is the gills. Gills are the red parts on each side of a fish's head, behind the eyes. They are made of thin layers that look like the pages of a book. Each layer is full of tiny blood vessels.

Water contains oxygen, though much less than air. A fish takes water into its mouth and then closes its mouth. The water is then pushed over the gills. As the water passes, oxygen goes from the water into the blood, and a waste gas goes out of the blood into the water. Finally, the water leaves the body through openings on the side.

Some fish, like sharks, must keep moving to breathe. Water passes over their gills only when they swim with their mouths open. If a shark stops, it will not get enough oxygen. Other fish can stay still and pump water over their gills.

Warm water holds less oxygen than cold water. This is why fish often swim near the surface or near moving water, where there is more oxygen. In very hot summers, some fish in ponds and rivers die because there is not enough oxygen.

If you take a fish out of the water, the gills stick together and dry out. The fish cannot breathe, even though there is plenty of air around it. So a fish out of water is really in trouble.

A few fish, however, can breathe air as well. They have special parts that work like simple lungs. These fish can live for a time on land.
