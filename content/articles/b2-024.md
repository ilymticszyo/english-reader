For more than a century, the five-day working week has been the standard in most countries. It seems natural, almost like a law of nature. Yet it was created by human decisions, and, some argue, it may be time to change it. Around the world, companies and governments are testing a shorter week, in which employees work four days instead of five, usually without a cut in pay.

The idea is not new. In 1926, a famous car manufacturer in the United States shortened the working week for his factory workers, believing that people with more free time would also have more time to buy things, including cars. Over the following decades, the working week gradually became shorter in many countries, as productivity increased. Supporters of the four-day week say that technology has made us far more productive than our grandparents, so it is reasonable that we should work fewer hours.

In recent years, a number of trials have attracted attention. In 2022, around sixty companies in Britain tried the shorter week for six months while researchers followed their progress. At the end, most of the businesses reported that their income had remained stable or even grown. Many staff said that they felt less stressed and less tired, and fewer people left their jobs. Most of the firms decided to keep the new schedule after the experiment finished.

Why might shorter hours work? One explanation is that people concentrate better when they know that time is limited. Companies in the trials often reduced the number of long meetings, cut unnecessary tasks, and encouraged workers to focus. Another is simple rest. A tired employee makes more mistakes, and a free day gives time for sleep, family, or hobbies, which may improve both health and mood.

Nevertheless, the idea has critics. Some jobs are difficult to compress. A hospital, a shop, or a restaurant needs people to be present for many hours, and cutting the week might require hiring more staff, which increases costs. Others worry that, in practice, workers will be expected to do five days of work in four, which could create stress rather than relief. Small companies with narrow profits may find the experiment too risky.

There is also a question of fairness. If office workers enjoy a three-day weekend while nurses and delivery drivers continue to work five days, the gap between different kinds of employment could widen. Policy makers will need to think about how to share the benefits across the whole society.

For now, the evidence is encouraging but incomplete. Most trials involved volunteer companies that were already interested, so the results may not apply everywhere. But the debate has already changed the way many of us think about work. It reminds us that the length of the working week is a choice, and that societies can decide to make a different one.
