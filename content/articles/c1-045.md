Most of us have experienced the link between our emotions and our stomach. Anxiety before an important interview can cause a knot in the stomach; fear may lead to a sudden need to visit the toilet; and a feeling of excitement is described as having "butterflies" in the stomach. For a long time, such sensations were treated as a curious by-product of the nervous system. Over the past two decades, however, scientists have come to view the relationship between the gut and the brain as a continuous, two-way conversation, one that may influence mood, behaviour, and even the risk of disease.
 
The physical basis of this conversation is remarkable. The tube that carries our food contains a network of hundreds of millions of nerve cells. This "gut nervous system" can control the work of the stomach largely on its own, and it is sometimes called the second brain, although this description is somewhat misleading, since it does not think or feel in any conscious sense. It is connected to the brain by the vagus nerve, a long route through which signals travel in both directions, and by chemical messengers in the blood. Interestingly, a large share of the body's serotonin, a chemical associated with mood, is produced in the gut.
 
The most exciting discoveries concern the countless tiny living things that live in our intestines. These bacteria help to digest food, produce vitamins, and train the body's defences against disease, and it now appears that they also communicate with the nervous system. In a series of experiments, mice that had been raised without any gut bacteria showed abnormal stress responses and changes in brain chemistry. When they were given bacteria from other mice, their behaviour altered: timid animals became bolder after receiving microbes from adventurous ones. Studies in humans, though less clear, have found that people with depression and anxiety often have a different mixture of gut bacteria from healthy individuals.
 
It is tempting to leap from such findings to bold claims, and the commercial world has done so with enthusiasm. Shops now sell products that promise to improve mood, and books advise readers to eat for happiness. Scientists urge caution. Most of the strongest evidence comes from animals, and the human studies are generally small and difficult to interpret. A link does not show cause: a person who is depressed may eat a poor diet, which changes the microbiome, rather than the other way round. At present, there is no firm proof that any commercial product truly treats a mental disorder.
 
Nevertheless, some practical conclusions seem justified. A diet rich in fibre, vegetables, and foods such as yoghurt appears to support a diverse and stable community of gut bacteria, and diversity is generally regarded as a sign of health. Highly processed food, which is low in fibre and high in sugar, tends to have the opposite effect. Antibiotics, which are vital when they are needed, kill harmless bacteria along with harmful ones, and recovery of the microbiome can take months, a reason for using them only when necessary.
 
The research may eventually lead to new treatments. Doctors are already using bacteria from healthy people to cure a serious stomach infection, and trials are under way to test whether similar methods can help in other conditions. Whether such therapies will ever become part of mental health care remains uncertain.
 
What the evidence does suggest is that the old division between body and mind is less sharp than we thought. Our feelings are not located only in the head, and caring for the digestive system may be one of the ways in which we care for the mind.
