If you look at a city from above, you will see a surprising amount of unused space. Millions of roofs sit empty in the sun, covered in grey tar or metal. Increasingly, city planners are asking a simple question: what if these surfaces were covered in plants instead?

A green roof is exactly what it sounds like: a roof that supports a layer of soil and vegetation. Some are thin carpets of tough, low plants that need almost no care. Others are real gardens, with trees, vegetable beds, and benches where office workers can eat lunch. Beneath the soil, a waterproof layer protects the building, and a drainage system carries away any extra water.

The benefits are surprisingly wide. First, green roofs help to keep buildings cool. On a hot summer afternoon, a dark roof can reach a temperature far above the air around it, and that heat passes into the rooms below. Plants, on the other hand, release water vapour, which cools their surroundings. Studies in several cities suggest that buildings with green roofs need less air conditioning, which saves both money and energy.

Second, green roofs reduce flooding. In a traditional city, rain falls on hard surfaces and rushes into the drains all at once. After a heavy storm, the pipes can fill up quickly, and water pours into the streets. A layer of soil acts like a sponge. It holds a large part of the rainfall and releases it slowly, which gives the drains time to cope.

Third, they support wildlife. Insects, birds, and even small plants find a home on roofs that would otherwise be empty. In busy urban areas, where natural land is rare, these small spaces can form a network that helps bees and butterflies move through the city.

Of course, there are drawbacks. A green roof is heavier than a normal one, so older buildings may need extra support. It is also more expensive to install, and someone has to maintain it. Critics point out that the savings can take years to appear, and that a poorly built roof may leak.

Even so, many governments believe that the advantages are worth the cost. Some cities now require green roofs on new buildings of a certain size, while others offer money to owners who build them voluntarily.

The idea is not entirely new. People in northern Europe have covered their houses with grass for centuries. What has changed is the scale. As cities grow and the climate becomes warmer, the humble roof may turn out to be one of the most valuable pieces of land we have.
