For more than a century, the central business district has been the symbol of economic life. Tall towers filled with desks, surrounded by cafes, sandwich shops, dry cleaners, and train stations, depended on one regular rhythm: thousands of people arriving every morning and leaving every evening. When millions of employees were suddenly sent home in 2020, that rhythm stopped. Several years later, it has not fully returned, and a question that once seemed absurd is now seriously discussed: what is the purpose of the city centre if the office is no longer essential?

The immediate effects have been easy to see. In many large cities, office attendance remains well below its previous level, and a considerable amount of commercial space stands empty. Businesses that served commuters, such as lunch counters and shoe repairers, have closed or moved. Public transport systems, which relied on rush-hour fares, face budget problems, and city governments that collected taxes from commercial property are worried about falling revenue. Landlords have started to convert offices into apartments, though the deep floor plans of many modern buildings, which have few windows, make this surprisingly difficult.

At the same time, other places have benefited. Suburbs and smaller towns have seen more weekday activity, as people who no longer commute spend money in their local shops and cafes. Some workers have moved to cheaper regions, and a few countries have even introduced special visas to attract those who can work from anywhere. For individuals, the advantages are real: the saved commuting time, the flexibility to care for children, and the freedom to choose where to live. Surveys regularly show that employees value remote work so highly that many would accept a lower salary to keep it.

Employers are divided. Some argue that remote work has proved to be as productive as the office, and that it allows them to hire from a wider pool of talent while saving on rent. Others worry that it weakens the informal exchanges through which junior staff learn, ideas are born, and trust between colleagues is built. A casual conversation by the coffee machine may seem trivial, but it can lead to a solution that a scheduled video call would never produce. There is some evidence that newcomers who work entirely from home advance more slowly and feel less attached to their companies.

The result, in many organisations, is a compromise known as hybrid work, in which employees attend the office on certain days and remain at home on others. This arrangement has its own difficulties. If colleagues choose different days, the benefits of meeting in person are lost, and the office may feel half empty on the days when it is open. Managers must also ensure that those who are present do not receive an unfair advantage over those who are not, since being visible can influence promotion.

The longer-term implications for cities depend largely on how they respond. Urban centres have always been more than collections of offices. They offer theatres, museums, restaurants, universities, and the unplanned encounters of crowded streets, qualities that attract residents and visitors in their own right. Cities that adapt by increasing housing downtown, supporting culture and nightlife, and improving public spaces may find that a smaller office population is not a disaster but an opportunity to become more varied and more liveable.

Others may struggle. Places that depended on a single industry or that offered little beyond workplaces could enter a cycle of decline, in which vacant buildings reduce property values, lower tax revenue forces cuts in services, and fewer services make the area less attractive. Policymakers therefore face a delicate task: to support the transition without trying to preserve an arrangement that people no longer want.

What seems certain is that the old pattern will not return in its original form. The office will remain important, but it will be one option among many, not an obligation. The cities that thrive may be those that offer people a reason to come together because they choose to, and not simply because they have to.
