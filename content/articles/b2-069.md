Many successful people can name a person who helped them at a crucial point in their career: a teacher, a manager, or an older colleague who offered advice, opened doors, or simply listened. This relationship is called mentoring. Once left to chance, it is now organised by many companies, and the evidence suggests that, done well, it benefits everyone involved.

A mentor is usually someone with more experience who shares knowledge, gives guidance, and offers support to a less experienced person, called the mentee. The relationship is different from that of a boss and an employee. A mentor does not evaluate performance or decide on pay. Rather, the aim is to help the other person to grow, think through problems, and understand how the organisation works. The best mentors ask questions rather than give orders, and they encourage the mentee to find answers.

For the mentee, the benefits are clear. A mentor can explain unwritten rules that no manual describes, such as how decisions are really made or how to handle a difficult colleague. They can recommend books, introduce useful contacts, and give honest feedback in a safe setting. Studies have found that people who have mentors tend to be promoted more often, earn more, and feel more satisfied with their jobs, although it is hard to be sure how much of this comes from the mentoring and how much from the fact that ambitious people are more likely to find mentors.

Mentors benefit too. Explaining ideas to someone else deepens understanding, and many mentors say that they learn new things from their younger colleagues, especially about technology and changing attitudes. The role can also bring a sense of purpose, particularly in later stages of a career. In some companies, "reverse mentoring" has appeared, in which a young employee advises a senior manager on matters such as social media or new tools.

Organisations gain as well. Mentoring helps new employees to settle in more quickly, and it can help companies to keep talented staff, who are more likely to stay if they feel supported. It also spreads knowledge, which might otherwise be lost when experienced workers retire. In addition, it can help underrepresented groups, who may lack informal networks, to develop their careers.

However, mentoring does not always work. The pairing must be right. If the mentor and mentee do not get on, or if the mentor lacks the time, the relationship may fade. Mentors who simply try to produce a copy of themselves can limit the mentee's own development. Programmes that force people into pairs without consideration of their interests can feel artificial.

Good practice, therefore, includes clear goals, regular meetings, and the freedom to end a pairing without blame. For individuals, it is worth looking for a mentor outside one's own department, and being clear about what one wants. A good mentee comes prepared with questions and follows up on advice.

Mentoring is not a magic formula, but it recognises something simple and true: that most of what we learn in work comes from other people.
