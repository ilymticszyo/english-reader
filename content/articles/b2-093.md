Rising sea levels are a well-known danger for coastal cities. Less well known is that many of those cities are also sinking. In some places, the ground is falling much faster than the sea is rising, and the combination makes flooding a growing threat to millions of people.
 
One of the clearest examples is Jakarta, the capital of Indonesia. Parts of the city have sunk by several metres in recent decades, and in some districts the ground is still dropping by many centimetres each year. A large part of the city now lies below sea level. The government has even announced plans to move the capital to another island.
 
The main cause is the pumping of water from beneath the ground. In Jakarta, as in many cities, the public water supply cannot meet the demand, so people and businesses dig wells. As water is removed from the layers of sand and clay below the city, the layers shrink, and the surface sinks. It is rather like squeezing a sponge. Once the clay has been compressed, it cannot easily recover, even if water is allowed to return.
 
Other forces contribute. The weight of heavy buildings presses on soft soil. Cities built on land that was once swamp or on land that has been created by filling in the sea are especially at risk. In some places, the removal of oil and gas has had a similar effect. Natural processes also play a part. In some regions, the ground is still slowly moving after the weight of ancient ice sheets disappeared thousands of years ago.
 
Examples can be found around the world. Mexico City has sunk by many metres over the past century, and its old buildings lean and crack. Parts of Bangkok, Manila, and Lagos are also sinking. In the United States, areas near Houston and in Louisiana have lost ground. Even Venice, which is famous for its flooding, has suffered from the pumping of groundwater in the past, although the practice was stopped.
 
The consequences are serious. Floods become more frequent and last longer. Roads, pipes, and buildings crack, and salty water enters the supplies of fresh water. The cost of repairs is huge, and poor neighbourhoods usually suffer first.
 
Solutions exist, but they are not easy. The most important step is to reduce the pumping of groundwater, which requires a reliable supply of piped water. Some cities have introduced fees for using wells, or have built systems to store rainwater. Others have constructed sea walls and large pumps, though such measures are costly. In some places, engineers are trying to put water back into the ground.
 
The story of sinking cities shows that environmental problems often have local causes as well as global ones. Acting on the local causes can buy valuable time.
