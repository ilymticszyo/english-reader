Science has a reputation for reliability that few other human activities can match. Its claims are supposed to be tested, challenged, and, if necessary, discarded. Over the past decade, however, researchers in several fields have become uneasy about the quality of their own work. When teams have attempted to repeat well-known experiments, they have often failed to obtain the same results. This situation, called the reproducibility crisis, has prompted a period of soul-searching that is still under way.

The scale of the problem became apparent in 2015, when a large group of psychologists tried to replicate one hundred studies that had been published in leading journals. Only about a third to a half of the results could be reproduced, depending on how success was defined, and the effects that did appear were generally smaller than those originally reported. Similar efforts in other fields have produced comparable findings. A biotechnology company reported that it had been able to confirm only a small fraction of the landmark cancer studies it examined, and surveys of working scientists show that a majority have, at some time, been unable to reproduce another researcher's experiment, or even their own.

Several factors help to explain how this could happen, and few of them involve outright fraud. The first is the way in which statistical evidence is interpreted. Researchers commonly treat a result as "significant" if it has less than a five percent probability of arising by chance. But if one tests many hypotheses, some will pass this threshold purely by luck. A scientist who analyses the data in many different ways, and then reports only the combinations that gave a striking result, can produce apparent discoveries from random noise, often without any deliberate intention to mislead. This practice is sometimes called "p-hacking".

A second factor is the pressure of academic careers. Journals prefer exciting findings to dull confirmations, and researchers who publish many positive results are rewarded with jobs and funding. Negative results, such as an experiment that found no effect, are rarely published, which means that the visible literature gives a distorted impression of the evidence. This "file drawer problem" encourages scientists to run small studies, to chase novelty, and to avoid the slow, unglamorous work of checking what others have claimed.

Third, many experiments are simply difficult to repeat. Methods may be described too briefly, the materials may be unavailable, and small differences in laboratory conditions can influence results, especially in biology. Replication is also unattractive: it costs time and money, and brings little prestige. A young researcher who spends a year repeating someone else's experiment risks falling behind colleagues who have produced new and publishable findings of their own.

The scientific community has responded in several ways. Some journals now accept papers on the basis of the research question and the planned method, before the results are known, which reduces the temptation to hunt for striking outcomes. Others require authors to share their data and computer code, and to register their hypotheses in advance. Large collaborative projects, in which many laboratories test the same question with the same protocol, have also become more common.

It would be wrong to conclude that science is broken. The fact that these problems were discovered, and are being discussed so openly, demonstrates the strength of the system rather than its weakness. Yet the episode carries a lesson for the public as well. A single surprising study, however widely reported, is rarely the final word, and we should place more trust in findings that have been repeated by independent teams. Journalists and readers alike would do well to ask how large a study was, whether it has been confirmed elsewhere, and who paid for it. Science advances not through isolated flashes of brilliance but through a slow, collective, and sometimes uncomfortable process of correction.
