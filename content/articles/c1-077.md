Few ideas are more attractive than meritocracy, the principle that rewards should go to those with talent and who work hard rather than to those with the right family or connections. Politicians of every persuasion praise it, parents encourage their children to believe in it, and many people see it as the very definition of fairness. Yet in recent years, a growing number of thinkers have asked whether the ideal, in practice, may be producing consequences that are less benign than its supporters imagined.
 
The word itself has an ironic history. It was coined in a satirical book by the British sociologist Michael Young, who published his essay on the subject in the late 1950s. In his imaginary future, society is organised around intelligence and effort, and those at the top come to believe that they deserve their position. The result is not harmony but a new kind of arrogance, and a resentful underclass who are told that their failure is entirely their own fault. Young meant it as a warning. Ironically, the term was soon adopted as a compliment.
 
The strongest objection concerns the supposed fairness of the competition. Talent is not distributed evenly, nor is it the only thing that matters. Children from wealthy families attend better schools, receive private tuition, and benefit from parents who understand how the system works. In many countries, a large proportion of places at the most prestigious universities go to students from the richest households. The race may be open to everyone, but some begin much closer to the finish line. In this way, meritocracy may transform inherited advantage into something that looks like earned success.
 
A second concern is moral. If success is thought to reflect merit, then those who succeed may feel that they owe little to anyone else, and those who struggle may feel personally responsible for their circumstances. The philosopher Michael Sandel has argued that this attitude erodes solidarity, because it weakens the sense that we are all dependent on luck, on the contributions of others, and on the society in which we live. It may also help to explain the anger of those who feel that elites look down on them, a resentment that has shaped political movements in many countries.
 
Then there is the question of what we mean by merit. Societies reward certain talents, such as the ability to perform well in examinations, to manipulate numbers or words, and to command high prices in the market, far more generously than others, such as care for the sick, teaching, or craftsmanship. These preferences are not facts of nature but reflect choices about what we value. A talent for one thing is rewarded lavishly in one age and ignored in another.
 
Defenders of meritocracy reply that the alternatives are worse. Societies that distribute positions by birth, favour, or political loyalty are not only unfair but inefficient, since they waste talent. The ideal of merit has opened doors for people who were once excluded because of their class, race, or sex. The problem, they argue, is not the principle but the failure to practise it fully, which could be corrected through better schools, fairer admissions, and more support for the disadvantaged.
 
Critics propose other remedies. Some suggest that universities should select by lottery among qualified applicants, which would reduce the pressure and the sense of entitlement. Others emphasise the dignity of work, arguing that the pay and respect given to essential jobs should be increased. What they share is a wish to loosen the link between worth and success.
 
The debate does not require us to abandon the idea that talent and effort should matter. It asks us to be modest about our achievements, generous toward those who have been less fortunate, and honest about how much of success is luck.
