Every traveller knows the moment: a few hours before the flight home, standing in a shop near the airport, wondering what to bring back. A fridge magnet, a small bottle of local wine, a scarf, a carved wooden bird. The objects are rarely useful, and they are often made in a factory far from the place they are supposed to represent. Yet millions of people buy them every year, and the souvenir industry is worth many billions. What are we really buying?
 
The word itself helps to explain the answer. "Souvenir" is French for "memory", and in its older sense, it meant a thing that helps us to remember. Its history is closely tied to the history of travel. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, wealthy young Europeans completed their education with a "Grand Tour" of France and Italy, and they returned home with paintings, sculptures, and pieces of ancient stone. The objects were proof that they had been there, and they were also signs of taste and status. As travel became cheaper in the nineteenth century, the market spread, and cheap copies of famous monuments appeared in every tourist town.
 
Psychologists suggest that souvenirs do several things at once. First, they act as triggers for memory. A shell from a beach or a ticket from a museum can bring back a whole afternoon, with its sounds and smells, in a way that a photograph might not. Second, they serve as evidence for others. When we show a friend the pot that we brought from a village in the mountains, we are also telling a story about ourselves: where we have been, and what kind of person goes there. Third, they are gifts, and a gift for someone at home is a way of saying that we thought of them while we were away.
 
Yet there are real problems with the trade. Many of the objects sold as local crafts are mass-produced elsewhere, and the money from their sale goes to distant companies instead of the people of the place. Some souvenirs cause real harm: the trade in coral, ivory, and the shells of endangered turtles has contributed to the decline of these species, and travellers who bring home sand, stones, or ancient fragments from historic sites may damage the places that they admire. Several countries now have laws against taking such objects out of the country, and customs officers regularly confiscate them.
 
There is also a philosophical question. If a souvenir is made quickly and in large numbers for visitors, is it still a true expression of the place? Some scholars argue that this is the wrong question. Culture is never fixed, they say, and crafts have always changed to meet new buyers. A pot that is made for tourists is still made by someone who needs a living, and the skill involved may be as real as that of any older piece. What matters is honesty about how and by whom it was made, and fairness in the price.
 
Many travellers are now looking for alternatives. Some buy directly from the makers, in markets or workshops, and prefer one thing of quality to many small things. Others bring home recipes, spices, or photographs, or simply write about the journey. A few choose to bring nothing at all, believing that the best memories do not need objects.
 
The impulse to bring something home is very old, and probably it is a good one. It reminds us that travel is not only about seeing but about carrying a little of the world back to our ordinary lives. The best souvenir, perhaps, is the one that keeps asking us to remember why we went.
