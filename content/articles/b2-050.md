When we are children, friends seem to arrive without effort. We meet them at school, in the street, or at the swimming pool, and we see them every day. But as adults, many people discover that friendships are harder to make and, above all, harder to keep. Jobs, children, and moving house all take time, and somehow years pass without a proper conversation with someone we once saw every day.

Researchers have studied how adult friendships work, and some of their findings are surprising. One well-known study suggests that it takes about fifty hours of time together to turn an acquaintance into a casual friend, and more than two hundred hours to become a close one. For children, these hours come naturally, because they spend so much time in the same place. For adults, they must be planned.

Another difficulty is that many adults believe that they are the only ones who feel lonely. In reality, surveys in several countries show that a large share of people report that they have fewer close friends than they would like. Men seem particularly affected. Some research suggests that men often rely on one person, usually a partner, for emotional support, and that they find it harder to talk about their feelings with other men. When the relationship ends, or when they retire from work, they can find themselves with no one to turn to.

The consequences are more serious than they may appear. A large body of research links strong social connections with longer life and better health. Some studies have found that the risk of dying early is as high for people who are isolated as it is for those who smoke fifteen cigarettes a day. Friends also give us practical help, such as a lift to the airport, advice about a job, or someone to look after a child in an emergency.

So how can we keep and make friends as adults? The first rule is regularity. Friendships are more likely to last when people meet at the same time, such as a weekly walk or a monthly dinner, than when they try to find a free evening each time. The second is to take the first step. Most people are pleased to be invited, but they wait for others to ask, and so nothing happens. A short message saying "I was thinking of you" can restart a friendship that has been quiet for years.

It also helps to join activities where you will see the same people again and again, such as a sports club, a choir, a language class, or a volunteer group. Shared interests give you something to talk about, and the repeated meetings give the friendship time to grow.

Finally, we should accept that not every friendship must last for ever. Some are linked to a particular period of life, and it is natural when they fade. What matters is to invest in the ones that bring us warmth and support, and to be the kind of friend that we would like to have.
