When we think of the ocean, we usually imagine bright blue water, sunny beaches, and colourful fish near the surface. But this is only a thin layer of a much larger world. Most of the ocean is dark, cold, and under enormous pressure, and it is the largest place for life on Earth. Even so, we know less about it than we do about the surface of the Moon.

Sunlight can only travel a short way through water. After about two hundred metres, there is too little light for plants to grow, and below one kilometre, the darkness is total. Here, the temperature is close to freezing, and the pressure is hundreds of times greater than at the surface. A human being would be crushed in an instant. It seems an impossible place to live.

Yet life is abundant, and it has found remarkable ways to adapt. Many animals produce their own light, a skill known as bioluminescence. The anglerfish, for example, carries a glowing piece of flesh above its mouth like a fishing rod, which attracts curious prey into its jaws. Other creatures use light to confuse enemies, to find partners, or to disappear by matching the faint glow from above. Some animals have huge eyes to catch every possible particle of light, while others have given up sight altogether and rely on touch or smell.

Food is scarce in the deep, so many species have learned to live slowly. Some fish have a very low rate of movement and use little energy. Others wait for "marine snow", a constant fall of dead plants and animals from the waters above. When a large animal such as a whale dies and sinks to the bottom, it can feed a whole community of creatures for decades.

One of the most surprising discoveries came in 1977, when scientists exploring the sea floor found hot water pouring from cracks in the rock. Around these openings, called hydrothermal vents, they found thick colonies of worms, shellfish, and other animals. These creatures did not depend on sunlight at all. Instead, tiny bacteria turned chemicals from the vents into energy, and the whole community was built upon them. The discovery changed our understanding of how life can begin, and it made some scientists consider that life might exist under the ice of other planets.

Unfortunately, the deep ocean is not as remote as it seems. Fishing boats now reach very deep waters, and companies are interested in mining metals from the sea floor. Many scientists warn that damage in such places may last for centuries, because the animals grow and recover so slowly. Plastic has even been found in the deepest known parts of the ocean.

There is still much to learn. Each time researchers send a camera down, they find new species, some of them unlike anything seen before. Protecting this hidden world may be one of the great challenges of our time, but first we must continue to explore it.
