In 1971, the economist and psychologist Herbert Simon made an observation that now reads like a prophecy. A wealth of information, he wrote, creates a poverty of attention. At the time, most people consumed news from a single newspaper and a few television channels. Simon could not have imagined the modern smartphone, which delivers an endless stream of messages, videos, and alerts, each competing for a few seconds of our minds. Yet his insight explains why attention has become one of the most valuable resources in the contemporary economy.

The logic is straightforward. Many of the world's most successful digital services do not charge their users any money. Instead, they earn their revenue from advertising, and advertisers pay according to how many people see their messages and for how long. A platform therefore has a powerful financial incentive to keep us looking at its screen. Every design decision, from the colour of a notification to the infinite scroll of a feed, can be tested and refined to maximise the time we spend there. In this arrangement, we are not simply the customers; our attention is the product being sold.

Defenders of this model make a reasonable case. Free services have brought extraordinary benefits, from instant maps to access to knowledge that was once locked in libraries, and advertising has always paid for media in one form or another. Moreover, people are not helpless. We choose what to watch, and nobody forces us to open an application at midnight.

Critics, however, argue that the contest is badly unbalanced. On one side stand teams of engineers and psychologists, armed with detailed data about our habits and weaknesses. On the other side stands a tired individual with limited willpower. Features such as automatic video playback and unpredictable rewards, similar to the mechanism of a slot machine, are designed to exploit tendencies that evolved long before screens existed. Calling the outcome a free choice, they suggest, is a little like blaming a person for eating a biscuit in a room where every wall is made of biscuits.

The costs of this competition are difficult to measure but potentially significant. Researchers have linked heavy social media use to anxiety and poor sleep among young people, although the evidence on causation remains contested. More subtly, a constant stream of interruptions may damage our capacity for deep, sustained thought. Reading a long book or following a complicated argument requires a kind of patience that is hard to maintain when a device buzzes every few minutes. A society that loses this capacity, some thinkers fear, will find it harder to solve its hardest problems.

Possible responses fall into three broad categories. Individuals can take practical steps, such as switching off notifications, leaving the phone in another room, or setting limits on certain applications. Companies can be pressured, or required by law, to offer simpler settings, to be transparent about how their recommendation systems work, and to give users a genuine choice about being tracked. And society as a whole can reconsider what it values, perhaps treating attention in a similar way to clean air: a shared resource that deserves protection.

There is also a cultural dimension that economists tend to overlook. Every era develops its own habits of attention, and these shape what a society regards as normal. Our ancestors, bored in the evenings, told stories around a fire; later generations read novels aloud to their families. The modern habit of glancing at a screen between every two sentences is not an inevitable consequence of human nature but the product of particular technologies and business choices. Recognising this should be encouraging, because choices can be changed. Several countries have already begun to restrict phones in schools, and some employers now protect their staff from messages outside working hours. Whether such measures prove effective remains to be seen, yet they suggest a growing willingness to treat attention as something worth defending.

None of these solutions is simple, and none will eliminate distraction altogether. But the first step is to recognise that attention is not infinite, and that someone is always competing for it. Once we understand that our focus has a price, we can begin to decide, more consciously, how to spend it.
