In the early hours of a Sunday morning in September, a small fire began in a bakery in a narrow street in London. The baker had gone to bed after leaving some embers in his oven, and sparks set fire to a pile of wood nearby. Within a few hours, the flames had spread to neighbouring houses. Over the next four days, they destroyed much of the old city. The year was 1666.
 
Several factors made the disaster worse. London was a crowded city of timber houses, built close together along narrow lanes. Many were coated with tar to keep out the weather, which burned fiercely. The summer had been dry, and a strong east wind drove the fire through the streets. The fire-fighting methods of the time were basic. People used leather buckets and hooks to pull down burning buildings, but the wind spread the sparks faster than they could work.
 
The authorities also hesitated. The Lord Mayor was woken during the night, but he considered the fire unimportant and went back to bed. When it became clear that the fire was out of control, valuable time had been lost. Eventually, the king ordered that houses should be pulled down to create firebreaks, but even then, the plan was carried out slowly. A man named Samuel Pepys, who worked for the navy, watched the fire from the river and described it in his famous diary. He recorded the panic of the people, who carried their belongings to boats or fled into the fields.
 
By the time the fire ended, about thirteen thousand houses, nearly ninety churches, and the great cathedral of Saint Paul had been destroyed. Perhaps a hundred thousand people were left without homes. Surprisingly, only a small number of deaths were officially recorded, although historians suspect that the true number was higher, since the poor were not always counted.
 
Rebuilding began quickly. Several people, including the scientist Christopher Wren, offered plans for a new city with wide streets. In the end, the old layout was largely kept, since property owners wanted to rebuild on their own land. But new laws required that houses be built of brick or stone, and that streets be widened. Wren designed over fifty churches, and a new Saint Paul's, which still dominates the skyline.
 
The fire also led to new ideas about safety. Insurance companies began to appear, offering protection against fire damage, and soon had their own fire brigades. Over time, cities developed organised fire services and stricter building rules.
 
Today, a tall column known as the Monument stands near the place where the fire began. It reminds visitors how a small accident can become a great disaster, and how a city can recover and learn.
