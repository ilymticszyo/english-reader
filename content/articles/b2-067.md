Drive through the countryside in many parts of the world, and you will see them: tall white towers with long blades turning slowly in the wind. Wind turbines now supply a growing share of the world's electricity, and in some countries, they provide more than a quarter. They are cheap, clean, and fast to build. But they also raise questions, and they cannot solve every problem.

The principle is old. People have used the wind to turn mills for more than a thousand years. A modern turbine works in much the same way, except that the turning blades drive a generator that produces electricity. The bigger the blades and the higher the tower, the more power is produced, because winds are stronger and steadier at height. The largest turbines today have blades longer than a football pitch, and a single one can supply thousands of homes.

The main advantage is that wind produces almost no emissions while it is working. Making and transporting the turbines does produce some, but over its life, a wind turbine generates many times more energy than was used to build it. The fuel is free, and costs have fallen dramatically in the past ten years, so that in many places, new wind farms are now the cheapest source of electricity. Offshore farms, built at sea, can use stronger and more regular winds, and they take up no land.

However, the wind does not always blow. Output rises and falls with the weather, and on a calm day, a large wind farm can produce almost nothing. This creates a challenge for the electricity network, which must keep supply and demand in balance at every moment. Solutions include storing energy in batteries or other forms, building power lines that link regions with different weather, and keeping flexible power stations ready for calm days. None of these is free, and as the share of wind increases, the cost of managing variability grows.

Local opposition is another difficulty. Some residents find turbines ugly or noisy, and others worry about their effect on property values. Wind farms can also harm wildlife. Birds and bats are sometimes killed by the blades, although studies suggest that the numbers are small compared with deaths caused by buildings, cars, and cats, and good planning can reduce the risk by avoiding migration routes. Painting one blade black has been shown, in at least one study, to reduce collisions.

There are also practical issues with the end of a turbine's life. Blades are made of strong materials that are hard to recycle, and thousands of old blades are now being buried. Manufacturers are working on new materials that can be reused.

Despite these problems, the outlook is promising. Turbines continue to grow larger and more efficient, floating designs open the way to deeper water, and better storage is becoming cheaper. Most experts expect wind to play a central role in future energy systems, together with solar power and other sources.

The wind will not solve everything. But as part of a mixture, it is one of the most effective tools we have for cutting emissions.
