Music is found in every human society that we know of. People sing at weddings and funerals, soldiers march to drums, and parents all over the world sing softly to their babies. Yet music is also strange. Unlike food or shelter, it does not seem to help us survive. So why do we have it, and why does it move us so deeply?

One idea is that music is a kind of language, but one that speaks directly to the emotions. When we hear a slow piece in a minor key, we often feel sadness, while a fast rhythm in a major key tends to make us feel happy. Researchers have tested this in different cultures. In one study, listeners from a remote community in Africa, who had never heard Western music, were played short pieces from Europe. They were able to say which pieces were meant to sound happy, sad, or frightening. This suggests that some of the links between sound and emotion do not depend on culture alone.

Other findings point to the importance of rhythm. Even very young babies move their bodies in time with a beat, and they seem to enjoy it. Many scientists believe that this ability helped our ancestors to coordinate their actions, for example when they hunted, danced, or sang together. Singing in a group produces chemicals in the brain that make us feel closer to the people around us, which may explain why choirs and football crowds create such a strong sense of belonging.

Music also has a powerful relationship with memory. Many people can recall songs from their teenage years, even if they cannot remember what happened last week. Doctors who work with patients suffering from dementia have noticed that familiar songs can bring back names, faces, and feelings that seemed to be lost. Some patients who hardly speak are able to sing every word of a song from their youth. For this reason, music is now used in many care homes as a form of therapy.

At the same time, we should be careful about claims that music makes us more intelligent. A few years ago, a popular idea suggested that listening to classical music could improve a child's results at school. Later studies did not support this. Learning to play an instrument, on the other hand, seems to bring real benefits, such as better attention and a stronger ability to understand speech in a noisy room.

Not everything about music can be explained by science. Why does a particular piece make one person cry and leave another cold? Why do we sometimes feel a thrill down the spine at a certain moment in a song? Researchers have begun to study these questions, but the answers are still far from complete.

Perhaps that is what makes music so special. We understand some of how it works, but it still has the power to surprise us.
