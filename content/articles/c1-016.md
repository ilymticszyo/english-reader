When powerful text-generating software became widely available, many schools and universities reacted with alarm. Students could suddenly produce a fluent essay in seconds, summarise a difficult book, or solve a mathematics problem with a short instruction. Some institutions banned the technology; others hurried to buy detection tools that proved unreliable. A few years later, the initial panic has given way to a more thoughtful debate. The question is no longer whether artificial intelligence will affect education, but how educators should respond to something that is already in the classroom.

The optimistic case is easy to state. A patient digital tutor, available at any hour, could give every learner the kind of individual attention that was once a privilege of the wealthy. It could explain a concept in several different ways, provide an endless supply of practice exercises, and give instant feedback on a draft. Students who are embarrassed to ask questions in front of their classmates might find it easier to ask a machine. For teachers, the tools could reduce the burden of marking and administration, leaving more time for the human aspects of the job, such as encouragement and discussion.

The risks are equally apparent. The first is simple dishonesty: if a machine can write the essay, what is the incentive to do so oneself? More subtle is the danger that students will lose the opportunity for productive struggle. Learning researchers have long observed that effort, including the effort of being confused and trying again, is not merely unpleasant but necessary for deep understanding. A tool that removes every difficulty may produce an impressive piece of work and leave the student's mind unchanged. There is also the problem of reliability. These systems can produce fluent but false statements with complete confidence, and a learner who lacks background knowledge may not be able to detect the mistakes.

Education systems have therefore begun to reconsider what they teach and how they assess it. Some teachers have returned to handwritten examinations, oral discussions, and in-class writing, where it is harder to rely on automated help. Others have chosen the opposite approach, designing assignments that assume the use of such tools and ask students to evaluate, correct, and improve what the machine produces. In this model, the skill that matters is not producing a first draft but judging its quality, a task that requires real knowledge of the subject.

That point deserves emphasis. It is sometimes argued that facts no longer need to be learned, since information is always available. Yet critical thinking depends on a stock of knowledge held in the mind. To notice that a claim is implausible, to see a connection between two ideas, or to ask a sharp question, one must already understand something. A student who has memorised nothing cannot evaluate an answer, and therefore cannot use powerful tools wisely.

Questions of fairness also arise. Access to good technology and to the guidance needed to use it effectively is unequal, and there is a risk that the benefits will flow mainly to students who are already advantaged. Policymakers will need to consider whether schools can provide reliable access, and whether the data that learners generate is handled responsibly.

Teachers themselves will need support in this transition. Many were trained in a world where information was scarce and the main task was to deliver it, and they now find themselves expected to guide students through an unstable landscape of tools that change every few months. Professional development, time to experiment, and clear guidance from schools are essential, as is honest discussion with students about the purposes of an assignment. When learners understand why a difficult task is valuable, they are more likely to engage with it for its own sake, and less likely to look for a shortcut. Trust and motivation, rather than surveillance, may turn out to be the most effective safeguards.

In the end, the best response may be a shift of emphasis. Schools should continue to cultivate curiosity, judgement, and the habit of careful thinking, qualities that no software can supply on a student's behalf. The technology may change the methods of learning, but it does not change its purpose: to produce people who can understand the world and act wisely in it.
