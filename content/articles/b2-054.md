Every year, hundreds of millions of people cross borders carrying a small booklet that says who they are and where they come from. The passport is so familiar that it is hard to imagine travelling without one. Yet the system that we know today is surprisingly new, and for much of history, people moved from place to place with little more than a letter from a local official.

The idea of a travel document is old. In the Bible, a Persian king gave letters to an official to guarantee his safe journey through foreign lands. In medieval Europe, rulers issued letters to protect messengers and merchants, and the word "passport" probably comes from the need to pass through the gate, or "port", of a town or a fortress. These documents were rarely required, though, and most ordinary people never travelled far from home.

The nineteenth century brought great changes. Railways and steamships made travel faster and cheaper, and millions of people crossed oceans to find work. In most of Europe, ordinary travellers needed no passport at all in the middle of the century. Governments saw little reason to control movement, and in fact, many believed that the free movement of people was good for trade. A traveller could cross most borders by simply arriving, and a British writer of the time remarked that such papers were hardly needed.

The First World War changed everything. Afraid of spies and enemies, governments introduced strict controls on foreigners. After the war, the League of Nations, the forerunner of the United Nations, organised a conference that agreed on a standard form of passport, with a photograph and a description of the holder. By the 1920s, the booklet that we know today was becoming the norm, and the border had become a place of checks and questions.

Passports have also become tools of politics and, at times, of cruelty. In the twentieth century, governments used them to prevent citizens from leaving, to refuse entry to refugees, and to mark people as members of a particular group. Many who tried to escape persecution found that without the right document, no country would accept them. For them, a piece of paper meant the difference between safety and danger.

Today, passports are changing again. Most now contain a small chip with the holder's photograph and personal details, and machines at airports can check them in seconds. Some countries are testing digital versions that can be stored on a phone. At the same time, not all passports have equal value. A citizen of one country may be able to visit more than a hundred and fifty countries without a visa, while a citizen of another can visit only a few dozen. This inequality, which depends on where one is born, shows that the freedom to travel is still not shared equally.

The passport began as a simple letter of protection. It has become one of the most powerful documents that most of us will ever own.
