Every nation tells a story about itself. The story decides which events are celebrated with public holidays and monuments, which are taught to schoolchildren, and which are quietly left out. Historians have long known that public memory is not the same as history. History is an attempt to understand what actually happened, using evidence that is often incomplete and contradictory. Memory, by contrast, is shaped by the needs of the present: it tells people who they are, whom they should admire, and what they owe to one another.

Consider how differently two countries can remember the same war. In one, the conflict may be recalled as a heroic struggle in which ordinary people showed extraordinary courage. In the other, the same conflict may be associated with defeat, occupation, or shame. Neither version is entirely false, yet neither is complete. Each emphasises what is useful for national self-respect and tends to ignore details that complicate the picture, such as the suffering of civilians on the other side, or the cooperation of some citizens with the invader.

Forgetting, in this sense, is rarely an accident. A French scholar of the nineteenth century argued that the existence of a nation depends as much on forgetting as on remembering, because a society made up of groups with a violent past can only live together if it agrees not to dwell on every old injury. There is some wisdom in this claim. After civil wars, new governments have often chosen a policy of silence, hoping that peace would be easier to maintain if no one demanded accounts of who had done what to whom.

But silence has a price. Silenced memories do not disappear; they survive in families, in songs, and in whispered conversations, and they tend to return, sometimes with more force than before. Several societies that tried to forget their authoritarian pasts have found, a generation later, that the grandchildren of the victims were asking the questions that their parents had been afraid to raise. The demand for recognition is rarely satisfied by official indifference.

An alternative approach is to confront the past openly. Truth commissions, public apologies, and museums dedicated to difficult subjects are all examples. Germany's long and sometimes painful effort to examine its Nazi past is often cited as a model, although it is worth remembering that this process took decades and began largely under external pressure. Even then, it was not a straight path. For many years after the war, ordinary Germans preferred to speak of themselves as victims, and it was a younger generation that insisted on a franker accounting.

Monuments reveal the same tension. A statue is built to honour a person or an event, but it also expresses the values of the people who paid for it, often at a moment when they wanted to assert their power. When these values change, so does the meaning of the monument. Recent disputes over statues of slave traders and colonial administrators have been described by some as an attack on history. Others reply that the statues were never neutral records of the past; they were political statements, and communities have the right to reconsider them. A sensible compromise, adopted in several cities, is to leave the monument in place but to add an explanation that tells the full story.

What, then, should citizens expect from public memory? Probably not a perfect, unified account, since no society agrees about everything. A more realistic aim is a culture in which different memories are allowed to coexist and to challenge one another, and in which historians remain free to complicate the stories that we find comforting. A nation that can bear to look at its failures is not weakened by that effort. On the contrary, it shows a confidence that no flattering legend can supply.
