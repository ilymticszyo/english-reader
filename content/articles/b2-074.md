Not long ago, if you needed a drill to put up a shelf, you bought one. It would then sit in a cupboard for years, used for perhaps twenty minutes in total. If you needed a car for a day, you rented one from a company. Today, a growing number of people are choosing a different approach: borrowing, renting from neighbours, or sharing. This is sometimes called the sharing economy, and it has changed the way that millions of people travel, stay in other cities, and even find work.

The idea behind it is simple. Many of the things we own spend most of their time unused. A car is parked for about ninety-five percent of its life, and a spare room may stay empty for months. Online platforms connect the owners of these idle resources with people who need them for a short time. The best-known examples are apps for sharing rides and for renting rooms or homes, but the principle extends to tools, clothes, and even parking spaces.

The benefits can be considerable. For consumers, sharing is often cheaper than owning. For owners, it offers extra income from things they already have. There are environmental advantages as well. If one drill serves ten households instead of ten drills, fewer resources are used to make them, and less waste is produced. Some studies suggest that car-sharing schemes have led some people to give up their own vehicles. Sharing can also build community. Neighbours who lend each other tools tend to get to know one another better.

However, the picture is more complicated. Several of the largest companies in this field have grown into huge businesses, driven by profit rather than community spirit. In many cities, short-term home rentals have taken housing away from residents, pushed up rents, and annoyed neighbours. Some cities have introduced limits on the number of nights that a home can be let.

There are also concerns about work. Drivers and delivery workers for sharing platforms are often described as independent, which means that they do not receive the sick pay, holiday pay, or pensions that employees do. Their income can vary, and they may have little power to bargain with the platform. Courts and governments in several countries have begun to ask whether such workers should be classed as employees, and the debate is far from settled.

Trust is a central issue. Strangers must feel safe about using each other's homes and cars. Platforms try to solve this through ratings and reviews, and by checking identities. In most cases, this works surprisingly well, but occasionally, serious problems occur, and responsibility is not always clear.

The most promising forms of sharing may be those that remain close to the original idea: small-scale and local. Libraries of things, where residents borrow tools and equipment for a small fee, and community groups that share food or clothes, are growing in many towns. They save money and resources and bring people together, with little commercial pressure.

Whether sharing will become a lasting part of the economy, or a passing fashion, depends on the rules we choose. At its best, it reminds us that having access to something can be more useful than owning it.
