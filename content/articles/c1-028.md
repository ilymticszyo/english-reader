It is tempting to think that a computer, unlike a human being, has no prejudices. It has no mood, no personal history, and no reason to favour one candidate over another. For this reason, many organisations have turned to algorithms to help them to make decisions that were once left to people: who is invited to a job interview, who receives a loan, which neighbourhoods receive extra police attention, and even how long a defendant should be kept in prison before trial. The promise is a system that is faster, cheaper, and fairer. The reality, as a growing body of evidence shows, is considerably more troubling.

The problem begins with the data. Modern machine-learning systems do not follow rules written by programmers; they discover patterns in large collections of past examples. If those examples reflect a world in which certain groups have been treated unfairly, the system will learn to treat them unfairly, too. A well-known case concerns a recruitment tool built by a large technology company, which was trained on ten years of applications. Because most of the successful candidates had been men, the program learned to give lower scores to CVs that included the word "women's", as in "women's chess club". The engineers tried to correct this particular flaw, but eventually concluded that they could not guarantee that the system would not find other ways to discriminate, and abandoned the project.

Bias can also enter through the choice of what to measure. A system designed to predict which patients need extra medical care might use past health costs as a stand-in for need. But if poorer patients have historically received less treatment, and therefore cost less, the algorithm will conclude that they are healthier than they really are. Researchers who examined such a system in the United States found that it consistently underestimated the needs of Black patients, so that people who were equally ill were less likely to be selected for additional help. Nobody had intended this outcome. The designers had simply used a measure that looked neutral.

A further difficulty is that these systems are often opaque. Many are protected as commercial secrets, and some are so complex that even their creators cannot fully explain why a particular decision was reached. A person who is refused a mortgage may never learn the reason, and so has no means to challenge it. When a human official makes a biased judgment, there is at least the possibility of argument, appeal, and public criticism. An algorithm, with its air of scientific objectivity, can make the same injustice appear natural and inevitable.

What can be done? Technical remedies exist, including better data, regular testing to see whether outcomes differ between groups, and methods that make models easier to interpret. Yet these tools raise a deeper question: what does fairness actually mean? Mathematicians have shown that several reasonable definitions of fairness cannot all be satisfied at the same time, so any system must make a choice, and that choice is a moral and political decision, not a technical one. It should therefore be debated openly, rather than hidden inside code.

Legal frameworks are beginning to respond. Some jurisdictions now require organisations to assess the effects of automated systems before they are used in sensitive areas, and to give people the right to an explanation. These measures are welcome, but they are only a start.

Ultimately, algorithms are mirrors. They show us the society that produced them, including aspects that we would prefer not to see. Used wisely, that reflection could help us to identify and correct old injustices. Used carelessly, it will simply repeat them, with greater speed and an unearned aura of authority.
