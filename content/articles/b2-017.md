Few medical discoveries have saved as many lives as vaccines. Diseases that once killed or disabled millions of children have been wiped out, or nearly wiped out, in many parts of the world. But how does a small injection manage to protect us for years?

To understand vaccines, we first need to know how the body defends itself. When a germ, such as a virus, enters your body, the body's defence system recognises that it does not belong there. Special cells attack it, and others make special weapons that attach themselves to the germ and mark it for destruction. The first time this happens, the process is slow, and you may become seriously ill before the defence is ready. However, the defence system also keeps a memory. If the same germ returns, the body remembers how to fight it and responds much faster, often before you notice any symptoms.

A vaccine takes advantage of this memory. It shows the defence system a harmless version of the germ, or a small piece of it, so that the body can practise without the danger of real disease. Some vaccines contain a weakened form of the germ, others use a dead one, and newer types give the body instructions to make just one small part of it. In every case, the goal is the same: to create memory cells that will recognise the real enemy in the future.

The idea has a long history. In 1796, an English doctor named Edward Jenner noticed that women who worked with cows and had caught a mild disease from them seemed to be protected against smallpox. He tested his theory on a boy, using material from one of the sores, and the boy did not become ill when later exposed to the dangerous disease. The word "vaccine" comes from the Latin word for cow. In 1980, after a worldwide campaign, smallpox was declared completely eliminated.

Vaccines also protect people who cannot be vaccinated themselves, such as newborn babies or patients with weak defences. When enough people in a community are protected, a disease finds it difficult to spread, and even those who are not protected are safer. Doctors call this herd protection.

Like any medicine, vaccines can cause side effects, most of which are mild, such as a sore arm or a short fever. Serious reactions are extremely rare, and they are monitored carefully. Nevertheless, rumours and false information about vaccines spread easily, and in some places, falling vaccination rates have led to the return of diseases that were nearly gone.

The science behind vaccines is a reminder of how remarkable the body is. With a little help, it can learn to defend itself against threats it has never met.
