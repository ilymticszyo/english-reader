Every year, there are about half a million earthquakes around the world. Most are too small to be felt, but a few are devastating. In the last century alone, earthquakes have killed millions of people. They remind us that, despite our technology, we live on a restless planet. Yet the damage depends less on the size of the earthquake than on how well a society has prepared for it.

The cause is the slow movement of the huge plates that make up the surface of the Earth. These plates move only a few centimetres a year, about as fast as fingernails grow, but they push against one another. For decades, the stress builds up along the edges. Eventually, the rock breaks, and the stored energy is released in seconds as waves that shake the ground. Most earthquakes occur along the boundaries between plates, such as around the Pacific Ocean, where several of the world's most active zones are found.

Can earthquakes be predicted? Scientists can say where they are likely to occur and, in broad terms, how often. But no reliable method exists to say exactly when. Researchers have studied animal behaviour, changes in underground water, and small tremors, but none of these signs has proved dependable. Some places now use early warning systems, which detect the first, faster waves and send alerts a few seconds before the strongest shaking arrives. Even a few seconds can be enough to stop trains, open fire station doors, and allow people to take cover.

Because prediction is not possible, preparation is the key. Experience shows that buildings, not the shaking itself, kill most people. Two earthquakes of similar strength can have very different results. In places with strict building rules, such as Japan and Chile, strong earthquakes may cause limited loss of life, while in areas where buildings are poorly made, a smaller one may lead to terrible disaster. Modern techniques include building on flexible bases that absorb movement, adding steel frames, and designing structures that sway rather than break.

Individuals can also prepare. Experts advise people to know the safest places in each room, away from windows and heavy furniture, and to practise the rule of "drop, cover, and hold on". It is wise to fix tall furniture to the wall and to keep a bag with water, food, a torch, and basic medicines. Families should agree on a meeting place and a way to contact each other. After the shaking, people should be prepared for aftershocks, which can continue for days.

Education matters, too. In earthquake-prone countries, children learn what to do through regular drills at school. Communities that practise together tend to act calmly and quickly when the real event occurs.

One of the hardest lessons concerns fairness. Poorer areas often have older, weaker buildings and less access to emergency help, so they suffer more. Reducing this risk requires investment before the disaster, which is less visible than relief afterwards, but far more effective.

We cannot stop the plates from moving. But with knowledge, good building, and preparation, we can make sure that the earth moves without the loss of so many lives.
