I had decided, a little impulsively, to walk the Camino de Santiago, an ancient pilgrimage route that crosses northern Spain. The most popular version begins in the French Pyrenees and ends, about eight hundred kilometres later, in the city of Santiago. People have been walking it for over a thousand years, and I was about to find out why.

The first days were a lesson in humility. My feet developed blisters the size of coins, my legs ached, and I started every morning in the dark, because it was better to walk before the sun became hot. I met a retired teacher who laughed at my new boots and told me that the secret was not strength but patience. "The Camino does not care how fast you are," she said. "Everybody arrives at the same time as their feet."

Gradually, the pattern of the days became comforting. We rose at dawn, walked until early afternoon, and then washed our clothes, ate a simple meal, and rested. The path led through vineyards, over dry plains, and through small stone villages where bells rang from the church towers. At night, we slept in hostels with dozens of other walkers, and the sound of snoring was a small price for the friendly company.

What surprised me most was the people. A walker from Brazil shared his food with me when I ran out. A young Korean couple taught me a few words of their language, and in exchange, I taught them a rude one in English. Strangers asked each other where they were from and why they were walking. Some were religious, others were recovering from an illness or a divorce, and many had no clear reason at all. Nobody seemed to mind, and the conversations were often more honest than those I have with old friends.

Around the third week, something changed. My body had stopped complaining, and my mind, freed from emails and deadlines, became unusually quiet. I noticed small things: the smell of wet grass, the colour of the hills, a farmer who waved without speaking. I realised that I had not looked at my phone for two days.

When I finally entered Santiago and saw the great cathedral, I expected to cry, and I did. But the emotion did not come from the building. It came from the thousands of steps behind me, and from the knowledge that I had done something difficult, slowly, and with the help of many people.

I came home with a lighter rucksack and, I hope, a lighter mind. The road, I discovered, is a good teacher, as long as you are willing to walk it.
