Stand on a busy street, and listen. Engines, horns, building work, sirens, conversations, and the distant hum of air conditioning blend into a constant background that city residents hardly notice. Yet researchers increasingly believe that this ever-present noise is not harmless. Next to air pollution, it may be one of the most important environmental threats to health in urban areas.

Sound becomes noise when it is unwanted, and its effects depend on both its loudness and its type. Sounds above about eighty-five decibels, such as heavy traffic close by, can damage hearing over time. But harm begins at much lower levels. Even moderate noise, particularly at night, can disturb sleep without waking the sleeper fully, and the body continues to react by raising the heart rate and releasing stress hormones.

Over years, the effects accumulate. The World Health Organization has estimated that, in Western Europe alone, traffic noise causes the loss of more than a million healthy years of life every year, mainly through heart disease, sleep disorders, and stress. Studies link long exposure to noise with high blood pressure, heart attacks, and anxiety. Children are especially affected. Those who attend schools under busy flight paths have been found to have lower reading scores, perhaps because noise interferes with concentration and hearing.

The sources of city noise are varied. Road traffic is the largest, followed by railways and aircraft. Building sites, nightlife, and delivery vehicles add to the din, as do leaf blowers and loud music. Even the sound of quiet electric vehicles, when it is lacking, creates a different problem, since pedestrians cannot hear them coming, so many are now required to produce a warning sound at low speeds.

Unfortunately, noise is not distributed fairly. People with low incomes are often forced to live near main roads, railways, and airports, where housing is cheaper. The wealthy can afford quieter streets and better windows. Noise thus adds to existing inequalities in health.

What can be done? The most effective approach is to reduce noise at its source. Quieter road surfaces, lower speed limits, and electric vehicles all cut noise from traffic. Cities can restrict heavy lorries at night, ban the noisiest motorcycles, and install noise barriers beside major roads. Planners can place buildings so that bedrooms face away from busy streets, and design windows that block sound while allowing fresh air. Parks and green spaces help by providing a place to retreat, and trees can reduce noise a little, although their psychological effect may be greater than their physical one.

Individuals can help by using headphones with care, since loud music can damage hearing, and by choosing quiet places to rest. Some people find that calming natural sounds, such as running water, help to mask the din.

Perhaps the most important step is to treat noise as a real health matter, not merely an annoyance. A quieter city is not a luxury. It is part of a healthier one.
