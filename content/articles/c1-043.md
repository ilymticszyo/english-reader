When the first modern state pension scheme was introduced in Germany in the 1880s, the age at which workers qualified was seventy, at a time when the average person did not live much beyond forty-five. The system, in other words, was designed to support a small minority who survived to old age. Today, in many countries, the majority of people can expect to live into their eighties, and some demographers believe that more than half of the children born in wealthy countries in recent years may live to a hundred. The institution of retirement, built for a very different world, is under strain, and societies are being forced to rethink what the later stages of life should look like.
 
The financial dimension is the most obvious. Most state pensions operate on a "pay as you go" basis, in which the contributions of current workers fund the benefits of current retirees. This works when there are many workers for each pensioner, but as birth rates fall and life expectancy rises, the ratio shrinks. In several European countries, there are now fewer than three people of working age for every person over sixty-five, and the figure is expected to approach two within a few decades. Governments have responded by raising the age of eligibility, reducing the generosity of benefits, or encouraging people to save privately, each of which is politically painful.
 
Raising the retirement age is the simplest remedy, but it is not as straightforward as it appears. Life expectancy has increased unevenly: the wealthy live considerably longer than the poor, and a worker in a physically demanding occupation may be in poor health long before he reaches an official pension age. A uniform increase therefore asks the most from those who have the least, which many people regard as unfair. There is also the question of whether older people can find employment. Surveys indicate that employers remain reluctant to hire people in their sixties, and workers who lose their jobs late in their careers often struggle to find new ones.
 
Yet focusing exclusively on cost may obscure a more hopeful point. A longer life is, after all, an achievement, and the added years are often healthy and active ones. The traditional pattern of three stages, education in youth, work in middle age, and rest in old age, no longer suits the way that many people wish to live. Some argue for a more flexible model, in which periods of work, study, and leisure are interspersed throughout life. A person might take a sabbatical to retrain at forty, work part-time in their sixties, and continue to contribute in some form into their seventies. Such arrangements might help to relieve the pressure on young parents, who are at present squeezed between careers and children, and to avoid the abrupt loss of purpose that many people experience when they stop working altogether.
 
Older people, moreover, are a valuable resource, whose experience is too often wasted. Many already provide unpaid childcare, volunteer in their communities, and care for even older relatives, contributions that are rarely counted in economic statistics. Some companies have begun to value the skills of mature workers, who tend to have lower turnover and considerable knowledge, and to create mixed-age teams that benefit from both fresh ideas and long experience.
 
Nevertheless, a more flexible approach demands changes that are hard to deliver. Workplaces must offer part-time posts and retraining; health systems must concentrate on keeping people well rather than merely treating illness; cities must provide housing and transport that suit an older population; and a culture accustomed to equating usefulness with youth must learn to value age. Loneliness, which affects many older people, will also need attention.
 
Whether the coming generations of older citizens will be a burden or a blessing will depend largely on the choices that societies make today. The demographic change itself is not a disaster. It is a challenge to our imagination, and it invites us to design a society in which a long life is truly a good one.
