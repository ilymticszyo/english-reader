For most of the last century, owning a car has been seen as a sign of success and freedom. Yet a growing number of city dwellers are choosing to live without one. Some do it to save money, some to protect the environment, and others because they find that a car is simply more trouble than it is worth.
 
The financial argument is strong. A car costs far more than the price on the showroom floor. Owners must pay for insurance, fuel, repairs, parking, and taxes, and the value of the car falls every year. Studies in several countries suggest that the total cost can reach thousands of pounds annually, even for a small vehicle. For a household in a city with good public transport, that money may be better spent elsewhere.
 
There are also environmental reasons. Road transport is a major source of pollution and carbon emissions, and cars are parked and unused most of the time, taking up valuable space. In crowded cities, many people feel that streets would be safer and more pleasant if there were fewer vehicles.
 
Living without a car does demand some planning. People who do it successfully often rely on a combination of options. They walk or cycle for short trips, use buses and trains for longer ones, and take a taxi or hire a car for special occasions. Many cities now have shared bicycles and car clubs, which provide access to a vehicle for a few hours when it is needed. Online shopping and home delivery have also reduced the need for trips to the supermarket.
 
The experience can bring unexpected benefits. People who walk and cycle every day get regular exercise without having to visit a gym. They notice their neighbourhood more, meet neighbours, and often feel less stressed than when sitting in traffic. Some say that they have learned to choose a home closer to work, which saves even more time.
 
Of course, the choice is not possible for everyone. In rural areas, distances are long and public transport may be rare. People who care for young children, have a disability, or work irregular hours may find a car essential. Moving heavy goods or visiting relatives in distant places can also be hard without one.
 
That is why urban design matters. A person can give up a car only if shops, schools, and workplaces are within reach, and if buses and trains are frequent and reliable. When cities invest in these things, the number of people who choose to live without a car grows naturally.
 
For many, the decision is not about giving something up. It is about spending time and money on things that matter more, and moving through the world at a more human speed.
