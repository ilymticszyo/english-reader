The first time I travelled alone, I was twenty-six, and I was terrified. I had booked a two-week trip to a country where I did not speak the language, and as the plane took off, I seriously considered asking the crew to let me off. Friends had warned me about loneliness, and my mother had called me every day for a week to remind me to be careful. Looking back, it was one of the best decisions I have ever made.

The first days were not easy. I felt awkward eating alone in restaurants, and I noticed that every other table seemed to be full of happy groups. I spent my first evening in my room, eating crackers and wondering why I had come. But the next morning, the sun was shining, and I decided to walk without a plan. Within an hour, I was lost, and, to my surprise, I did not mind. I found a tiny bakery, where an old woman gave me a warm pastry and, with gestures, explained how to get back to my hotel.

That was the lesson of the trip. When you are alone, you are far more open to the people around you. In a group, we usually talk to our companions. On my own, I had conversations with a shopkeeper about his family, with a student who offered to show me his favourite park, and with a couple at a train station who invited me to share their lunch. None of these things would have happened if I had been with a friend.

Travelling alone also means that every decision is yours. If I wanted to spend three hours in a museum, I could. If I preferred to sit by a river and read, nobody complained. I woke up when I wanted and changed my plans at the last minute. After years of compromising, this freedom was quite intoxicating.

There were difficulties as well. Once, I missed a train and had to find a room late at night, and I learned to ask for help instead of feeling embarrassed. I also learned to take sensible precautions: telling someone my plans, keeping copies of my documents, and avoiding unlit streets late at night. These habits never spoiled my enjoyment, and they made me feel more in control.

By the end of the second week, I had changed. I felt more confident, and I trusted myself in a way that I had not done before. If I could find my way in a strange city, make friends in a different language, and solve my own problems, I felt that I could handle many other things at home.

I have since travelled alone many times. I still enjoy company, but I no longer fear my own. And I always recommend that those who are unsure should try, even for a weekend. The worst that can happen is that you discover how much you miss your friends. The best is that you discover yourself.
