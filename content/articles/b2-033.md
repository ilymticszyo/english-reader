Not long ago, a trip to an unfamiliar place began with a map. You studied the roads in advance, folded the paper into your pocket, and, if you were unlucky, argued with your passenger about where to turn. Today, almost nobody does this. A voice from the phone says "turn left in two hundred metres", and we obey. It is convenient, certainly. But is something being lost?

The advantages of navigation apps are obvious. They know about traffic jams and road works, they can find the fastest route in seconds, and they rarely make a mistake. For people who travel for work, or who have to drive in places they do not know, they save time and reduce stress. They also help people with a poor sense of direction, and people who feel nervous about travelling alone, to move around with more confidence.

However, researchers have begun to ask what happens to our brains when we follow instructions without thinking. A part of the brain called the hippocampus is used to build mental maps of the places we know. A well-known study of London taxi drivers, who must memorise thousands of streets, found that this part of the brain was larger than average. Other studies suggest that people who rely on GPS develop a weaker memory for routes. When they were asked to find the way again without the device, they often failed, even after they had been to the same place several times.

The reason seems to be attention. When we follow a voice, we do not need to notice our surroundings, so we do not pay attention to buildings, signs, or the shape of the streets. We arrive, but we have not really learned where we are. A person who reads a map must decide what is important, think about where north is, and link the picture on the paper with the real world. This effort is what builds a lasting memory.

There is also a more personal cost. Many people who have used only a phone say that they feel less connected to the places they live in. They know how to move from one point to another, but they cannot explain how the parts of their city fit together. Getting lost, which was once a nuisance, was also a way to discover things, such as a quiet street, a small shop, or a beautiful view that no app would suggest.

None of this means that we should throw away our phones. A sensible approach is to use both. Look at the map before you set out. Try to guess the route and then check it. Turn off the voice from time to time and see what you notice.

Technology can take us anywhere. But if we want to know where we are, we may still have to use our own minds.
