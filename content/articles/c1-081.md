Few everyday choices raise as many moral questions as the decision about what to eat. For most of human history, meat was a prized and occasional food, and the idea that eating it might be wrong would have seemed absurd to most people. Today, however, the ethics of meat consumption is the subject of serious public debate. The number of people who avoid meat for moral reasons is growing, and philosophers, scientists, and farmers are being drawn into an argument about what we owe to animals, to the planet, and to each other.
 
The most direct objection concerns animal suffering. Modern farming has become highly intensive, and the great majority of the animals raised for food in wealthy countries live in large industrial units. Chickens may spend their short lives in crowded sheds, pigs in pens with little room to move, and cattle in feedlots. Scientific research has confirmed that many animals, including fish, can feel pain and distress, and some show signs of complex emotions. The philosopher Peter Singer argued in the 1970s that the capacity to suffer, rather than intelligence or species, is what gives a being moral standing. If a pig can suffer as much as a dog, he argued, it is hard to defend treating one as a pet and the other as a product.
 
A second argument concerns the environment. Livestock farming uses vast areas of land, contributes substantially to greenhouse gas emissions, and is a major cause of deforestation and water pollution. Studies suggest that if everyone ate a plant-based diet, the land needed for agriculture could be reduced by more than half. For those who are concerned about climate change, reducing meat intake is one of the few individual actions with a significant effect.
 
Defenders of meat-eating offer several replies. Some point out that humans have evolved as omnivores, and that eating animals is natural. Critics respond that what is natural is not necessarily right, and that most of us are not obliged by hunger to eat meat, since healthy diets without it are available to those who can afford them. Others argue that well-managed farming can be humane, with animals living outdoors and being slaughtered quickly, and that some land, such as steep mountain pasture, is useless for crops but suitable for grazing. Many cultures also place enormous value on traditional dishes, and to ask people to abandon them can feel like an attack on identity.
 
There are also questions of livelihood and justice. Millions of people, from small farmers to herders, depend on animals for their income, and in poorer regions, meat and milk may be an important source of nutrition for children. A sudden change would harm those who are least responsible for the problem. It is not obvious that a moral rule that suits a wealthy city dweller should be applied to a family living on a dry plain.
 
Between the extremes lies a range of positions. Some people have chosen to eat less meat, but of higher quality, from farms that treat animals well. Others avoid certain products, such as veal or foie gras, which they find especially troubling. Technology may change the debate. Meat grown from animal cells in a laboratory, and plant products designed to taste like meat, promise a way to satisfy the appetite without the harm, although these remain expensive and controversial.
 
What matters in the end may be the willingness to think about the question, rather than to follow habit. Most of us rarely consider where our food comes from, and the distance between farm and table has made it easy to avoid doing so. Whatever conclusion we reach, asking whether our habits are consistent with our values is a reasonable starting point.
