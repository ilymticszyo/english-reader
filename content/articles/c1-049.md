Announcements about quantum computers tend to follow a familiar pattern. A company declares that it has built a machine that can solve in minutes a problem that would take the fastest conventional supercomputer thousands of years; newspapers report that a new era of computing has arrived; and, a few weeks later, experts point out that the problem had been chosen to suit the machine and has no practical use. Such episodes illustrate both the real excitement and the inflated expectations that surround a technology that is still, for the most part, a laboratory experiment.
 
To understand why researchers are enthusiastic, one has to appreciate how quantum computers differ from ordinary ones. A conventional computer stores information in "bits", each of which is either zero or one. A quantum computer uses "qubits", which, according to the laws of quantum physics, can exist in a combination of both states at once. Furthermore, qubits can be linked in a way known as entanglement, so that the state of one depends on that of another, however far apart they may be. By manipulating these properties, a quantum algorithm can, for certain types of problem, explore an immense number of possibilities in a manner that has no counterpart in classical machines.
 
It is crucial to understand, however, that a quantum computer is not simply a faster version of a laptop. It does not perform every calculation more quickly, and a popular description, that it tries every possible answer at the same time, is misleading. Its advantage appears only for a small number of specific tasks, in which the mathematics can be arranged so that wrong answers cancel each other out and the right one remains. Two applications attract particular attention. The first is the simulation of molecules and materials, a problem that quantum mechanics itself makes extremely hard for conventional machines, and one that could accelerate the discovery of new medicines, batteries, and fertilisers. The second is the breaking of widely used encryption systems, which rely on the difficulty of dividing very large numbers into their prime factors, something a sufficiently powerful quantum computer could do far more efficiently.
 
The threat to encryption is the reason that governments and security agencies are paying close attention. Although no machine capable of breaking current codes exists, and experts disagree about when, or whether, one will be built, information that is encrypted today may be stored by adversaries and decoded in the future. Cryptographers have therefore begun to design new methods that are believed to be secure even against quantum attack, and official bodies have started to select standards for adoption.
 
The obstacles to building a useful machine are formidable. Qubits are extremely fragile: the slightest vibration, heat, or stray radiation can destroy their delicate state, causing errors. Many current designs must be cooled to temperatures colder than outer space, and even then, they make mistakes so frequently that the machines cannot carry out long calculations. The solution, in principle, is error correction, in which many imperfect physical qubits are combined to form a single reliable one. But estimates suggest that a truly useful computer might need thousands, or even millions, of physical qubits for each dependable unit, whereas today's best machines contain only hundreds or a few thousand.
 
For this reason, many scientists regard the current period as an early stage, comparable to the decades of electronic computing before the invention of the transistor. Progress is real, and the sums invested by governments and companies are very large. Yet optimism should be tempered by recognition that timelines have repeatedly slipped, and that commercial pressure tends to encourage exaggerated claims.
 
How should the rest of us react? With curiosity and patience, rather than alarm or credulity. When a headline describes a quantum breakthrough, it is worth asking what problem was solved, whether it has any practical value, and what a conventional computer could do if given the same attention. Quantum computing may well transform parts of science and security. But it will not do so tomorrow, and it will not replace the phone in your pocket.
