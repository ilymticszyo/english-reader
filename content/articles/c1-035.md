Consider what it takes to keep a household running. Meals must be planned and cooked, clothes washed, appointments booked, children comforted and taken to school, elderly parents visited, and an endless list of small tasks remembered and completed. None of this appears in national statistics as productive activity, and almost none of it is paid. Yet if it were suddenly to stop, society would quickly collapse. Economists and sociologists have a name for this vast, mostly invisible activity: unpaid care work.

The scale of it is enormous. According to estimates by international organisations, unpaid care accounts for several billion hours of work every day around the world, and if it were assigned a monetary value, it would represent a significant share of the output of most economies, in some estimates comparable to the largest industrial sectors. The burden is also unevenly distributed. In virtually every country for which data exist, women perform a far greater portion of this work than men, spending on average more than twice as many hours on it, and in some regions as much as five times. This imbalance persists even among couples in which both partners have full-time jobs, a pattern that sociologists have named "the second shift".

The consequences for women are considerable. Time spent caring is time not spent earning, and many women reduce their hours, interrupt their careers, or turn down promotions in order to meet family responsibilities. The effects accumulate over a lifetime: lower wages, smaller pensions, and greater vulnerability to poverty in old age. Researchers have identified this "motherhood penalty" in many countries, and have found that fathers, by contrast, often experience a small rise in earnings after having children. The gap in pay between men and women, which is often attributed to discrimination, owes a significant part of its persistence to this unequal division of care.

Paid care work, meanwhile, suffers from similar neglect. Nurses, nursery staff, and home helpers carry out tasks that are essential, demanding, and emotionally exhausting, but they are generally among the lowest-paid workers in the economy. Many are women, and a substantial number are migrants who leave their own children and parents behind to look after those of other families. The pandemic made the importance of these workers undeniable, but the public gratitude expressed at the time has seldom been followed by lasting improvements in pay or conditions.

Looking ahead, the problem is likely to grow. Populations are ageing in most wealthy countries, and in some developing ones, and the number of people who need long-term care is rising, while families are smaller and often more scattered than before. Informal care by relatives, which has traditionally filled the gap, cannot be expected to bear the entire load.

Possible responses have been tried in various forms. Several Nordic countries provide extensive and subsidised childcare, together with parental leave that is reserved for fathers on a "use it or lose it" basis; this has measurably increased the amount of time that men spend with their children. Other governments give pension credits to people who leave work to care for relatives, or pay allowances to those who look after disabled family members. Employers, for their part, can offer flexible hours and make it easier for staff of any gender to take time off.

At a deeper level, what is required is a change in how we value care. As long as it is regarded as a private matter, performed out of love and therefore unworthy of payment or statistical recognition, its costs will continue to fall on a few people who are rarely thanked. A society that wishes to be fair must begin by acknowledging that caring is work, and that all of us, at some stage of our lives, depend on it.
