Several generations ago, allergies were relatively rare. Today, in many wealthy countries, up to a third of children suffer from one, whether to flowers, food, animals, or dust. Some food reactions can be life-threatening, and they have increased sharply. What has changed, and can we do anything about it?

The body protects us by recognising and attacking harmful germs. An allergy occurs when this defence makes a mistake. It treats a harmless substance, such as a peanut, as a dangerous enemy and reacts with great force. The result may be a rash, sneezing, difficulty in breathing, or, in the worst cases, a severe reaction that needs immediate medical attention.

Why has this become more frequent? One of the oldest explanations is the "hygiene idea". It was proposed in 1989 by a British doctor who noticed that children from large families, who caught more infections from their brothers and sisters, were less likely to have hay fever. His suggestion was that in clean modern homes, with fewer infections and more medicine, the body has less to do in early life and may begin to react to harmless things instead.

The idea has since changed. Scientists now think that the key may not be dirt in itself, but contact with a rich variety of microorganisms. Studies show that children who grow up on farms, with animals, have much lower rates of allergy than children in cities. Living with a dog in early life also seems to offer some protection. In contrast, people who live in crowded cities and spend most of their time indoors may meet fewer of these small living things.

Other explanations have also been proposed. Air pollution can irritate the lungs. Changes in diet, a lack of sunlight, and even the way that babies are born have all been investigated. No single cause has been proved, and it is likely that several factors work together.

Advice on peanuts has changed dramatically. For years, doctors told parents to avoid giving peanuts to babies in order to prevent allergy. Then, in 2015, a large study found the opposite: babies at high risk who ate peanut products regularly from early in life were far less likely to develop an allergy. Many doctors now recommend introducing common foods early, in a safe way.

Treatment is also improving. Besides medicines that reduce symptoms, doctors can sometimes give very small, increasing amounts of the food over many months, which helps the body to become used to it.

For now, the most useful advice is to avoid extremes. A child does not need to live in a dirty house, but it is likely that playing outside, meeting animals, and eating a varied diet are good for the immune system, as well as for the rest of the body.
