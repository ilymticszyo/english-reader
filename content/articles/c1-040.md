Today, a jar of pepper costs little and sits on every kitchen table, and we rarely think about its origins. Yet for much of recorded history, spices such as pepper, cinnamon, nutmeg, and cloves were among the most valuable commodities on Earth, and the pursuit of them reshaped the world. Empires rose and fell over them, sailors risked their lives for them, and the search for a sea route to the places where they grew led, indirectly, to the discovery of the Americas.

Spices were prized in ancient and medieval Europe for several reasons. They flavoured food, naturally, but they were also used as medicines, perfumes, and ingredients in religious ceremonies, and a popular belief held that they could protect against disease. It is often said that they were also used to hide the taste of meat that had begun to go bad, although historians now doubt that this was a major reason, since the rich, who bought most of the spices, could usually afford fresh food. Above all, they were symbols of wealth. Most were produced in a small number of tropical regions, particularly India and the islands of what is now Indonesia, and the long and dangerous journey to Europe made them extremely expensive. The Moluccan islands, known as the Spice Islands, were the only source of cloves and nutmeg, and their remoteness added to their mystery.

For centuries, the trade was carried along overland and maritime routes, passing through the hands of many intermediaries. Arab and Indian merchants carried the goods across the Indian Ocean, caravans took them through the deserts of the Middle East, and Italian cities such as Venice grew rich by distributing them in Europe. Each intermediary added a profit, so that by the time a peppercorn reached a European market, its price had risen many times. Rumours circulated that the merchants deliberately encouraged mystery about the origin of the goods, inventing tales of monsters and poisonous snakes to discourage competitors.

In the fifteenth century, European rulers became determined to bypass these middlemen. The Portuguese, who had pioneered the exploration of the African coast, finally succeeded in 1498, when Vasco da Gama reached India by sailing around the southern tip of Africa. The Spanish pursued a different idea, which was to reach Asia by sailing west, and it was in this search that Columbus arrived in the Caribbean. The Portuguese soon established fortified trading posts along the coasts of the Indian Ocean, and used their naval strength to force local traders to buy licences.

They did not hold the advantage for long. In the seventeenth century, the Dutch, who had formed a powerful trading company, drove the Portuguese from many of their positions and took control of the production of nutmeg and cloves. The methods employed were often brutal. On a small island where nutmeg was grown, the company killed or enslaved most of the inhabitants, and, to keep prices high, it destroyed trees on other islands. The British and the French, who were also competing, eventually obtained plants and seedlings and established plantations elsewhere, which broke the monopoly. As supply increased, prices fell, and spices ceased to be luxuries.

The legacy of the spice trade is complex. On one hand, it stimulated the development of navigation, shipbuilding, banking, and the early forms of the joint-stock company, and it brought new flavours to the kitchens of the world. On the other, it was bound up with violence, exploitation, and the beginnings of European colonial rule in Asia, and the wealth that it created paid for many of the grand buildings and collections that we still admire in European cities. Every modern meal that includes a pinch of pepper is a reminder of how far and how painfully the world has been connected by what people were willing to pay for taste.
