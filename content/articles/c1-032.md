For most of human history, cities were built for people on foot. Streets were narrow because there was nothing wider than a cart to accommodate, and everything that a person needed, such as the market, the workshop, and the church, lay within a short walk. The arrival of the motor car in the twentieth century changed that pattern fundamentally. Planners widened roads, separated housing from workplaces, and constructed vast areas of parking, on the assumption that the future belonged to the driver. Several generations later, many of those cities are discovering that the assumption had hidden costs, and a growing movement is attempting to put pedestrians back at the centre of urban design.

The most obvious cost is physical. People who live in neighbourhoods where it is impractical to walk tend to be less active, and public health researchers have linked car-dependent suburbs to higher rates of obesity, heart disease, and diabetes. Then there is the matter of safety. Each year, more than a million people die in road accidents worldwide, and pedestrians and cyclists make up a large share of the victims. The danger is not distributed equally: poorer districts often have fewer crossings and faster traffic, so that those with the least choice about how to travel face the greatest risk.

There are economic and social costs, too. A space given to cars is a space taken from other uses. In many city centres, up to a third of the land is devoted to roads and parking, an extraordinary amount for a resource that is so scarce and valuable. Studies of shopping streets that have been closed to traffic have often found that, contrary to the fears of local traders, business improved, since pedestrians tend to visit more often and to linger. Moreover, a street that is pleasant to walk along becomes a place where neighbours meet, children play, and strangers exchange a few words. The urban theorist Jane Jacobs, writing in 1961, argued that the "eyes on the street" provided by people going about their daily lives were the best protection against crime.

What, then, makes a city walkable? Planners usually emphasise a combination of features. The first is proximity: schools, shops, and services should be close enough to reach on foot, which implies a mixture of uses in each neighbourhood rather than rigid separation. The second is a sense of safety, which requires slow traffic, well-marked crossings, and adequate lighting. The third is comfort and interest. Pavements need to be wide and shaded, with benches to rest on, and buildings should have doors and windows facing the street instead of blank walls. Tellingly, people will walk much further along an interesting route than along a dull one.

A number of cities have achieved striking results. Paris has announced plans to remove tens of thousands of parking spaces and to ensure that every resident can reach basic services within fifteen minutes of their home. Barcelona has created "superblocks", clusters of streets from which through traffic is excluded, leaving the space for gardens and playgrounds. Even in car-oriented countries, some suburbs are being redesigned with local centres and safer routes to school.

These changes are rarely easy. Drivers resent the loss of convenience, shopkeepers fear for their income, and residents worry that improving a neighbourhood will raise rents. Such objections need to be taken seriously, and successful projects typically begin with experiments that can be reversed, and with extensive consultation.

Nevertheless, the direction of change seems clear. A city designed for walking is quieter, cleaner, healthier, and more sociable, and it offers a quality of daily life that no highway can provide. The lesson of the last century is not that cars are evil, but that a city built entirely around them is a poor place for the people who live there.
