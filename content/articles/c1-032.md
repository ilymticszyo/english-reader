Most of us now travel through cities with a phone in our hands. A blue dot shows where we are, a line shows where to go, and a voice tells us when to turn. The technology is remarkable, and it has removed a great deal of anxiety from unfamiliar places. Yet a growing number of writers and psychologists suggest that something has been lost. If we never take a wrong turn, they argue, we also never stumble upon the unexpected, and we may be slowly losing a skill that human beings have relied on for thousands of years.
 
The idea of purposeless walking has a distinguished history. In nineteenth-century Paris, the poet Charles Baudelaire described the figure of the flâneur, a person who strolls through the city simply to observe it, drifting among crowds, arcades, and cafés with no destination in mind. A century later, a group of French thinkers called the Situationists proposed the dérive, or "drift", in which people would let the mood of the streets lead them, ignoring the usual reasons for moving about. Their aim was to see familiar places in a new way, and to resist a city organised entirely around work and shopping. More recently, the writer Rebecca Solnit, in her book on getting lost, has argued that the willingness to be lost is a form of openness. "Never to get lost," she suggests, "is not to live."
 
There is also scientific evidence that finding our own way is good for the mind. In a well-known study published in 2000, researchers at University College London scanned the brains of licensed taxi drivers, who must memorise the layout of some twenty-five thousand streets. They found that the rear part of the hippocampus, a region of the brain involved in memory and navigation, was larger in the drivers than in other people, and that it was larger in those who had been driving for longer. Later studies suggest that people who rely heavily on satellite navigation tend to remember routes less well, and to show less activity in the same brain region. The conclusion is not that technology damages the brain, but that skills which are not used can fade.
 
Wandering has other benefits that are harder to measure. Many writers and artists describe walking as a way of thinking. Ideas that resist all effort at a desk often arrive on a long walk, and experiments have shown that people produce more creative ideas while walking than while sitting. Without a goal, the mind is free to make unexpected connections. A walk without a plan also encourages small encounters: the bookshop in a side street, the conversation with a stranger, the courtyard that no guidebook mentions. These chance discoveries are rarely found by following a recommended route.
 
It would be wrong, however, to treat maps and phones as the enemy. They make travel safer and more accessible, particularly for people who are anxious, disabled, or travelling alone, and they save time that most of us do not have in abundance. The solution is not to abandon them but to use them with care. Some travellers now turn off their phones for an afternoon, or set a simple rule such as taking every third street on the left. Others use the map to find their way home, after the walk has taken them somewhere new.
 
Wandering is easier in some places than in others. Old towns with winding streets invite exploration, while districts designed for cars can be hostile to anyone on foot. A city that wishes to encourage this kind of discovery needs pavements, safe crossings, and interesting things to find. It also requires that its residents feel safe, and not all do. The freedom to wander aimlessly has never been shared equally.
 
For most of us, the practical lesson is modest. Once in a while, leave early, put the phone in a pocket, and walk without deciding where to go. You may arrive late, and you may feel uneasy for the first few minutes. But you will almost certainly see the place, and perhaps yourself, a little differently.
