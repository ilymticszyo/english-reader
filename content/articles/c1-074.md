We live in an age of abundant text. Messages, news alerts, articles, and posts arrive all day, and most of us read more words than any previous generation. Yet many people report that they find it harder than before to read a whole book. Their attention drifts, they check their phones, and they skim rather than absorb. The fear that we are losing the ability to read deeply has produced a growing movement that advocates something that once needed no defence: reading slowly.
 
There is evidence that the way we read is changing. Researchers who study reading on screens have found that people tend to scan texts in a pattern, reading the first lines and then jumping down the page, picking out keywords rather than following an argument. This is a sensible response to the flood of information, and it is useful for finding facts. But it comes at a cost. Studies suggest that readers who skim understand less of complex material and remember less of what they have read, and that the habit of skimming, once established, can carry over into situations where careful reading is needed.
 
Deep reading, by contrast, is a demanding activity. It involves following a sustained argument or story, holding details in mind, making connections, and pausing to reflect. Brain research shows that it engages many areas, including those that handle language, imagery, and emotion. Reading fiction, in particular, appears to strengthen empathy, as readers imagine the thoughts and feelings of characters. Some scholars believe that the slow, quiet absorption in a book has played an essential part in the development of independent thought, and that its decline could affect the quality of public discussion.
 
It is easy, though, to romanticise the past. People have always worried that new media would damage the mind. Socrates complained that writing would weaken memory, and in the eighteenth century, critics warned that novels would corrupt young readers and spoil their taste for serious literature. Many of these fears proved exaggerated. Moreover, reading on a screen is not inherently worse: what matters is how it is done. Digital reading offers advantages, including access to vast libraries, searchable text, and the ability to adjust the size of print, which is a real benefit for those with poor eyesight.
 
The practical question, then, is how to protect deep reading in an environment designed to interrupt it. Some suggestions are simple. Set aside a regular time, even twenty minutes, and put the phone in another room. Choose a physical book or a device without notifications. Read actively, by underlining, writing notes in the margin, or summarising a chapter afterwards. Allow yourself to stop reading books that do not interest you, but give the difficult ones a fair chance, because the pleasure often arrives only after the first effort.
 
Schools face an especially important task. Children who learn to read mainly on screens, surrounded by distractions, may not develop the patience that complex texts require. Teachers increasingly stress the importance of reading aloud, of shared books, and of time for silent reading, while also teaching students how to evaluate online sources. The aim is not to reject technology but to become "bilingual", able to read quickly when speed is useful and slowly when depth is needed.
 
Even a few pages a day, read without hurry, can rebuild the habit, and many readers find that their concentration improves in other parts of life as well. Reading slowly is, in the end, a form of respect, both for the writer and for oneself. It means giving full attention to another person's thoughts, and allowing them time to change one's own. In a world that rewards speed, there is value in the deliberate pleasure of a good book, read at the pace of thought.
