Every year, rich individuals and foundations give away enormous sums of money. Some of it builds hospitals, funds universities, and supports research into disease; some of it pays for museums, concert halls, and parks. The practice has a long history. In the late nineteenth century, a Scottish-born steel magnate, Andrew Carnegie, wrote that it was a duty of the rich to distribute their surplus wealth for the public good, and he financed thousands of public libraries. Today, a new generation of technology billionaires has made similar promises. Yet generosity of this kind has always had its critics, and the argument over whether philanthropy is a blessing or a problem has become more heated.
 
Supporters begin with the obvious point that the money does good. Philanthropic funds have helped to eradicate diseases, such as smallpox and polio, to develop vaccines, and to support the agricultural research that fed millions in the twentieth century. Private donors can take risks that governments cannot. They are not constrained by election cycles, they can fund unpopular or untested ideas, and they can act quickly in an emergency. A government that must answer to voters will rarely finance a project whose benefits may appear only after decades, but a patient foundation may be able to do so.
 
A newer approach, known as "effective altruism", has given the debate a new character. Its followers argue that giving should be guided by evidence, and that donors should ask which gifts do the most good per pound or dollar. Studies suggest that some charities are hundreds of times more effective than others, so that a small donation to provide mosquito nets or treat parasitic infections can save a life at a cost that would be hard to match in a wealthy country. For supporters, the moral conclusion is that people in rich nations should give much more than they do, and that they should choose carefully where to give it.
 
The critics offer several objections. The first concerns democracy. When a billionaire decides how to spend a fortune on education or health policy, a private individual is effectively setting priorities that ought to be decided by the public. Nobody voted for these decisions, and the donors are accountable to no one. The second objection is that philanthropy may distract attention from the causes of inequality. Giving can create a flattering image of the donor while leaving untouched the systems of taxation and employment that produced the fortune in the first place. Some critics argue that the money would be better collected through taxes and allocated by elected representatives.
 
A third criticism is that donors tend to favour projects that are visible and measurable, such as buildings and new programmes, over the unglamorous, long-term work of running institutions and paying staff. Charities often complain that donors give money for a particular purpose and then withdraw, leaving the organisation with costs it cannot meet. Local communities, too, may feel that their needs are defined by distant donors with little understanding of their circumstances. In some well-known cases, projects imposed from outside have failed because they ignored what the people concerned actually wanted.
 
Defenders reply that these objections do not make the donations worthless. The money exists, and the alternative to giving it away is not necessarily higher taxation but more private consumption. They also point out that the best philanthropists have learned to listen, to give flexible support, and to trust local organisations. A growing movement favours "participatory" giving, in which communities help to decide how funds are used, and some wealthy donors have chosen to give away their entire fortune within their lifetimes in order to avoid creating dynasties of influence.
 
The honest conclusion may be that philanthropy is neither a substitute for public policy nor a threat to it, but a supplement whose quality depends on how it is done. Generosity should be welcomed, but it should not excuse injustice in the way wealth is made and shared, and the public has the right to ask hard questions about the power that comes with giving.
 
In a fair society, perhaps, the need for large-scale charity would be smaller. Until then, the argument is not about whether the rich should give, but about how, to whom, and on whose terms.
