Every language that is spoken by a large number of people exists in many varieties. Speakers from different regions pronounce the same words in different ways, use different vocabulary, and sometimes construct their sentences differently. Linguists use the term "dialect" for a variety that has its own grammar and words, and "accent" for the way it is pronounced. Yet in everyday conversation, these differences are rarely treated as neutral. Almost everywhere, certain ways of speaking are regarded as more correct, more educated, or more attractive than others, and the judgement has less to do with language than with the people who speak it.

From the point of view of linguistics, no dialect is inherently better than another. Each follows consistent rules that speakers learn naturally as children. The sentence "I ain't got none", for example, would be marked as an error in a school essay, but it follows a logical pattern, just as double negatives do in many other languages, including those that are held in high prestige. The reason that a particular form is regarded as standard is usually historical. In many countries, it was the speech of the capital city, the royal court, or the wealthy classes, and it was spread through schools, newspapers, and later through broadcasting. As the saying goes, a language is a dialect with an army and a navy.

Because accent is so strongly associated with social background, it often becomes a tool of discrimination. In one classic study carried out in New York in 1962, a linguist visited three department stores, one expensive, one middle-ranking, and one cheap, and asked employees a question whose answer was "the fourth floor". He recorded how often they pronounced the letter "r" in each word. The more upmarket the store, the more frequently the staff used the prestigious pronunciation, and they also used it more often when they were asked to repeat themselves carefully. The experiment suggested that speakers adjust their speech to signal status, often without realising it.

Later research has documented the real consequences. Studies of job applications have shown that candidates with non-standard accents are sometimes judged to be less intelligent or less competent, even when the content of their answers is identical. Landlords, according to some field experiments, are less likely to answer a telephone enquiry from someone whose speech suggests a minority background. Broadcasters and professionals have often been advised to "neutralise" their accents, and many people report feeling embarrassed or having to switch between two ways of speaking, one for home and one for work.

There is, nonetheless, a growing counter-movement. In recent years, regional accents have become more visible in the media, and campaigners have argued that accent should be treated as a protected characteristic in the way that other forms of prejudice are. Many people take pride in their local speech as an expression of identity, solidarity, and humour. Dialect words and expressions, far from being signs of ignorance, often preserve older forms of the language and capture ideas that standard vocabulary lacks.

The picture is complicated by mobility and technology. Young people who move between cities or who grow up online often develop mixed ways of speaking, borrowing from different regions and from other languages. Some linguists expect that a few traditional dialects will fade, while new varieties will emerge among urban communities. Language has never been static, and every generation tends to complain that the next is spoiling it.

What should we do? Perhaps the first step is awareness. If we notice our own reactions when we hear a particular accent, we can ask whether they reflect something about the speaker's message or only our prejudices. Schools can teach standard forms, which remain useful for formal writing and wide communication, without suggesting that other varieties are defective. Understanding that there are many legitimate ways to speak a language is not a threat to communication. It is a way of respecting the people who make a language live.
