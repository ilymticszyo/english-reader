Imagine a modern office. Open desks, group projects, frequent meetings, and a cheerful manager who believes that the best ideas come from lively discussion. For some employees, this is a dream. For others, it is a daily struggle. These are the introverts, people who feel more energetic when they have time alone and who find long periods of social contact tiring. In a world designed for the outgoing, are they being overlooked?

First, it is important to understand what introversion is not. It is not the same as shyness. A shy person fears social situations and worries about what others think, while a quiet person may enjoy company, but in smaller doses, and needs quiet time to recover afterwards. Nor does it mean being unfriendly. Many of them have a few close friendships that they value deeply, and are excellent listeners. Psychologists believe that around a third to a half of the population is quiet by nature, although most people fall somewhere between the two extremes.

Unfortunately, modern societies often reward extroverted behaviour. Schools frequently emphasise group work and class participation, and quiet children may be described as "shy" or "not engaged". At work, the employee who speaks most in meetings is often seen as the most capable, even if his ideas are no better than those of a quieter colleague. Leadership, too, is commonly linked to confidence and charisma, although research suggests that quiet leaders can be especially effective, because they tend to listen carefully to their teams and let capable people take the initiative.

The costs of this bias can be high. Quiet people may be passed over for promotion, and they may feel pressure to pretend to be someone they are not. This kind of acting is tiring and can lead to stress and a feeling of being misunderstood. Organisations also lose out. Studies suggest that large groups often produce fewer good ideas than the same number of people working separately and then comparing their results, because loud voices dominate and others stay silent. Quiet employees often have valuable thoughts that never get heard.

What can be done? Employers can make simple changes. They can send an agenda before a meeting so that everyone has time to prepare, and ask for written ideas as well as spoken ones. They can offer quiet spaces for focused work, and avoid expecting people to be sociable at all times. Teachers, too, can give students different ways to take part, such as writing, small-group discussion, or preparing a presentation in advance, rather than relying only on those who speak up quickly.

Quiet people can also help themselves. They can plan time to rest after a busy day, choose the settings where they work best, and prepare their contributions in advance. Many find that speaking early in a meeting is easier than waiting, and that smaller conversations bring out their strengths.

The goal is not to turn the world upside down, or to say that one type of personality is better than the other. Both are needed. A society that makes space for different temperaments will probably be more creative, more thoughtful, and more kind.
