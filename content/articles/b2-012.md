Every year, millions of people buy tickets to watch horror films, read frightening novels, or walk through dark houses full of actors in masks. This seems strange. In everyday life, we do our best to avoid danger. Why do we pay money to be scared?

One explanation is that fear, in a safe setting, can be enjoyable. When we watch something frightening, our bodies react just as they would to real danger. The heart beats faster, the muscles tighten, and the brain releases chemicals that prepare us to fight or run away. But because we know that the monster is not real, the brain also understands that we are safe. Once the scene ends, the tension disappears, and the contrast creates a feeling of relief and even happiness. Psychologists sometimes compare it to a roller coaster.

A second explanation is that scary stories let us practise. Humans have always lived with threats, and imagining them may help us to prepare. A child who hears a story about a wolf learns, without risk, that wolves are dangerous and that clever people can survive them. Some researchers believe that horror works in a similar way for adults, giving us a chance to test our reactions to frightening situations from the comfort of a sofa.

Culture also plays a part. Almost every society has its own ghost stories and legends, and they often reflect local worries. Old European tales warned children not to enter the forest alone. Modern horror films often deal with fears of technology, disease, or strangers. In this sense, scary stories are a mirror: they show us what a society is afraid of at a particular time.

There is also a social side. Watching a horror film with friends is a shared experience. Studies suggest that people feel closer to those who have been through something intense together, even if it was only a film. Screaming at the same moment, and then laughing about it, can bring people together.

Of course, not everyone enjoys this kind of entertainment. Some people are more sensitive to fear, and for them, a scary film can cause sleepless nights rather than excitement. Experts advise that children in particular should not watch things that are too frightening for their age, since their imagination can make the images feel real for days.

For those who do enjoy it, however, the pleasure is real. We want to feel our hearts race, to hold our breath, and then to be reminded that we are safe at home. Perhaps this is the secret of scary stories: they are really about the comfort of being alive.
