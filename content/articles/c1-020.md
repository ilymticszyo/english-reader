In the past, boredom was an unavoidable feature of daily existence. There were long journeys with nothing to look at but the window, queues with nothing to do but wait, and quiet evenings with no entertainment but one's own thoughts. Today, we can fill nearly every spare moment. A glance at a phone while waiting for a lift, a podcast while washing dishes, a video during a meal: the habit of avoiding boredom has become almost automatic. Some psychologists now wonder whether we are losing something valuable.

Boredom is generally defined as the unpleasant feeling of wanting to engage in a satisfying activity but being unable to do so. It is not the same as depression or sadness, although they may coexist. Because the sensation is uncomfortable, we do our best to escape it, and a famous experiment demonstrates how strong this impulse can be. In a series of studies, volunteers were asked to sit alone in an empty room with their thoughts for up to fifteen minutes. Many found the experience unpleasant, and in one version, in which participants could give themselves a mild electric shock if they wished, a surprising proportion chose to do so, despite having said earlier that they would pay to avoid it.

Yet the same discomfort may serve a function. When our minds are free from external demands, they do not simply stop. Brain-imaging research has identified a network of regions that become more active when we are not focused on a task, and these regions seem to be involved in recalling memories, imagining the future, and thinking about other people. Daydreaming, in other words, is not idleness but a form of mental work, and it may be necessary for planning our lives and understanding ourselves.

Creativity appears to benefit as well. In one well-known experiment, participants who first carried out a dull task, such as copying numbers from a telephone directory, then produced more original ideas in a creative exercise than those who had done something more interesting. The explanation proposed is that boredom pushes the mind to look for stimulation, leading it to wander into unusual associations. Many writers and scientists have described their best ideas as arriving in the shower, on a walk, or during a long train journey, precisely when nothing else demanded their attention.

Children, too, may need boredom. Parents often feel pressure to keep their children constantly entertained, but some educators argue that unstructured time is essential for developing independence. A child who is bored must invent a game, build something from what is at hand, or tell a story to herself. These skills are exercised only when no one else supplies the entertainment.

It would be foolish to romanticise tedium. Chronic boredom at work or school is associated with poor performance and, in some cases, with risk-taking and mental health problems. People living in difficult conditions, such as prisoners or those who are unemployed for a long time, may experience it as a torment. The argument is not that we should seek out dullness, but that we should avoid eliminating all of it.

Technology companies, of course, have little commercial interest in our boredom. Applications are deliberately designed to fill the gaps in our day, offering a new video, a new message, or a new piece of news at exactly the moment we might otherwise have started to think. This does not mean that devices are evil, only that their business model depends on engagement. Understanding this may help us to make conscious decisions. If we notice that we reach for the phone every time we feel the first signs of restlessness, we can begin to ask what we are avoiding, and whether the restlessness might be trying to tell us something.

How might we preserve some space for it? Small habits can help: leaving the phone behind on a walk, waiting in a queue without reaching for a screen, or setting aside an hour without any input at all. The first few minutes may feel strange, even irritating. If researchers are right, however, what follows is often the most valuable part, as the mind, free to wander, begins to return to the thoughts that matter. Boredom, it seems, is not the enemy of a good life but one of its quiet foundations.
