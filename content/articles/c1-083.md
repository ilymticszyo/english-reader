In 1960, the physicist Eugene Wigner published an essay with the memorable title "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences". His puzzle was simple but profound. Mathematics is created by human minds, often with no thought of practical application, yet again and again, ideas invented for their own sake turn out to describe the physical world with astonishing precision. Why should this be so?
 
Examples are abundant. The ancient Greeks studied the properties of curves known as ellipses purely out of geometrical curiosity. Nearly two thousand years later, Johannes Kepler discovered that the planets move along ellipses, and his observation became the foundation of Newton's theory of gravity. In the nineteenth century, mathematicians explored strange geometries that seemed to have no connection with reality, and in the twentieth, Einstein used them to describe space and time. Complex numbers, which were once dismissed as imaginary and absurd, are now indispensable in electrical engineering and in the theory of the smallest particles.
 
Several explanations have been offered. One view holds that mathematics is a kind of language that we have developed for describing patterns, and that it is hardly surprising that a language designed for patterns should be useful in a universe that contains many. According to this view, we notice and develop the mathematics that works, and we forget the rest. The puzzle is therefore smaller than it seems, because we are looking at a selection of successes.
 
A second view, associated with the Platonic tradition, is that mathematical truths exist independently of us, in some abstract realm, and that the universe is somehow built on them. On this account, mathematicians are explorers who discover structures rather than inventors who create them. This explains why people in different times and places arrive at the same results, and why a proof, once established, remains true forever. Many mathematicians feel instinctively that this is the case, though philosophers continue to argue about what it could mean for numbers to "exist".
 
A third suggestion is evolutionary. Our brains have developed to deal with a world in which counting, measuring, and recognising shapes are useful for survival. Mathematics, in this view, is an extension of abilities that we already possess, and its success reflects the fact that we are part of the same physical world that it describes. Yet this cannot fully explain why abstract branches that are far removed from everyday experience, such as the study of infinite sets, have later found unexpected applications.
 
There is also a more practical answer. Mathematics gives us a way of making precise and testable claims, and it allows us to draw out the consequences of assumptions with complete rigour. When a mathematical model fits the data, we gain not only a description but also the power to predict. When it fails, we learn that our assumptions were wrong. The effectiveness of mathematics, in this sense, lies in its ability to expose our errors as well as to confirm our ideas.
 
Some mathematicians have a different worry: not that mathematics works too well, but that we are tempted to forget its limits. A model is only as good as its assumptions, and the history of economics and finance is full of examples in which elegant equations gave a false sense of certainty. The beauty of a theory is no guarantee that it is true. What makes mathematics so powerful in physics is that its predictions can be tested again and again against careful measurements, a luxury that is not available in every field. Where the world is messy and human, mathematics can illuminate, but it cannot replace judgment.
 
For teachers, there is a lesson here. Mathematics is often presented to students as a collection of rules to be memorised, but it is better understood as a way of thinking, with a long history of curiosity and surprise. The fact that these strange constructions of the human mind can describe the stars is perhaps the most remarkable thing about it. After more than sixty years, Wigner's puzzle is still unsolved.
