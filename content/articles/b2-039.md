Fifty years ago, running 42 kilometres was an activity for a small number of athletes. Today, big city marathons attract tens of thousands of runners, and hundreds of thousands more try to win a place in a lottery. People of all ages and backgrounds, from grandmothers to office workers, are training for what was once seen as the limit of human endurance. What explains this craze?

The history of the marathon is half true and half legend. According to the story, a Greek soldier ran from the town of Marathon to Athens in 490 BC to announce a victory in battle, and then fell down and died. The distance became the main event of the first modern Olympic Games in 1896. For most of the twentieth century, though, it remained a specialist event. The change began in the 1970s, when a wave of interest in jogging and fitness spread through the United States and Europe, and the first big city races began to welcome ordinary runners.

Part of the appeal today is the challenge itself. In a world where many tasks are done on a screen, a marathon is clear and physical. You cannot fake it. The distance is the same for everyone, and the finish line is a real place that you reach on your own legs. Many runners say that finishing a marathon changed how they see themselves. If they can do that, they feel, they can do other difficult things as well.

There is also a social side. Runners often join clubs, and the long training sessions are a chance to talk with friends. Many people run for a charity, collecting money from friends and family, and this gives them an extra reason to keep going when training becomes hard. On the day of the race, crowds line the streets to cheer for strangers, and many runners say that this support is the thing they remember best.

Marathons can bring real benefits to health, but they also have risks. Training reduces the chance of heart disease, helps control weight, and improves mood. However, too many runners start too fast, increase the distance too quickly, and suffer injuries. In rare cases, people with undiscovered heart problems have collapsed during a race. Doctors advise beginners to build up slowly, over many months, and to see a doctor first if they have any doubts about their health.

Critics also point to cost. Entry fees, shoes, watches, and travel to races can add up, and an activity that once needed only a pair of shoes has become an industry. Some people also worry that the pressure to finish a marathon makes running seem like a test to pass instead of a pleasure.

Still, for millions of people, the marathon has become a symbol of a simple and powerful idea: that with patience and hard work, we can do much more than we think.
