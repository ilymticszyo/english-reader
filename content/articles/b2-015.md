Imagine you are at a party, and you see two people talking from across the room. You cannot hear a word, yet you can often tell a lot. One person is leaning forward with a smile, while the other is looking at the floor with arms crossed. Without any sound, you guess that one is interested and the other is uncomfortable. This is body language: the messages we send without speaking.

Researchers have studied it for decades. One of the best-known ideas comes from a psychologist who claimed that only a small part of communication depends on words, while the rest comes from tone and body movement. This claim has often been repeated, but it was based on very narrow experiments, and it is certainly not true for every situation. If someone tells you directions to the station, you need their words. Still, when words and body language disagree, many people do trust the body more.

Some signals are fairly reliable. A genuine smile, for example, involves the muscles around the eyes as well as the mouth, and it is difficult to fake. Eye contact usually shows attention, though too much can feel aggressive. Leaning towards someone suggests interest, and standing with your body turned away may show that you want to leave.

However, experts warn against reading single gestures as if they were a dictionary. Crossed arms might mean defensiveness, but they might also mean that the person is cold or simply comfortable. Looking away could be a sign of dishonesty, or just shyness. In studies, even trained police officers are only a little better than chance at telling when someone is lying from their behaviour alone. A better approach is to look for several signals together and to compare them with the person's normal behaviour.

Culture matters enormously, too. In some countries, direct eye contact is a sign of respect; in others, it is considered rude, especially towards older people. The "thumbs up" gesture is positive in many places but offensive in a few. People also differ in how close they stand. In some cultures, friends talk at a distance of a few centimetres, while in others, that would feel uncomfortable.

The good news is that we can improve how we use our own body language. Standing straight, keeping an open posture, and making steady eye contact can make a speaker seem more confident, and some studies suggest that these changes can even affect how confident a person feels. Practising in front of a mirror or a camera helps us notice habits we did not know we had.

In the end, body language is not magic. It is simply another way of listening, one that rewards patience and curiosity about the people around us.
