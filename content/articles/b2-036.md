When did you last write a long text by hand? For many people, the answer is "I can't remember". We write messages on phones, reports on laptops, and shopping lists in an app. Some schools have reduced lessons in handwriting, or have stopped teaching joined-up writing altogether. Is this a harmless change, or are we losing something important?

Supporters of the change say that handwriting is a skill from another time. Typing is faster, and the results are easier to read, to correct, and to share. Children will spend most of their adult lives using keyboards, so, the argument goes, school time should be used to teach them how to type quickly and accurately. In addition, some children with weak muscle control find handwriting very hard, and a keyboard can make them feel more confident.

But a growing number of researchers disagree. Brain studies have found that writing by hand activates more areas of the brain than typing does. Each letter must be formed by a series of movements, and this seems to help children to recognise and remember letters. In one experiment, young children who learned letters by writing them showed stronger brain activity, when they later saw the same letters, than children who had only typed them.

There is also evidence about learning. In a well-known study, students took notes in a lecture, either by hand or on a laptop. Those who used laptops wrote more, but often copied the words of the speaker without thinking. Those who wrote by hand could not keep up with everything, so they were forced to listen, choose what was important, and put it into their own words. When tested a week later, the handwriting group understood the ideas better. Some later studies have questioned the size of this effect, but many have found a similar pattern.

Handwriting has a personal value as well. A letter or a card in a friend's handwriting feels different from an email. It shows that someone spent time on us, and it keeps something of the writer's personality. Many people keep old letters from their grandparents for exactly this reason. Handwriting is also part of our identity, and for centuries, a signature has been used as proof of who we are.

None of this means that schools should reject technology. A sensible balance seems best. Children should learn to write by hand in their early years, when it supports reading and memory, and they should also learn to type, since both skills will be useful. For adults, writing by hand can be a way to slow down, to think more clearly, and to enjoy something that a screen cannot give.

Perhaps the best advice is simple. Keep a notebook, write a letter to a friend, and let your hand think for a while.
