In 1995, the British public was warned that a certain type of contraceptive pill doubled the risk of a dangerous blood clot. The announcement, widely reported under alarming headlines, led thousands of women to stop taking the pill. In the following year, there were about thirteen thousand more abortions than usual in England and Wales, and many more unwanted pregnancies, which themselves carry health risks. What the headlines had neglected to explain was the size of the original danger. The chance of a clot had risen from about one in seven thousand to about two in seven thousand. The risk had indeed doubled, but it remained very small.
 
This episode illustrates a common failure to understand how risk is expressed. Statisticians distinguish between "relative" and "absolute" risk. A relative risk tells us how much the likelihood of an event has changed in proportion; an absolute risk tells us how probable the event actually is. A one hundred percent increase sounds alarming when it applies to a tiny baseline, and a thirty percent reduction sounds impressive when it applies to a harm that was already rare. Journalists, advertisers, and sometimes researchers prefer relative figures because they are more dramatic. Anyone trying to make sensible decisions should always ask the same question: "Relative to what?"

A related problem is our difficulty in dealing with probabilities that are conditional upon other facts. Suppose that a test for a disease is ninety-nine percent accurate, and that you test positive. How worried should you be? Most people, and, in several studies, a surprising proportion of doctors, answer that there is a ninety-nine percent chance that they are ill. The correct answer depends on how common the disease is. If only one person in ten thousand has it, then among ten thousand people tested, about one will be ill and test positive, but roughly one hundred healthy people will also receive a false positive result. A positive result therefore implies a probability of less than one percent. Psychologists have found that the confusion diminishes sharply when the information is presented in terms of natural frequencies, such as "one hundred out of ten thousand", rather than percentages.

Our intuitions about risk are shaped by factors that have little to do with arithmetic. People tend to fear dramatic, unfamiliar, and involuntary dangers, such as terrorist attacks, plane crashes, or shark bites, more than ordinary ones that are statistically far more lethal, such as car accidents, heart disease, and falls at home. Vivid stories are remembered more easily than numbers, and the news media, which favour the unusual, reinforce the effect. After the attacks of September 2001, many Americans chose to drive rather than fly, and one researcher later estimated that the resulting increase in road traffic caused about fifteen hundred extra deaths, several times the number lost on the aircraft themselves, although other researchers have criticised his method.

Other errors are structural. We see patterns where none exist, and a run of coincidences can seem to demand an explanation, although such runs occur regularly by chance alone. We confuse correlation with causation, concluding, for example, that because ice cream sales and drowning accidents both rise in summer, one must cause the other. And we are inclined to believe that unlikely events are rarer than they are. With millions of people, and thousands of occasions each day, even a "one in a million" coincidence will happen to someone, somewhere, very regularly.

None of this means that experts always know best, or that numbers should be trusted blindly. Statistics can be misused, selected, and manipulated. But a basic familiarity with the ideas involved is among the most useful forms of literacy in the modern world, since it affects medical decisions, financial choices, and the way we vote. Teaching it well, with real examples and clear language, would equip citizens to resist both unnecessary panic and false reassurance.
