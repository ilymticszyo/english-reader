Read a page of English written six hundred years ago, and you will probably understand very little. Words have changed their meaning, grammar has become simpler, and pronunciation has moved so far that the same letters sound completely different. All living languages change constantly, and the process never stops. Why does it happen, and can anything be done to prevent it?

One cause is contact between peoples. When groups of people meet, through trade, conquest, or migration, they borrow words from one another. English is a famous example. After the Norman conquest of 1066, thousands of French words entered the language, and this is why English has pairs such as "cow" and "beef", or "pig" and "pork". The first word of each pair comes from the Germanic speech of the ordinary people who looked after the animals, while the second comes from the French of the upper classes who ate the meat. Today, English itself is borrowed by many other languages, and words such as "computer" and "okay" can be heard worldwide.

A second cause is the simple need for ease. Speakers tend to shorten and simplify. We say "gonna" for "going to", and "cos" for "because". Over centuries, these small shortcuts accumulate, and spoken forms drift away from written ones. This helps to explain why English spelling is so confusing: it often preserves the way words were pronounced many centuries ago, when the system was fixed by the early printers.

New things need new names. The invention of the telephone, the aeroplane, and the internet each brought a wave of new words, and everyday life continues to add more. At the same time, old words disappear when the things they describe vanish from daily use. Few people now need to talk about a "blacksmith's bellows".

Social factors matter, too. Young people often use language to show that they belong to their own group, and to separate themselves from their parents. Slang spreads quickly, and while most of it disappears, some expressions stay and become part of the standard language. Social media has made this process much faster, because new words and meanings can now travel around the world in a few hours.

Not everyone welcomes the changes. In every age, some people have complained that the language is being damaged, usually by the young. Some countries have established official bodies to guard the purity of their national languages. Yet most linguists argue that change is not decay. A language is not a fixed object that can be broken; it is a tool that adapts to the needs of its users. If it did not change, it would soon fail to serve them.

This does not mean that there are no rules. At any time, a community agrees on conventions, and following them helps to make communication clear. But those conventions are agreements, not laws of nature, and they evolve. The English that we speak today would seem strange to Shakespeare, and our own words will seem old-fashioned to people in the future.

Change is therefore a sign of life. Only languages that nobody speaks stand still.
