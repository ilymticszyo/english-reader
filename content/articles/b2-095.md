Today, almost everyone carries a camera in their pocket. Billions of photographs are taken every day, and we hardly think about the process. Yet the ability to capture an image of the world is only about two hundred years old, and its history is a story of patient experiment.
 
The idea began with a simple optical effect. In a dark room, a small hole in one wall will project an upside-down picture of the outside scene onto the opposite wall. This was known to ancient scholars, and from the sixteenth century, artists used a version of this device, called the camera obscura, to help them draw. But the image vanished as soon as the light did. No one knew how to make it permanent.
 
The breakthrough came in the 1820s, when a French inventor named Nicéphore Niépce coated a metal plate with a light-sensitive material and exposed it for many hours. The result, a blurry view of rooftops from his window, is considered the oldest surviving photograph. A little later, another Frenchman, Louis Daguerre, developed a faster process. His "daguerreotypes" needed only minutes of exposure and produced sharp, detailed pictures. The invention was announced in 1839, and within a few years, portrait studios were opening in cities around the world.
 
These early photographs were costly and complicated. Subjects had to sit still for a long time, and every picture was unique, since there was no negative to make copies. Over the following decades, new methods allowed several prints to be made from one negative, and exposure times fell to a fraction of a second.
 
The next major step was made by an American businessman, George Eastman. In 1888, he introduced a simple box camera, loaded with a roll of film, with the slogan, "You press the button, we do the rest." Customers sent the camera back to the company, which developed the pictures and returned the camera with new film. For the first time, photography was available to ordinary families. Pictures of birthdays, holidays, and weddings became part of everyday life.
 
Colour film appeared in the 1930s, and by the middle of the century, cameras were smaller, cheaper, and easier to use. The first digital camera was built by an engineer at Kodak in 1975, although the company did not recognise its importance. By the early twenty-first century, digital cameras had replaced film, and then they were absorbed into phones.
 
Photography has changed the way we remember, learn, and see. It allows us to look at the faces of people who lived long ago, and to share an experience across the world in seconds. At the same time, in a world full of images, it takes more skill than ever to look carefully.
