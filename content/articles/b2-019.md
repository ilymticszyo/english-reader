Ask a group of students what they think about homework, and you will probably hear a long groan. Ask their parents, and the response may be more complicated. Some believe that homework teaches discipline, while others see it as a source of stress that steals family time. In recent years, a few schools and even a few countries have experimented with reducing it or removing it altogether. Is this a wise idea?

Those who want to limit homework make several arguments. First, they point to research suggesting that the benefits are smaller than we assume, particularly for young children. Many studies find little connection between the amount of homework in primary school and later success. For older students, a moderate amount seems helpful, but beyond about two hours a night, the results often get worse rather than better.

Second, they emphasise well-being. Many teenagers report that they sleep too little and feel anxious about their workload. After a full day at school, they spend the evening on exercises, and there is little time left for sport, hobbies, or simply relaxing. Critics say that childhood should include play and rest, and that these are also important for learning.

Third, homework can increase inequality. A child with a quiet room, a computer, and parents who can explain the maths problem has an obvious advantage over a child who shares a small flat and has nobody to ask. If homework counts towards grades, it may measure a family's resources rather than a student's ability.

On the other side, supporters of homework have strong points. They argue that practice is essential for mastering a skill, whether it is a language, an instrument, or algebra. Class time is limited, and extra practice helps students to remember what they have learned. Homework also develops habits such as planning, managing time, and working independently, which will be valuable at university and at work. Some teachers add that it helps parents to see what their children are studying.

The most convincing view may be that the quality of homework matters more than the quantity. A short, thoughtful task that connects to the lesson, such as reading a story or interviewing a grandparent, can be far more useful than fifty identical questions. Some schools now allow students to choose from a list of projects, or they limit the time to a certain number of minutes depending on age.

A complete ban seems extreme. Yet the debate has forced many people to ask an important question: what is school for? If the answer is to develop curious, healthy, and independent learners, then we should judge every task, including homework, by whether it moves us towards that goal.
