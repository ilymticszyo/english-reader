Within certain corners of the business world, it has long been fashionable to boast about how little sleep one needs. Executives describe waking at four in the morning, entrepreneurs claim that rest is for the unambitious, and the phrase "I will sleep when I am dead" is treated as a mark of dedication rather than a warning. Yet the accumulated evidence from sleep research points firmly in the opposite direction: chronic sleep deprivation damages precisely those abilities that professional success relies upon.

Consider what happens to the brain when it is consistently short of rest. Attention becomes unreliable, so that tasks which require sustained concentration take longer and contain more errors. Memory consolidation is impaired, which means that information absorbed during the day is less likely to be retained. Emotional regulation deteriorates as well; tired people tend to react more strongly to minor irritations and to interpret ambiguous situations negatively. In experiments, volunteers who had slept poorly were more likely to take foolish risks and less able to recognise their own decline in performance, a particularly troubling combination.

This last point is crucial, because it helps to explain why the hustle culture persists. People who sleep very little often feel that they have adapted, when in reality they have simply lost the ability to judge their own limitations. A small number of individuals do appear to function well on unusually short sleep owing to rare genetic variations, but they are far less common than self-descriptions would suggest. For the great majority of adults, between seven and nine hours remains the sensible range.

The consequences extend beyond individual performance. Organisations that reward long hours may inadvertently encourage mistakes, conflict and burnout. In some professions, such as medicine, transport and heavy industry, fatigue is directly linked to accidents with serious human costs. It is therefore somewhat ironic that the very workplaces most preoccupied with efficiency are often the ones that treat rest as a luxury.

What, then, can be done? On a personal level, it helps to treat sleep as a priority rather than whatever remains once everything else is finished. Regular times for going to bed and waking up, a dark and cool bedroom, and a reduction in screen use before bedtime all contribute to better quality rest. Caffeine late in the day, though tempting, tends to undermine the sleep that follows.

At the organisational level, managers can set an example by not sending messages late at night, by respecting the boundaries between work and personal time, and by evaluating employees on results rather than visible exhaustion. Some companies have experimented with shorter working weeks and report that output holds steady while employee wellbeing improves, although the evidence is still emerging.

It is worth noting, too, that the relationship between sleep and creativity has attracted growing interest. Several experiments indicate that people are more likely to solve a difficult problem after a night's rest than after an equivalent period spent awake, apparently because the sleeping brain reorganises information and uncovers connections that conscious effort had missed. Many writers, scientists and musicians have described waking with a solution that eluded them the evening before. If this is correct, then the person who works through the night to meet a deadline may be sacrificing not only accuracy but also the very insight that distinguishes outstanding work from the merely competent, which is a poor exchange by any reasonable measure.

None of this implies that ambition is misguided. It suggests merely that sustained achievement depends on recovery, and that the most productive people are not those who work every waking hour, but those who understand that rest is part of the work.
