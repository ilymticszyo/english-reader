Ask almost any office worker what they dislike about their job, and "too many meetings" will appear near the top of the list. A typical manager may spend more than half of the working week in meetings, and many employees complain that they only begin their real work after everyone else has gone home. If meetings are so unpopular, why do we keep having them?

Part of the answer is that meetings can be useful. When a group needs to make a difficult decision, solve a complex problem, or build trust, talking together is often faster and more effective than a long series of emails. A good meeting allows people to ask questions, to hear different views, and to leave with a shared understanding of what to do next. In a new team, regular meetings also help people to feel connected.

The problem is that many meetings are not good ones. Some have no clear purpose, and others include people who have nothing to contribute. Some are used to share information that could have been sent in a short message. Others are really a way for managers to feel in control, or for employees to show that they are busy. One survey found that workers believed that more than a third of the meetings they attended were a waste of time. Since each meeting also interrupts the time before and after it, in which a person might have concentrated on a hard task, the real cost is even higher.

There is also a financial side. If ten people with an average salary spend an hour in a meeting, the company is paying for ten hours of work. Some firms have begun to calculate this cost and display it on a screen during the meeting. Not surprisingly, people start to think more carefully about whether the meeting is needed.

Several organisations have experimented with solutions. Some have introduced "meeting-free days", when nobody may arrange a meeting, so that employees can work without interruption. Others have shortened the standard length of meetings from an hour to twenty-five or fifty minutes, to give people time to rest in between. And a few have banned chairs in the meeting room, since standing people tend to finish more quickly.

Experts also suggest simple rules. Before you call a meeting, ask whether an email would do the same job. If it is necessary, invite only the people who are really needed. Write an agenda in advance, and make clear what decision the group must reach. During the meeting, stick to the topic, and at the end, record who will do what and by when.

None of this is difficult, but it requires discipline, and above all, a change of culture. In a workplace where being busy is admired, to say "I do not think we need to meet" can feel like a risk. Yet the organisations that dare to do so often find that people are happier and produce better work.

The aim is not to remove meetings completely. It is to make sure that when people do come together, the time is really worth it.
