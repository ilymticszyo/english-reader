For most of human history, the night sky was part of everyday experience. On clear evenings, thousands of stars were visible to the naked eye, and the pale band of the Milky Way stretched from one side of the horizon to the other. Today, a large share of the world's population can no longer see it. In cities, where the glow of streets, signs, and buildings drowns out all but the brightest stars, a child may grow up without once seeing a truly dark sky. Light pollution, as this effect is called, has spread so quickly that many scientists now regard it as a form of environmental damage.
 
Measurements taken from space show that the brightness of the night has been increasing across the world by a few percent each year. The change is driven partly by growth in population and wealth, and partly by technology. The arrival of cheap, efficient lamps has made lighting more affordable, but it has also encouraged people to use more of it. Instead of reducing consumption, the savings in energy have often been spent on brighter and more widespread illumination, an example of the rebound effect.
 
The consequences are not limited to astronomy, although astronomers were the first to complain. Living things evolved over millions of years with a regular cycle of day and night, and many depend on it. Birds that migrate at night can be confused by bright lights and fly into buildings in vast numbers. Young sea turtles, which find the ocean by moving toward the brightest horizon, may crawl toward a coastal road instead. Insects gather around lamps until they are exhausted or eaten, and some researchers believe that artificial light may be contributing to the decline of insect populations. Even plants may be affected, as trees near street lamps keep their leaves later in autumn.
 
Human health may also suffer. Our bodies produce a hormone called melatonin during darkness, which helps to regulate sleep. Light in the evening, especially the bluish light emitted by many modern lamps and screens, suppresses its production and can disturb our internal clock. Studies have linked night-shift work and bright bedrooms with sleep problems, and some have suggested a connection with higher rates of certain illnesses, though the evidence is still developing.
 
There is also a cultural loss. People in every society have looked at the stars to tell stories, to find their way, to measure time, and to wonder about their place in the universe. Many of the greatest achievements in science began with such observation. To lose the sight of the stars is to lose a connection with something that has inspired humanity for as long as we have existed.
 
Fortunately, light pollution is one of the easiest forms of pollution to reduce, because it can be switched off. Several simple measures help. Lamps can be shielded so that light shines downward instead of upward and outward, directed only where it is needed, and fitted with timers or sensors. Warmer-coloured bulbs cause less disruption than harsh white ones. Some cities have adopted rules to limit unnecessary lighting, and certain regions, known as dark-sky reserves, have been designated to protect the view of the stars.
 
Public attitudes are often shaped by fear. Many people assume that more light means less crime, and so bright lamps are installed in car parks, alleys, and streets as a matter of routine. The evidence, however, is less clear than we might think. Studies that have compared neighbourhoods before and after changes in lighting have found no consistent link between brightness and crime, and glare can make it harder to see into shadows. Light that is carefully placed and of moderate strength often helps pedestrians more than a harsh floodlight, which leaves the eyes unable to adjust to the dark.
 
Lighting is, of course, necessary for safety and comfort, and nobody suggests a return to darkness. But the aim should be better light, not more. A well-designed street can be safe and also allow the stars to be seen. The night, after all, is part of nature, and it deserves protection.
