Twice a year, millions of people in many countries change their clocks. In spring, they move them forward by one hour, losing an hour of sleep. In autumn, they move them back and enjoy an extra hour in bed. The system is called daylight saving time, and it has been the subject of argument for more than a century.
 
The idea was first used widely during the First World War, when governments hoped to save fuel by making better use of natural light. By moving the clock forward, people would wake up earlier and use less electricity in the evening. Many countries dropped the practice after the war, and brought it back again during the next one. Today, around seventy countries use some form of it, while many others, including most of Asia and Africa, do not.
 
Supporters argue that the longer evenings are good for daily life. People have more time for sport, shopping, and meeting friends after work, and some studies suggest that this increases spending in shops and restaurants. Others say that more light in the evening reduces road accidents, because fewer people drive home in the dark.
 
Critics point out that the evidence for energy savings is weak. In modern societies, people use heating, cooling, and electronic devices throughout the day, and studies in different regions have found only very small effects, or none at all. More serious are the health concerns. Doctors have reported that the change in spring is linked with a short rise in heart attacks and traffic accidents, probably because people lose sleep. Our body clocks are tied to the natural cycle of light, and shifting the clock forces them out of line with the sun.
 
Sleep scientists increasingly believe that permanent standard time, rather than permanent daylight saving time, would be best for health. Under standard time, the sun is highest in the sky around noon, which matches the body's natural rhythm. Permanent summer time would mean very dark winter mornings, especially for children walking to school.
 
Politicians, though, have found it hard to act. In Europe, a vote in the European Parliament in favour of ending the clock changes was never turned into law, because member states could not agree on which time to choose. In the United States, some states have passed laws to stay on one time all year, but they need approval from the national government. Meanwhile, neighbours who choose differently could create confusion for travel and business.
 
There is no perfect answer. Choosing one time all year would end the disruption of changing the clocks, but it would also bring a permanent trade-off between light in the morning and light in the evening. What most experts agree on is that the twice-yearly change is the worst option of all.
