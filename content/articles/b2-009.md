Think about your morning. You probably wake up, go to the bathroom, make a drink, and check your phone in more or less the same order every day. You do not decide each step. Your brain has turned the whole routine into a habit, and this is a very useful thing.

Scientists who study habits explain that the brain is always looking for ways to save energy. Making a decision takes effort, so when we repeat an action in the same situation, the brain begins to treat it as automatic. A habit has three parts. First, there is a cue, such as the smell of coffee or the sound of an alarm. Then there is the routine, which is the action itself. Finally, there is a reward, something pleasant that tells the brain the action is worth repeating.

This explains why bad habits are so hard to change. If you eat sweets every afternoon, the cue might be tiredness, the routine is walking to the kitchen, and the reward is a quick feeling of pleasure. Simply deciding to stop rarely works, because the cue and the reward are still there, and the old pattern continues to pull you.

Researchers suggest that a more successful strategy is to keep the cue and the reward but change the routine. If you feel tired at three o'clock, you might go outside for a short walk instead. The walk gives you fresh air and a break, which can satisfy the same need. After a few weeks, the new routine may become as automatic as the old one.

What about forming a good habit? One popular belief is that it takes twenty-one days. In fact, a well-known study found that the average time was about two months, and that the range was very wide, from a few weeks to most of a year. Missing a single day did not seem to ruin the process, which is encouraging for anyone who has failed in the past.

Experts give several practical tips. Start with a very small action, such as two minutes of reading rather than an hour. Link the new habit to something you already do, for example by stretching right after you brush your teeth. Make it easy by preparing what you need in advance. And celebrate small successes, because the feeling of progress is a reward in itself.

Habits are not a matter of weak or strong character. They are the result of how the brain works, and once you understand the pattern, you can begin to design it.
