Every autumn, billions of birds leave their summer homes and fly thousands of kilometres to warmer places, and every spring they return, often to the same tree or the same chimney. A small bird weighing less than a coin may cross a desert or an ocean without stopping, guided by no map or compass. For centuries, this journey was one of the great mysteries of nature. Today, scientists have begun to understand how it works, although many questions remain.

The first problem is why birds migrate at all. The answer is mostly food. In summer, the northern countries have long days and plenty of insects, which make an excellent place to raise young. But in winter, the insects disappear, the ground freezes, and food becomes scarce. Flying south is safer than staying. The journey is dangerous and uses vast amounts of energy, but for many species, it is worth the risk.

How do they know where to go? The simplest part of the answer is that much of the knowledge is inherited. In experiments, young birds raised in cages, which had never seen their parents or any other birds, tried to fly in the correct direction when the season came. They seemed to be born with an internal programme, which tells them roughly which direction to follow and for how long. Some birds that travel alone for the first time, such as young cuckoos, reach a place they have never seen.

To follow the right direction, birds use several kinds of guide. One is the sun. By checking the position of the sun and using their own body clock to account for the time of day, birds can work out south. Those that fly at night use the stars. Experiments with birds in planetariums showed that they turn in the right direction when they see the correct pattern of stars on the ceiling.

Perhaps the most surprising guide is the Earth's magnetic field. Scientists have shown that birds can sense it, and that they use it like a compass, even on cloudy nights. How they do this is still being investigated. One leading idea is that special molecules in their eyes react to magnetic forces, so that the bird may actually "see" the direction of the field as a pattern in its vision. Other evidence suggests that tiny particles of iron in the beak may also help.

Birds also learn the landscape. Rivers, mountains, and coastlines serve as signposts, and many birds can recognise familiar places from the air. Older birds that have made the trip before are usually better at finding the way than younger ones, and in some species, young birds follow experienced adults.

Sadly, migration is becoming more difficult. Bright city lights confuse night-flying birds, and tall glass buildings kill many each year. Habitat loss along the route removes the places where birds rest and feed, and climate change alters the timing of the seasons, so that birds may arrive too early or too late for the insects they need. Conservation groups try to protect key resting places.

The journey of a small bird across a continent remains one of the most remarkable feats in the natural world.
