Few technologies arouse stronger feelings than nuclear power. To its opponents, it is associated with catastrophic accidents, radioactive waste that remains dangerous for thousands of years, and the shadow of nuclear weapons. To its supporters, it is a reliable source of electricity that produces almost no greenhouse gases and may therefore be indispensable in the struggle against climate change. For several decades, the opponents appeared to be winning in many wealthy countries. Recently, however, the argument has begun to shift, and governments that once planned to abandon nuclear energy are reconsidering.

The case in favour begins with the arithmetic of emissions. Over its whole life cycle, including the mining of uranium and the construction of the plant, nuclear electricity produces roughly the same quantity of carbon dioxide per unit of energy as wind power, and far less than coal or gas. Unlike wind and solar installations, a nuclear station operates continuously, regardless of the weather or the time of day. This quality is becoming increasingly valuable as electricity grids take in larger shares of intermittent renewable sources. Without a means of storing very large amounts of energy cheaply, which does not yet exist, some form of steady, low-carbon supply will be needed to cover the gaps when the sun does not shine and the wind does not blow.

Supporters also contend that the dangers have been exaggerated. Measured by deaths per unit of electricity generated, nuclear power has been shown to be among the safest forms of energy, if one takes into account the deaths from air pollution caused by burning fossil fuels. Even the disasters at Chernobyl in 1986 and Fukushima in 2011, they argue, caused far fewer deaths than the daily toll of coal. Waste, in their view, is a manageable problem, since its volume is small, and several countries have designed deep underground storage sites to contain it safely.

Critics respond with arguments that are not easily dismissed. The first is cost. In Europe and North America, recent nuclear projects have been completed years late and at several times the original budget, in part because each plant tends to be a unique construction, which prevents the savings that come from mass production. Meanwhile, the price of solar panels and wind turbines has fallen dramatically, so that in many places they are now the cheapest sources of new electricity. A pound spent on renewables, critics say, will reduce emissions faster than a pound spent on a reactor that will not operate for fifteen years.

The second objection concerns waste and risk. Although the quantity of waste is small, the question of where to put it remains politically unresolved in many countries, and its protection must be guaranteed for a period longer than any human institution has lasted. Accidents are rare, but their consequences, including the forced evacuation of entire regions, are so severe that the public's fear cannot be dismissed as irrational. Finally, civil nuclear programmes can, in some circumstances, provide a route to the construction of weapons.

New technology might alter this balance. Designers are developing small modular reactors that could be built in factories rather than on site, with the aim of cutting costs and shortening construction. Others are working on reactor designs that shut themselves down safely without human intervention, or that use existing waste as fuel. Such proposals are promising, but they remain largely unproven at commercial scale, and past experience suggests caution about optimistic forecasts.

What seems most reasonable is to avoid treating the question as a matter of ideology. The choice between nuclear and renewable energy is not necessarily either-or, and countries differ greatly in their geography, their resources, and their capacity to manage complex projects. Some, with abundant hydropower or wind, may have little need of nuclear plants. Others, which have few alternatives and a strong industrial base, may find that excluding it makes the transition to clean energy slower and more expensive. Given the scale of the climate challenge, the sensible approach is probably to judge each option by its costs, its risks, and its speed, rather than by the emotions it provokes.
