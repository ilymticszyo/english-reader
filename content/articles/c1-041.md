When a company moves into a new headquarters, the chances are high that the main working area will be a single large room, with rows of desks, no walls, and perhaps a few sofas and pot plants to suggest a relaxed atmosphere. The open-plan office, which now houses a majority of office workers in many countries, was promoted with attractive promises. Without walls, it was said, employees would talk to each other more, ideas would circulate freely, and hierarchies would soften. Yet after several decades, a considerable body of research suggests that many of those promises have not been kept.
 
The idea has a long history. In the 1950s, German consultants developed a layout intended to improve the flow of information, and by the 1960s, American designers had produced the first modular partitions, which were soon turned into the infamous "cubicles". From the beginning, however, the motive was not purely social. Open space is cheaper. It allows more people to be fitted into each square metre, it can be rearranged easily as teams grow or shrink, and it reduces the costs of heating, lighting, and construction. For many employers, the philosophy of collaboration conveniently coincided with the economics of saving money.
 
Researchers who have measured the effects have often reached uncomfortable conclusions. In a widely cited study of two companies that removed their walls, face-to-face interaction fell by about seventy percent, while the exchange of emails and messages rose. Staff, it appeared, responded to the lack of privacy by withdrawing: they put on headphones, avoided conversations that others could overhear, and communicated electronically even with colleagues sitting a few metres away. The architecture designed to encourage conversation had, in practice, made people less willing to speak.
 
Other costs are more easily predicted. Noise is the complaint most often heard, and the problem is not volume alone. Overheard conversation is particularly distracting, since the human brain is designed to pay attention to speech, and it is difficult to ignore even when one is trying to. Studies have associated the noise of open offices with lower concentration, poorer performance on complex tasks, and higher levels of stress. Employees in open spaces also tend to take more sick days, perhaps because germs spread more readily among people who share the same air, or because the strain of constant exposure leaves them less resilient. Many workers also report a feeling of being watched, which reduces their ability to relax and to think freely.
 
The picture is not wholly negative. Some people thrive in lively surroundings, and certain kinds of work, such as the rapid exchange of information in a newsroom or a trading floor, benefit from proximity. Younger employees often value the opportunity to observe and learn from experienced colleagues. And the evidence is far from uniform: much depends on the size of the room, the nature of the tasks, and whether people have alternatives when they need quiet.
 
This last point suggests the most promising direction. Rather than a choice between open space and closed offices, many organisations are now experimenting with "activity-based working", in which a building contains a variety of areas for different purposes: quiet rooms for concentration, small booths for private calls, meeting spaces for collaboration, and social areas for informal talk. Employees move to whichever space suits the task at hand. When it is done well, this approach combines the flexibility of open layouts with the privacy that people need. When it is done badly, and the quiet rooms are too few, it amounts to the same open-plan office under a more fashionable name.
 
The pandemic added a further twist. Having discovered that they could work effectively from home, many employees now expect their offices to offer something that home cannot, such as the chance to meet colleagues, to attend workshops, and to share a sense of belonging. A room full of identical desks, where people sit in silence wearing headphones, is a poor answer to that expectation.
 
The lesson is not that open offices are always wrong, but that workplaces should be designed around the way people actually work, rather than around a theory of how they ought to behave. That demands something that the original enthusiasts neglected: asking the employees themselves.
