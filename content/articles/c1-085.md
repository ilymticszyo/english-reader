In recent years, some of the world's most famous museums have found themselves facing an uncomfortable question. Many of the treasures that fill their galleries, from sculptures and masks to manuscripts and jewellery, were acquired in circumstances that would be widely condemned today. They were taken during wars, seized by colonial armies, bought for trivial sums from people with little power, or removed from sites in ways that were legal at the time but are now seen as unjust. Should they be returned to the places from which they came?
 
The debate has a long history, but it has gained force in recent decades. Countries such as Greece have long demanded the return of marble sculptures removed from the Parthenon in the early nineteenth century and displayed in London. Nigeria has asked for the return of the bronze plaques taken from the royal palace of Benin when British troops attacked the city in the late nineteenth century. Egypt, Ethiopia, and many others have made claims for objects held abroad. In some cases, there is no dispute about how the items were taken. They were the spoils of conquest.
 
Museums that hold these objects have traditionally advanced several arguments. The first is legal. Many items were acquired legitimately according to the laws of their time, and some museums are forbidden by their own statutes from giving away items in their collections. The second is that great museums offer a unique service. By gathering the achievements of many civilisations under one roof, they allow millions of visitors to compare and appreciate them, and their experts are able to preserve fragile works safely. Some also argue that the objects have become part of the history of the museum itself and of the countries that have cared for them.
 
Those who demand return give very different reasons. They stress that the objects are not merely art but part of the identity and heritage of living communities, and that their removal was an act of violence. To see one's ancestors' sacred or historic treasures displayed in a distant capital, they say, is a reminder of humiliation. Moreover, the argument that poorer countries cannot look after such items no longer holds, as modern museums are being built, such as the one in Benin City, specially to receive them.
 
Recent developments suggest that attitudes are shifting. Germany has agreed to hand back hundreds of bronzes to Nigeria, and France has passed laws to allow the return of certain items to former colonies. Universities and smaller museums have returned human remains and sacred objects to the communities from which they were taken. Several museums have also adopted new approaches, such as long-term loans, shared ownership, and joint exhibitions, which avoid the stark choice between keeping and giving.
 
The practical details are often more difficult than the principle. Proving where an object came from can take years of research in archives, and some records were never kept or were lost. Questions of ownership can be disputed between neighbouring countries, or between a national government and a local community that feels more closely tied to the object. There are also concerns about security and conservation, though these are sometimes used as an excuse. Many experts therefore favour case-by-case decisions guided by evidence and by consultation with the communities concerned, rather than a single rule applied to every object.
 
Yet the issue is complex. If every object taken in dubious circumstances were to be returned, many collections would be emptied, and it is not always clear to whom an object should be returned, as borders and governments have changed. Some people fear that return would reduce public access to world culture. Others reply that sharing treasures with people in their countries of origin is a form of access too.
 
Perhaps the most useful result of the debate is that it has forced institutions to examine their own histories honestly. Whatever the outcome in individual cases, museums can no longer treat the origins of their collections as an embarrassing secret, and the stories behind the objects are now a part of what they teach.
