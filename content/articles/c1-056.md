Parents of teenagers often despair at behaviour that seems irrational. A sixteen-year-old who understands perfectly well that a dangerous jump may end in injury will do it anyway, and the same young person who cannot remember to bring a homework book may spend hours perfecting a skill that interests him. For generations, adults explained such behaviour with the word "hormones". Over the past twenty years, brain scientists have offered a more precise and, in many ways, more sympathetic explanation: the brain of an adolescent is still under construction.

The key discovery, made with brain-scanning techniques, was that the brain does not reach full maturity in childhood. Instead, it continues to change well into the twenties. The most dramatic change occurs in the prefrontal cortex, the region behind the forehead that is responsible for planning, weighing consequences, and controlling impulses. During adolescence, this area undergoes a process of pruning, in which unused connections between neurons are removed, while the ones that remain are strengthened and insulated so that signals travel faster. The result is a more efficient system, but one that is not complete until about the age of twenty-five.

Meanwhile, the parts of the brain that respond to emotion and reward develop earlier. In particular, the circuits that react to the prospect of pleasure are unusually active in teenagers, who tend to experience rewards more intensely than either children or adults. This imbalance, between a strong accelerator and still imperfect brakes, helps to explain the pattern of risk-taking that is so familiar. It is particularly clear when friends are present. In one well-known experiment, volunteers played a driving simulation. When alone, teenagers took no more risks than adults, but when they thought that their friends were watching, they took significantly more.

It would be wrong, however, to describe adolescent behaviour only as a weakness. The same features that produce danger also drive exploration. Willingness to take risks leads young people to leave the safety of their family, to meet new people, and to try new things, which, in evolutionary terms, is exactly what is needed at the age at which an animal must find its own place in the world. Teenagers also learn extremely quickly, and their brains are especially sensitive to social information, which is why friendships and acceptance matter so much to them. Many creative and political movements have been driven by the energy and hopes of the young.

These findings have practical implications. They suggest that teenagers should be treated neither as children nor as small adults. For parents and teachers, it may be more useful to provide clear boundaries and a safe space to experiment than to rely on warnings alone, since a young person will find it hard to weigh a distant risk against an immediate thrill. Sleep is another factor, as adolescents need more of it than adults, and the natural shift in their body clocks toward later waking is often at odds with early school timetables.

The research also raises questions for the justice system. Several courts, including the United States Supreme Court, have cited brain science in decisions that limit the harshest punishments for crimes committed by young people, on the grounds that they are less able to control impulses and more open to change. Critics respond that the science describes averages, not individuals, and that many teenagers behave responsibly while some adults do not.

Finally, scientists urge caution about reading too much into brain scans. The evidence is largely based on groups, the differences between individuals are large, and culture plainly influences behaviour. Nevertheless, the main message is clear and encouraging. The adolescent brain is not broken but unfinished, and the years between childhood and adulthood are a period of great opportunity, as well as of risk.
