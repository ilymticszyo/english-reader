We walk on it, build on it, and often call it "dirt". Yet soil is one of the most complex and valuable things on Earth. A small amount of it may contain more living things than there are people on the planet. Almost all of our food depends on it, but few of us give it a second thought.

Soil is a mixture of tiny pieces of rock, decaying plant and animal matter, water, air, and an enormous number of living creatures. Tiny living things break down dead leaves and animals and release the nutrients that plants need. Worms dig tunnels that allow air and water to move through the ground, and they mix the layers as they eat. Underneath a forest, thin threads under the ground connect the roots of different trees, passing sugar and water between them. Some scientists call this network the "wood wide web", although they continue to debate how much it really resembles communication.

The process that creates soil is very slow. It can take hundreds of years to form just a centimetre of good earth, as rock is gradually broken down by wind, water, and the roots of plants. This means that soil is, in practice, a resource that cannot be renewed within a human lifetime. When it is lost, it is lost for good.

Unfortunately, it is being lost at a fast rate. When land is farmed heavily, the soil is often left bare, and without plants to hold it together, wind and rain carry it away. Heavy use of strong chemicals can damage the living community within it. Some experts estimate that a third of the world's farmland is already damaged. About ninety years ago, a combination of drought and poor farming methods turned parts of the American Midwest into a "Dust Bowl", forcing hundreds of thousands of families to leave their farms.

Soil also plays an important part in the climate. It contains more carbon than all the plants and the atmosphere combined. When healthy soil is protected, it stores this carbon. When it is dug up or destroyed, some of the carbon escapes into the air as carbon dioxide, which adds to global warming.

Fortunately, there are methods that help to protect and rebuild it. Farmers can plant "cover crops" between harvests, so that the soil is never bare. They can dig less, change the crops each year, and add natural material such as compost. Some farmers who have adopted these methods report that their soil holds more water, needs less chemicals, and produces equally good harvests. At home, gardeners can make a difference, too, by making compost from kitchen waste and by avoiding strong chemicals.

Soil rarely makes headlines. But as the global population grows, protecting it may be one of the most practical things we can do for the future of our food.
