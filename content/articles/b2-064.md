On the top of a supermarket in Paris, one of the biggest farm on a roofs in the world produces thousands of kilograms of vegetables each season. In an empty building in Singapore, tomatoes grow in layers under purple lights. And in many cities, from Detroit to Havana, vacant plots of land have been turned into community gardens. Urban agriculture, once a curiosity, is becoming a serious part of how cities think about food.

The main reason is simple. More than half of the world's population now lives in cities, and the number is rising. Most of their food travels long distances from farms. This depends on complicated chains of transport and storage, which can be disturbed by bad weather, strikes, or, as the pandemic showed, closed borders. Growing some food nearby can make a city less weak, and it also means that vegetables reach the table fresher.

There are many kinds of urban farm. The simplest are gardens in yards, on balconies, or on shared land, where residents grow vegetables for themselves. Farms on roofs use the empty space above buildings and can also help to cool the building below. At the high-tech end, "indoor farms" grow plants in stacked trays inside buildings, using artificial light and, often, a system that feeds the roots with water containing nutrients instead of soil. Because everything is controlled, such farms can produce food all year, use up to ninety percent less water than ordinary fields, and need no strong chemicals.

The benefits are not only practical. Community gardens bring neighbours together, particularly in places where people hardly know each other. They give young people the chance to learn where food comes from, and they provide green space in areas that have little. Several studies suggest that people who garden regularly eat more vegetables and enjoy better mental health. In poorer districts, access to fresh produce can make a real difference to diet.

However, urban farming has limits. Land in cities is costly, and a plot of vegetables competes with housing and offices. Indoor farms use a great deal of electricity for lighting and climate control, and unless that electricity comes from clean sources, the environmental benefit disappears. Their products also tend to be limited to leaves and herbs, as these are light and grow quickly, while foods like wheat, rice, and potatoes need much more space. Several well-known indoor farms have also struggled financially.

Another concern is safety. Soil in some cities contains lead and other pollutants from past industry, and food grown there must be tested or grown in raised beds with clean soil. 

In practice, cities will never feed themselves from their own rooftops. Studies suggest that urban farming could supply a modest share of a city's vegetables, but not its grain or meat. Yet its value goes beyond tonnes. It changes how city people think about food, brings nature into daily life, and makes food easier to see.

In that sense, the small garden and the high-tech farm have more in common than they seem. Both ask us to see the city not just as a place to consume, but also as a place that can produce.
