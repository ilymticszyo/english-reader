Few subjects arouse such strong feeling, or such confusion, as inequality. Newspapers regularly report that the gap between rich and poor has never been wider, and political movements around the world have made the claim central to their message. Yet economists who study the question often seem to give contradictory answers. Some say that inequality is rising dramatically; others say that it has fallen over recent decades. The explanation is that they are often talking about different things.

The first distinction is between inequality within countries and inequality between them. Within most wealthy countries, and in several large developing ones, the gap between the richest and the rest has widened since about 1980. In the United States, for example, the share of national income going to the top one percent has roughly doubled over that time, while the incomes of many middle-income families have grown slowly. A Nobel-winning study of long-term data has traced similar changes in several countries, arguing that the post-war decades, in which the gap narrowed, were an exception rather than the rule.

By contrast, inequality between countries has declined. The rapid growth of China, India, and other large economies has lifted hundreds of millions of people out of extreme poverty, and the average incomes of these nations have moved closer to those of the wealthy world. When people around the globe are counted as a single population, the picture is therefore more complicated: the poorest have gained, the middle has gained substantially, and the groups that have gained least are the lower-middle classes in rich countries. This pattern has been called the "elephant curve", after the shape of the graph that shows it.

A second distinction is between income and wealth. Income is what people earn in a year, while wealth is what they own, including savings, property, and investments. Wealth is much more unequally distributed than income, and it tends to be passed from generation to generation. A young person who inherits a house starts life with an enormous advantage over one who must rent, and in several countries, rising property prices have widened this gap.

Third, we need to ask what is being measured, and over what period. Statistics based on income before taxes and benefits give a different picture from those that include them. In some countries, the government's system of taxes and payments reduces inequality considerably; in others, it does little. Also, incomes change as people age. A student may be poor, while the same person at forty-five may earn a good salary, so snapshots can exaggerate the lasting differences between individuals. At the same time, studies of social mobility indicate that, in a number of rich countries, a child's chances depend strongly on the circumstances of his parents.

The causes of the recent rise are disputed. Candidates include technological change, which rewards people with high skills and replaces routine jobs; globalisation, which exposes some workers to competition from lower-paid labour abroad; the decline of unions; and changes in taxation that reduced rates for the highest earners. Most experts believe that several factors are involved, and that political decisions have mattered as much as economic forces.

Does it matter? Some argue that inequality is acceptable if everyone is becoming better off, and that attempts to reduce it may damage growth. Others reply that extreme gaps weaken trust, undermine democracy by giving the wealthy more political influence, and may harm health and opportunity for those at the bottom. The disagreement is partly about facts, and partly about values: how much difference between citizens a society is willing to accept.

Understanding the numbers, at least, is a necessary beginning. Before asking whether inequality is rising, we should ask which kind, between whom, and measured in what way.
