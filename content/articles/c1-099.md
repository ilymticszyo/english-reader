Throughout history, people dissatisfied with the world around them have dreamed of building a better one. The word "utopia" itself comes from a book published in 1516 by the English writer Thomas More, who described an imaginary island where property was shared, work was moderate, and everyone was educated. The name was a pun in Greek, meaning both "good place" and "no place". Perhaps More knew that the vision could never be fully realised. Yet many have tried, and the history of planned communities is a fascinating record of hope, ingenuity, and, often, disappointment.
 
In the nineteenth century, the spread of industry gave new urgency to the dream. Reformers were appalled by the poverty and disease of the factory towns and believed that society could be redesigned. The Welsh manufacturer Robert Owen built a model community around his cotton mills at New Lanark in Scotland, providing decent housing, schools, and shorter working hours, and later tried to found an entirely cooperative village in America, which failed within a few years. In France, the thinker Charles Fourier imagined communities in which people lived together in a great building and worked at tasks that suited their personalities. Several attempts to put his ideas into practice collapsed.
 
Other movements were inspired by religion. The Shakers, an American Christian group, lived in communal villages, shared their goods, and became famous for the quality of their furniture, though their commitment to celibacy meant that they gradually dwindled. The kibbutzim of Israel, collective farms founded in the early twentieth century, combined socialist principles with the effort to build a new nation, and for some decades, they were admired as examples of equality in practice. Most have since adopted more individual arrangements.
 
Planners also tried to build better cities. In 1898, the English reformer Ebenezer Howard proposed the "garden city", a town of limited size surrounded by countryside, combining the benefits of urban and rural life. Two such towns were built in England, and the idea influenced the design of suburbs and new towns around the world. In the twentieth century, the architect Le Corbusier proposed replacing old streets with tall towers set among parkland, an idea that influenced housing projects across Europe and America, although many residents found them cold and isolating. The city of Brasília, designed from scratch in the 1950s, was meant to embody a modern and rational society, but critics noted that it was built for cars rather than for people.
 
Why do so many of these experiments fail? Some collapsed from internal quarrels, when the enthusiasm of the founders was not shared by their followers, or from the difficulty of maintaining equality when people differ in talent and ambition. Others failed because the plans were too rigid, imposing a single vision on people who wanted to live in their own ways. The writer Jane Jacobs argued that cities succeed because of their complexity and variety, which cannot be designed from above. And the darker examples, in which utopian ideals were enforced by violence, show that the pursuit of perfection can become dangerous.
 
Yet it would be wrong to dismiss all this as folly. Many practical improvements, such as public parks, good housing, and shorter working hours, began as the dreams of reformers who were thought impractical. The utopian impulse expresses a legitimate desire to ask whether the world must be as it is.
 
Some modern experiments continue the tradition in quieter forms. Cohousing communities, in which families have private homes but share kitchens, gardens, and workshops, are spreading in Denmark, the Netherlands, and North America. Some villages are built to live with a small environmental footprint, and online groups design new kinds of cooperative living. These are modest projects, aware of the history of failure, and they make no claim to perfect society. Their aim is simply to show that everyday life can be organised differently, and that people can choose to share more than they are usually expected to.
 
The lesson may be that utopias are most useful as directions rather than destinations. They help us to imagine alternatives and to criticise what is wrong, so long as we do not demand perfection and are willing to learn from experience.
