It is hard to imagine a world without numbers. We use them to tell the time, to pay for food, to measure our height, and to count the days until a holiday. Yet numbers, as we know them, were not discovered in a single moment. They developed slowly, over thousands of years, and the story reveals how human thinking has changed.

The earliest counting was probably done with fingers and simple marks. Archaeologists have found bones with rows of scratches that are about forty thousand years old, and some believe that they were used to count days or animals. As societies became more complex, with farming, trade, and taxes, the need for a more reliable system grew. In ancient Mesopotamia, around five thousand years ago, merchants and officials pressed marks into soft tablets to record how much grain or how many sheep had been delivered.

Different peoples developed different systems. The Egyptians used pictures for big numbers, and the Romans used letters of their alphabet. The Roman method worked well for recording numbers, but it was clumsy for calculation. Try multiplying two large Roman numbers without translating them into modern ones, and you will see the problem.

The great breakthrough came from India. Around fifteen hundred years ago, Indian mathematicians developed a system in which the position of a sign determines its value, so that the "2" in 25 means twenty, but in 52 it means two. They also invented a symbol for nothing: zero. This idea seems simple, but it was revolutionary. Zero allowed the system to work with only ten symbols, made it possible to write very large numbers, and led to new kinds of mathematics. Without it, modern science and computing would be impossible.

The system travelled west through the Arab world. Scholars in Baghdad improved it and wrote important books on the subject, and the word "algebra" itself comes from the title of one of them. About eight hundred years ago, an Italian merchant named Fibonacci, who had learned the system while travelling in North Africa, wrote a famous book that explained its advantages to Europeans. Even so, acceptance was slow. Some governments forbade the new numbers, partly because it was easier to change a written zero into a nine than to alter a Roman letter, and partly because people did not trust anything unfamiliar. It took several centuries before the new numbers became standard in Europe.

Later developments added further tools. Negative numbers and new kinds of mathematics extended the system, and later, a system that uses only zero and one became the language of computers.

The story of numbers is a reminder that even the most basic ideas have a history. They were created by people, improved by many hands, and spread across continents, long before they appeared in a school notebook.
