A few years ago, a watch could tell you the time and little else. Today, the device on your wrist may count your steps, measure your heartbeat, track your sleep, and warn you if your pulse seems unusual. Wearable technology has turned a personal habit, looking after one's health, into a stream of data. The question is whether this data makes us healthier.

Most wearable devices rely on small sensors. A light on the underside of a watch shines onto the skin, and the amount that comes back changes with each heartbeat, allowing the device to calculate the pulse. Other sensors detect movement, which is how the device knows that you are walking, running, or asleep. More advanced models can take an electrical reading of the heart or measure the amount of oxygen in the blood.

For some people, the benefits are real. There are well-known cases in which a watch warned its owner of an irregular heartbeat, which led to a visit to a doctor and treatment that may have prevented a stroke. People with diabetes use small sensors on their arms to monitor their blood sugar without needing to prick a finger several times a day. Doctors can also use data from devices to follow patients who are recovering at home, which means shorter stays in hospital.

Wearables can also motivate healthy habits. Seeing your daily step count, or receiving a reminder to stand up after an hour of sitting, can encourage people to move more. Some studies have found that people who use such devices become more active, at least in the first months. Friends often compete with each other through apps, which adds a social element.

However, there are reasons for caution. The effect on motivation does not always last, and many devices end up in a drawer after a few months. Accuracy is another problem. Cheaper devices can make mistakes, especially for people with darker skin or during intense exercise, and the numbers on a screen can give a false sense of certainty. Some users become anxious when they check their sleep score every morning, and they actually sleep worse as a result.

Privacy is a further concern. Health information is very personal, and it is stored by companies whose main interest may be business rather than care. Users should ask who can see their data, whether it is sold, and what would happen if it were stolen. Laws in many countries are still catching up with the technology.

There is also an issue of fairness. Those who most need to improve their health are often those who cannot afford the devices, and a rise in the number of false alarms could put pressure on doctors, who must decide which warnings deserve attention.

In short, wearables are a useful tool, not a cure. They are best used as a way to start a conversation with a doctor, or to build a habit, rather than as a replacement for professional advice.
