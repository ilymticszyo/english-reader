Every year, newspapers publish predictions about the economy, politics, and technology, and every year, a large share of them turn out to be wrong. Few people seem surprised. Yet we continue to listen to confident forecasters, and we often reward those who sound the most certain. What does the evidence tell us about how good experts really are at predicting the future?

The most famous answer comes from the psychologist Philip Tetlock, who began a remarkable project in 1984. He asked several hundred professional commentators and advisers to make predictions about political and economic events, and then he waited. Over about twenty years, he collected tens of thousands of forecasts and compared them with what actually happened. The result was uncomfortable. On average, the experts performed only slightly better than random guessing, and some were no better than a simple rule that assumes tomorrow will resemble today.

Tetlock noticed, however, that the experts were not all equally poor. Borrowing a phrase from an ancient Greek poem, he divided them into "hedgehogs" and "foxes". Hedgehogs know one big thing: they hold a single powerful theory about how the world works, and they interpret every event through it. Foxes, by contrast, know many small things. They gather information from different sources, doubt their own ideas, and change their minds when the facts change. The foxes consistently produced better predictions, while the hedgehogs, though more entertaining on television, were frequently wrong. Ironically, the more famous an expert was, the less reliable the forecasts tended to be.

Why does expertise fail in this way? One reason is that complex systems, such as economies and societies, contain so many interacting parts that small events can produce large, unpredictable effects. A single decision, a chance meeting, or an unexpected invention may alter the course of history, but no model can include it in advance. Another reason is psychological. Humans are naturally drawn to stories, and a clear narrative feels more convincing than a collection of probabilities. Experts also suffer from hindsight, remembering their earlier predictions as more accurate than they were. When a forecast fails, they tend to explain that they were nearly right, or that the unexpected event was a special case.

Incentives play a part as well. A commentator who says "there is a sixty per cent chance" will not attract much attention, while one who declares that disaster is certain will be invited back. Because there is rarely a penalty for being wrong, and because nobody keeps score, bold claims are rewarded more often than careful ones.

Encouragingly, forecasting can be improved. In a later project funded by an intelligence agency, thousands of volunteers competed to predict world events, and a small group of "superforecasters" repeatedly outperformed professional analysts. Their habits were instructive. They broke large questions into smaller parts, searched for evidence that contradicted their first impression, expressed their views in precise numbers, and updated their estimates frequently. They were also modest. Most importantly, they treated their beliefs as hypotheses to be tested, not as possessions to be defended.

Another useful distinction is between questions where prediction is possible and those where it is not. Weather forecasters, for example, achieve respectable accuracy over a few days, because they receive immediate feedback, work with well-understood physical laws, and can compare thousands of past forecasts with the outcomes. By contrast, a prediction about whether a new technology will transform society, or whether a government will survive the next decade, offers few comparable cases and very slow feedback. The problem is therefore not that every expert is incompetent, but that many are asked to predict things that are not predictable, and they rarely admit it.

The lesson is not that we should ignore experts, who still know far more than the rest of us about their fields. It is that we should treat their predictions with sensible scepticism, ask them how confident they are, and prefer those who can admit uncertainty. In a world that is complicated and unpredictable, honesty about what we do not know may be the most reliable forecast of all.
