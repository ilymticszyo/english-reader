The bicycle looks so simple that it is hard to believe that it took people hundreds of years to invent it. Yet its history is full of strange machines, unexpected changes, and ideas that gave ordinary people a new kind of freedom.

The story usually begins in 1817, when a German inventor built a wooden machine with two wheels and no pedals. The rider sat on it and pushed along the ground with his feet. It appeared a year after a very cold summer had ruined harvests, and a popular story says that horses then died for lack of food, so people needed a cheaper way to travel, although historians doubt that this was the cause. The machine was fun, but it was uncomfortable, and the fashion did not last.

The next important step came in the 1860s, when French workers added pedals to the front wheel. To go faster, makers made the front wheel bigger and bigger, until the machine became the famous penny-farthing, with a huge wheel in front and a tiny one behind. It looked impressive but was dangerous. Riders sat high above the ground, and a stone in the road could throw them over the handlebars. Only young and brave men rode them.

The real breakthrough was the safety bicycle of the 1880s. It had two wheels of equal size, a chain that connected the pedals to the back wheel, and, soon after, air-filled tyres that made the ride much smoother. Suddenly, almost anybody could ride a bicycle. Prices fell, and by the 1890s, millions of people were cycling.

The bicycle changed society in ways that its inventors never expected. Workers could live farther from the factory, and young people from different villages could meet and marry, which, some historians say, made populations more varied. For women, it was especially important. It allowed them to travel alone and, because tight clothes were impossible on a bicycle, it helped to change fashion. One American campaigner for women's rights said in an interview in 1896 that the bicycle had done more for women's freedom than anything else in the world.

In the twentieth century, cars took over the roads, and in many countries, the bicycle became a toy for children or a sport for enthusiasts. But in recent decades, it has returned. Cities now build cycle lanes, and electric bicycles help people to climb hills without effort. Cycling is cheap, healthy, and produces no pollution, and it is often faster than a car in a crowded city.

After more than two hundred years, the basic design has barely changed. Few other inventions have been so simple, so useful, and so long-lasting.
