In 1800, only about one in ten people lived in a city. Today, more than half of humanity does, and by the middle of the century, the proportion is expected to reach two thirds. This shift, from fields to streets, from villages to apartment blocks, is one of the most important changes in human history. Its benefits are obvious and widely celebrated. Its costs, particularly for the places that people leave behind, are less often discussed.

The reasons for the movement are not difficult to identify. Cities offer jobs, education, healthcare, and a variety of experiences that rural areas cannot match. Mechanisation has reduced the number of workers needed on farms, so that a single farmer can now produce food for dozens of people, and younger generations naturally look for other opportunities. For many, especially young women, the city also means escape from restrictive traditions and a chance to choose their own lives. In developing countries, rural poverty is often severe, and migration is a rational response.

The economic evidence supports this view. Cities concentrate talent, ideas, and capital, which makes them more productive; people in cities tend to earn more than those who stay behind. Densely populated areas can also deliver services, such as water, electricity, and schools, more cheaply than scattered villages. Some environmental scientists add that compact cities, if well designed, use less land and energy per person than sprawling suburbs.

Yet for the countryside, the consequences can be severe. When young and educated people depart, they leave an ageing population, falling school enrolments, and closing shops. In parts of Europe, East Asia, and North America, villages are literally disappearing, and some regions now contain more empty houses than inhabitants. Services that rely on a minimum number of users, such as buses, clinics, and post offices, withdraw, making life harder for those who remain and encouraging yet more people to leave. The process feeds itself.

There is also a loss that is hard to measure. Rural communities carry knowledge about land, local crops, and traditional building and cooking, and these skills are not easily recorded. Festivals, dialects, and customs that grew from agricultural life fade when the people who maintain them move away. Some observers see an attempt to preserve rural heritage as sentimental, arguing that nostalgia should not hold back people who want better lives. Others reply that a society that forgets how to farm sustainably, or that no longer values the knowledge of its elders, becomes poorer in ways that statistics do not capture.

The picture is not entirely one-way. In recent years, some people have moved back, drawn by cheaper housing, cleaner air, and, since the pandemic, the possibility of working from home. Governments have tried to encourage this with fast internet, tax benefits, and programmes to attract young families and entrepreneurs. Some villages have succeeded, through tourism, specialised food products, or by becoming centres of renewable energy. But such successes remain exceptions, and many efforts have failed because jobs and services follow people, not the other way round.

A balanced view might accept that urbanisation will continue, while insisting that the countryside should not be treated as a mere supplier of food and a place for weekend visits. Rural areas deserve investment in transport, broadband, health, and education, not out of charity but because a healthy relationship between city and country serves both. Cities depend on the land for their food, water, and recreation, and the people who work it have a legitimate claim to a decent life. Experience from countries such as South Korea and Japan, where rural depopulation has been especially rapid, suggests that governments act best when they start early, before the last school and the last clinic have closed.
