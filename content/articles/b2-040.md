Some of Europe's most impressive buildings are more than eight hundred years old. The great cathedrals of cities such as Paris, Chartres, and Canterbury rise high above the surrounding streets, with tall towers, huge windows of coloured glass, and stone ceilings that seem to float in the air. They were built by people who had no electricity, no cranes with engines, and no modern mathematics. How did they do it?

First, we need to ask why they wanted to. In the Middle Ages, religion was at the centre of life, and a cathedral was the most important building in a city. It was a place for prayer, but also for meetings, markets, and celebrations. A beautiful cathedral also brought a town honour and attracted pilgrims, whose visits brought business to local shops and inns. Rivalry played a part, too. Cities competed to build higher towers, and some of the plans were so ambitious that they could not be finished in the lifetime of the people who started them.

The work took a very long time. Many cathedrals needed a hundred years or more, and some were never completed. A master mason, who was part architect and part manager, led the work. He did not use detailed drawings as we do today. Instead, he used simple geometry, with ropes, compasses, and wooden patterns, to find the shape of each part. Teams of skilled stone workers cut the blocks in workshops, and each worker often marked his stones with a personal sign so that he could be paid for them.

Lifting the stones was a slow and dangerous job. Workers used a wooden wheel, in which men walked like hamsters, to turn a rope and lift heavy blocks to the top of the walls. Scaffolding was also made of wood, and accidents must have been frequent, although few records of them survive.

The most important technical idea was the pointed arch. It spreads the weight of the roof more evenly than a round arch, so walls can be thinner and taller. Builders then added "flying buttresses", stone supports on the outside, that push against the walls and stop them from falling outward. With this system, the walls no longer had to carry all the weight, and builders could fill the space between with enormous windows. When the sun shone through the coloured glass, the whole interior glowed.

Today, cathedrals continue to cause amazement. They need constant care, since stone wears away, and wars and fires have destroyed or damaged many of them. When the Paris cathedral, Notre-Dame, caught fire in 2019, people around the world watched in horror, and large sums of money were quickly offered for its repair.

These buildings remind us that people can achieve extraordinary things when they share a vision, even if they will never see it finished.
