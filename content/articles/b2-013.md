Ten years ago, few people had heard the phrase "gig economy". Today, it is part of everyday life. When you order a taxi from your phone, ask someone to deliver your dinner, or hire a person online to design a logo, you are using it. In this system, workers do not hold permanent jobs. Instead, they take short tasks, or "gigs", often found through an app, and are paid for each one.

Supporters of this model emphasise freedom. A driver can decide to work for three hours or ten, on a weekday or at night. A student can earn extra money between lessons, and a parent can fit work around school hours. For people who dislike a fixed schedule, or who cannot find a traditional job, the gig economy offers a way to earn money quickly, with few requirements.

Companies also benefit. They do not need to pay for offices, equipment, or training, and they can increase or reduce the number of workers according to demand. They argue that this efficiency allows them to offer cheaper services to customers.

However, critics see the situation very differently. Most gig workers are classified as independent contractors rather than employees. This means that they usually receive no paid holidays, no sick pay, and no pension. If a worker is ill or has an accident, the income stops immediately. Because pay is often calculated per task, the hourly rate can fall below the minimum wage once waiting time and costs, such as fuel and repairs, are included.

There is also the question of control. Although the workers are described as independent, many apps decide the price of each job, measure performance through customer ratings, and can remove a person from the platform without warning. Some workers say that they feel pressure to accept every offer, in case their rating drops. This is a strange kind of freedom.

Governments around the world are trying to find a balance. Some courts have ruled that certain drivers should be treated as employees, while other places have created a new category of worker with limited rights. The companies often resist these changes, claiming that they would destroy the flexibility that customers and workers like.

The truth is probably somewhere in the middle. For some people, short-term work is a useful extra; for others, it is the only option available, and the lack of protection can be dangerous. As more of our economy moves online, the key question is how to keep the benefits of flexibility without leaving workers to carry all the risk.
