For most of the twentieth century, economists assumed that the best measure of a society's progress was its income. A richer country, it seemed, was a better one. Gross domestic product, the total value of goods and services produced, became the central statistic of politics, and governments competed to increase it. Yet in recent decades, a growing group of researchers has asked a simple but unsettling question: do people actually become happier as they become richer?

The first serious challenge came in the 1970s, when an American economist noticed that, although incomes in the United States had risen sharply after the Second World War, surveys showed no increase in the proportion of people who described themselves as happy. Comparing countries, he found that richer ones were generally happier than poorer ones, but only up to a point. Beyond a certain level, extra income seemed to make little difference. This finding, later called the Easterlin paradox, provoked a fierce debate that continues today.

Later studies have complicated the picture. Some researchers, using much larger data sets, have found that happiness continues to rise with income, even at high levels, though the gains become smaller, since each additional pound or dollar matters less to a rich person than to a poor one. Others point out that, within a country, a person's happiness depends not only on how much he earns, but on how that compares with the incomes of others. People care about their relative position: a rise in salary brings less pleasure if neighbours and colleagues are receiving the same rise. This helps to explain why growth does not always lead to greater contentment.

If money is only part of the story, what else matters? The evidence points to a small number of consistent factors. The most important is relationships. A long study of adults followed over more than eighty years found that the quality of a person's close relationships was the best predictor of health and happiness in old age, better than wealth, fame, or social class. Good health, a sense of control over one's life, trust in others, and meaningful work are also strongly linked to wellbeing. Unemployment, by contrast, reduces happiness far more than the loss of income alone would suggest, because it takes away structure, status, and purpose.

These findings have begun to influence policy. Several governments now measure wellbeing alongside economic output, and some have introduced programmes that aim to improve it directly. A small Himalayan kingdom famously declared that "gross national happiness" was more important than gross national product, and a number of countries have since adopted indicators of life satisfaction. Supporters argue that such measures encourage policies that improve the quality of life, such as mental health services, green spaces, and flexible working, rather than simply increasing production.

Critics offer several objections. Happiness is subjective and hard to compare across cultures, and people may answer survey questions differently depending on their mood that day. There is also the danger that a government obsessed with the happiness of its citizens could become intrusive, or could ignore forms of progress, such as justice or freedom, that are not reflected in a score. Moreover, a country's wealth does matter for many things that make a good life possible, including medicine and education, and poverty is clearly associated with misery.

What emerges from this research is a more modest and, perhaps, more honest view. Income is important, particularly for those who do not have enough. But beyond the point of security, a society that wishes to improve the lives of its members will do well to attend to time, health, community, and the quality of relationships, which no growth rate can buy. Perhaps the most striking finding is how little we adapt to some conditions: people grow used to a bigger house very quickly, but not to a long journey to work or to constant noise.
