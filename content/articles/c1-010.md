On a summer afternoon, a city can be several degrees hotter than the countryside around it. This phenomenon, known as the urban heat island effect, is easy to understand. Dark roofs, asphalt roads, and concrete walls absorb sunlight during the day and release it slowly at night, while the scarcity of vegetation reduces the cooling that plants provide by releasing water vapour. Tall buildings trap warm air between them, and air conditioners, cars, and factories add yet more heat. The result is an environment in which residents get little relief even after dark.

For a long time, this was treated as a minor discomfort. As the climate warms and heat waves become more frequent, it is increasingly recognised as a serious threat to public health. High temperatures place strain on the heart and lungs, and they are particularly dangerous for the elderly, for small children, and for people with chronic illnesses. In several European heat waves over the past two decades, tens of thousands of additional deaths were recorded, and many victims were older people living alone in hot apartments. Heat also reduces productivity, disrupts sleep, and increases the demand for electricity at exactly the moment that power grids are most stressed.

What makes the problem especially troubling is that it is distributed unfairly. Studies in many cities show that wealthier neighbourhoods tend to have more trees, more parks, and better-insulated housing, while poorer districts are paved and exposed. In some American cities, the difference between two areas on the same afternoon has been measured at more than ten degrees. Researchers have traced part of this pattern to historical decisions, such as discriminatory housing policies decades ago, whose effects are still visible in the pattern of shade on the map.

Fortunately, there are practical remedies, and many are surprisingly simple. The most effective is probably planting trees. A mature tree provides shade, which reduces the temperature of the surface beneath it far more than the air above, and it also cools its surroundings through evaporation. Some cities have set targets for the proportion of their area covered by a canopy of leaves, and a few have mapped the hottest streets to decide where to plant first. In Colombia, the city of Medellín connected dozens of green corridors along roads and waterways, and officials reported a drop in local temperatures of roughly two degrees.

Other measures target the buildings themselves. Painting roofs white or covering them with reflective material sends sunlight back into the sky instead of absorbing it. Green roofs and walls add insulation and vegetation. Light-coloured pavements, permeable surfaces that allow rain to soak through, and shaded bus stops all contribute. Some architects have revived older traditions, such as narrow, shaded alleys and courtyards with fountains, which were developed in hot regions long before electric cooling existed.

None of these solutions is free from difficulty. Trees need water and care, and in dry regions, planting too many can strain scarce resources. Reflective roofs may slightly increase the need for heating in winter. Space in dense city centres is limited, and the benefits of shade take years to arrive, which makes it hard to persuade politicians who think in terms of election cycles. There is also the risk that improvements make an area more attractive and, by raising property prices, push out the very residents who needed them most.

The question of who pays for cooling deserves particular attention. In many places, the burden currently falls on individual households, which buy air conditioners to escape the heat. This is understandable, but it creates a vicious circle: air conditioners expel warm air into the street, raising the outdoor temperature further and increasing the electricity demand that causes more emissions. Households that cannot afford the equipment, meanwhile, are left to endure the heat. Public investment in shade, parks, and reflective surfaces therefore works as a form of collective insurance, protecting those who would otherwise be most exposed and reducing the strain on the entire energy system.

Nonetheless, the case for action is strong. Cooling a city does not require a technological miracle; it requires the patient, deliberate redesign of ordinary streets. A well-shaded street is not a luxury. As temperatures rise, it may prove to be one of the most important pieces of public infrastructure a community can build, and one that benefits everyone who walks beneath it.
