For many people, a credit card is simply a convenient piece of plastic. It lets you buy something now and pay for it later, and it often brings rewards such as points or cash back. But behavioural scientists have found that paying by card changes how we think about money in ways that we do not always notice.

One of the best-known findings is that people spend more when they pay with a card than when they pay with cash. In a series of experiments, researchers offered the same items to two groups of customers. Those who could use a card were willing to pay much more, sometimes twice as much, for the same product. The explanation seems to be psychological. Handing over notes and coins is a visible loss. Swiping a card feels less real, and the pain of paying is delayed until the bill arrives.

This delay encourages what economists call present bias. We tend to give too much weight to the enjoyment of buying something today and too little to the cost that we will face next month. When the bill comes, many people find that they have spent more than they intended. If they cannot pay in full, they are charged interest, which in some countries is very high. A small debt can grow quickly, and it can take years to clear.

Rewards schemes add another effect. Points and cash back make spending feel like earning. A person may choose a more expensive shop or buy something they do not need in order to collect a bonus. But the value of such rewards is usually small compared with the extra amount spent, and with the interest paid by those who carry a balance.

Of course, credit cards have real advantages. They are safer than carrying cash, and they offer protection if goods are faulty or never arrive. For those who pay the full amount every month, they cost nothing and may bring modest benefits. Cards also help people to build a record of reliable payments, which can be useful when applying for a loan.

How can we use cards wisely? Some people set a monthly budget and check their statement every week. Others use a debit card, which takes money straight from the account, so that spending is limited to what they have. An automatic payment that clears the full balance each month can prevent interest. Even simple habits, such as asking oneself whether a purchase is really needed, can help.

Money is not just a matter of numbers. How we pay shapes how we feel about what we spend, and understanding this is a step toward better control.
