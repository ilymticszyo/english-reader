Anyone who has wandered through the old centre of a Mediterranean city, such as Naples, Marseille, or Istanbul, will remember the experience of narrow streets, crowded markets, balconies hung with washing, and squares in which neighbours gather in the evening. These places seem to have grown naturally, in the way that coral reefs grow, rather than being designed. In an age of planned suburbs and shopping malls, they exert a powerful attraction, and visitors often ask what it is that makes such places feel so alive.
 
Part of the answer lies in history. The cities around the Mediterranean are among the oldest in the world, and many have been continuously inhabited for thousands of years. Greeks, Romans, Arabs, Venetians, and Ottomans have all left their marks, and the result is a layering of styles, in which a Roman column may support a medieval wall, itself part of a house with a modern shop at the bottom. Such cities are not museums, because the buildings remain in use, and history is not separate from daily life but woven through it.
 
Climate has also shaped them. In regions of hot summers, narrow, winding streets provide shade, and thick walls and small windows keep houses cool. Courtyards and rooftops serve as extra living space, so that a great deal of life takes place outdoors, in view of neighbours. This public character of life, combined with the compactness of the city, encourages spontaneous encounters. People know the baker, the shopkeeper, and the old man who sits outside the café every afternoon, and this familiarity creates a sense of security that no police force can supply.
 
Urban theorists have studied these qualities with admiration. The writer Jane Jacobs argued that successful city districts are those with a mixture of uses, short blocks, old buildings, and a high density of people, so that streets are busy at all hours and many "eyes" watch over them. Traditional Mediterranean quarters, by accident rather than design, fit this description well. Walking is the natural form of movement, shops are within a few minutes' reach, and the line between public and private space is blurred in a way that encourages community.
 
But the picture should not be romanticised. Many of these neighbourhoods are poor, and the buildings that charm visitors can be damp, cramped, and dangerous in earthquakes. Many residents would be glad to exchange their historic apartments for modern comforts. In addition, the popularity of such places has become a threat to them. Tourism has driven up rents in cities such as Venice and Barcelona, forcing out the very residents who gave the districts their character, and replacing grocers and hardware shops with souvenir stalls and rented holiday flats. Some cities have begun to limit the number of visitors or to restrict short-term rentals, in an effort to protect their communities.
 
The lesson for other places is not to copy the appearance of such towns, since a new development with imitation cobblestones feels artificial, but to understand the qualities underneath. Mixed use, human scale, walkable streets, and public spaces in which people can meet are possible anywhere, in new districts as well as old ones, and several modern cities have restored them by closing streets to cars or converting car parks into squares.
 
Residents of such districts often say that they would not exchange the noise and crowding for the quiet of a suburb, because the street gives them company, and a feeling that they belong to something larger than their own front door. Home, in the end, is more than a building. It is a web of relationships and routines, of familiar faces and the memory of places, and the best of the old Mediterranean cities show how the design of streets can support such a web, or destroy it. That is why the protection of such places matters, not as scenery for visitors, but as living examples of how people can share space and look after one another.
