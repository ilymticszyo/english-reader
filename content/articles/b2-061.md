Ask any parent of a teenager about mornings, and you will probably hear the same story. The child who used to jump out of bed at six o'clock now has to be pulled from under the blanket, and spends the first lesson of the day half asleep. It is easy to blame laziness or late nights on the phone. But scientists now believe that something more fundamental is happening: the body clock of a teenager really does change.

All of us have a daily rhythm, controlled by a small part of the brain, that tells us when to feel awake and when to feel sleepy. During puberty, this clock shifts. The brain starts to produce the hormone that makes us sleepy about two hours later than it does in younger children or in adults. As a result, a typical teenager does not feel tired until eleven o'clock at night or even later, and would naturally wake up around nine or ten in the morning. This shift is biological and appears in many cultures, and even in other mammals.

The problem is that schools often start early, usually between eight and eight thirty, and in some places, much earlier. A student who goes to bed at midnight but must get up at six thirty gets only six and a half hours of sleep, while experts recommend between eight and ten for teenagers. Studies suggest that most teenagers in wealthy countries are chronically short of sleep. Some researchers compare this to travelling across several time zones every Monday, and never recovering.

The effects of too little sleep are serious. Tired students find it harder to concentrate, remember information, and control their emotions. They are more likely to be involved in accidents, especially when driving, and more likely to suffer from depression and anxiety. Teachers report that students are less alert in the morning, and that more of them fall asleep in class.

In response, several school districts have experimented with later start times. The results are encouraging. In one large study in the United States, schools that moved their start time by an hour or more reported that students slept longer, were less often late, and had fewer car accidents.

Yet change is not simple. Later lessons mean later finishes, which can interfere with sport, part-time jobs, and the timetables of buses and of parents who must be at work early. Some argue that teenagers should simply learn to go to bed earlier. But although better habits can help, they cannot completely overcome a shift in the body clock.

There are also things that families can do. Limiting screens in the hour before bed is useful, because bright light tells the brain that it is still day. Keeping a regular timetable, even at weekends, helps the body clock, and so does getting daylight in the morning. A quiet, dark bedroom matters, too.

The central message is that a sleepy teenager is not necessarily a lazy one. If adults treat sleep as a health issue rather than a matter of character, young people may have the chance to learn and grow at their best.
