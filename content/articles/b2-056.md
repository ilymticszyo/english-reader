Few diets have received as much praise from doctors as the Mediterranean diet. It is regularly named among the healthiest ways to eat, and studies have linked it to a lower risk of heart disease, certain cancers, and early death. Yet, like many popular ideas, it is sometimes misunderstood. What does the diet really involve, and how strong is the evidence?

The term was first used in the 1960s by an American scientist, Ancel Keys, who noticed that people in southern Italy and Greece suffered far less from heart disease than people in the United States, despite having little money and doing hard physical work. He and his colleagues began to study the eating habits of these communities, and they found a pattern that was quite different from that of northern countries.

The traditional Mediterranean way of eating is based on plants. Vegetables, fruit, beans, nuts, and whole grains form the main part of each meal. The main source of fat is olive oil, rather than butter. Fish and seafood are eaten regularly, but meat and sweets are enjoyed only occasionally, and dairy products, such as cheese and yoghurt, are eaten in moderate amounts. Many people drink a small glass of wine with their meals, although experts now doubt that alcohol is beneficial and certainly do not suggest that people who do not drink should start.

What does the evidence say? The strongest support comes from a large trial in Spain, which began in 2003. Thousands of people at high risk of heart disease were divided into groups. Those who followed a Mediterranean diet, with extra olive oil or nuts, had around thirty percent fewer heart attacks and strokes than those who were advised to eat less fat. Other studies suggest that the diet may also protect the brain and help to prevent diabetes.

Scientists believe that the benefits come from the whole pattern rather than from any single food. Olive oil and nuts contain healthy fats. Vegetables, fruit, and beans provide fibre, vitamins, and substances that may protect the body's cells from damage. Eating little processed food and sugar also helps to keep weight under control.

However, there are some limits to the evidence. Much of the research has been done by observing what people eat and what happens to their health later, and such studies cannot prove cause. People who follow the diet may also have other healthy habits, such as exercising, not smoking, and eating meals with family. In addition, the way of life in the Mediterranean has itself changed. In some parts of Greece and Italy, many young people now eat more fast food than their grandparents, and rates of obesity have risen.

There are practical questions as well. Olive oil, fish, and fresh vegetables can be expensive in some countries, and the diet may not suit everyone's culture or taste. Fortunately, the idea can be adapted. In other places, people can use local vegetables, beans, and grains and choose cheaper kinds of fish.

The main lesson is simple, and perhaps more useful than any strict rules: eat more plants, less processed food, and enjoy meals with other people.
