The principle that people should be free to express their opinions without fear of punishment is among the foundations of modern democracy. Philosophers have long defended it on the grounds that truth emerges from open argument, that individuals deserve respect, and that governments cannot be trusted to decide which ideas are acceptable. Yet no society has ever permitted unlimited speech. Even the strongest defenders of free expression accept that people may not incite violence, spread deliberate lies that cause harm, or threaten others. The difficult questions concern where to draw the line, and, in the age of the internet, who should draw it.

For most of history, the main danger to free speech was the state. Rulers imprisoned or silenced critics, censored newspapers, and burned books. In many countries, this remains true. But in democratic ones, a new kind of problem has appeared. Much of public conversation now takes place on platforms owned by private companies, and these businesses make daily decisions about which content to permit, promote, or remove. Since they are not governments, they are not bound by constitutional protections for speech, and they can set whatever rules they wish. Yet their influence over public debate can exceed that of many states.

The scale of the task is astonishing. Platforms host billions of messages every day, in hundreds of languages, and they cannot read them all. They rely on a combination of automated systems and human moderators, who must judge whether a post contains hate, harassment, or false information, often in a few seconds and with little knowledge of the cultural context. Mistakes are inevitable: some harmful material remains online, while legitimate speech is sometimes removed. Moderators themselves can suffer lasting psychological damage from the images they see.

Many people argue that platforms should do more to remove abuse. Online harassment can drive people, especially women and members of minority groups, out of public life, and the result is that their voices are lost. In this view, a failure to act is not neutral. It means that those who shout loudest, and those who are most cruel, silence everyone else. False information, such as dangerous medical advice, can also have real consequences, and some insist that platforms have a responsibility to reduce its spread.

Others warn that over-zealous moderation is also dangerous. Rules that seem reasonable can be applied unevenly, and they may suppress unpopular but legitimate opinions, such as criticism of governments or discussion of controversial science. Opinions that were once considered false have sometimes turned out to be correct, and a system in which a handful of companies decides what is true would be an alarming concentration of power. For this reason, some defenders of free speech favour a minimal approach, removing only illegal content, and trusting users to counter bad ideas with better ones.

Governments have begun to intervene, and their approaches differ widely. Some countries, such as those in the European Union, have introduced rules that require large platforms to remove illegal content quickly, to explain their decisions, and to give users a way to appeal. Others, with authoritarian systems, use the language of safety to demand the removal of any criticism of the authorities. This is a reminder that regulation can protect speech or threaten it, depending on who makes the rules and how they are checked.

Technology complicates matters further. Artificial intelligence can now generate convincing text, images, and video, which makes it cheaper than ever to produce false material at scale. At the same time, the same tools can help to detect it. The old idea that more speech is the remedy for bad speech assumes that audiences can tell the difference, and that assumption is being tested.

No simple formula exists. A sensible approach is likely to combine clear rules, transparency about enforcement, the right of appeal, and independent oversight, together with education that helps people to judge what they read. Free speech has never been self-protecting. Each generation has to decide, again, how to defend it.
