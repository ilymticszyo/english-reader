Anyone who has flown between two cities that are only a few hundred kilometres apart will recognise the experience. The journey to the airport takes an hour, the security procedure another, the flight itself barely long enough for the cabin crew to serve a drink, and the final transfer into the city centre adds yet more time. Door to door, the entire trip may take as long as a good train, and it generates many times more carbon dioxide per passenger. As governments try to reduce emissions from transport, short-haul aviation has become a symbol of the choices, and the contradictions, of climate policy.
 
The environmental argument is straightforward. Per passenger and per kilometre, a short flight emits roughly several times as much carbon dioxide as an electric train, and, since take-off and climb consume a disproportionate amount of fuel, short flights are less efficient than long ones. Aviation as a whole accounts for a modest share of global emissions, perhaps two to three percent, but its importance is greater than the figure suggests. It is growing quickly, it is difficult to decarbonise, and a large proportion of flights are taken by a small, wealthy minority of the population. Moreover, the impact of aircraft on the climate is increased by effects other than carbon dioxide, such as the clouds that condense behind the engines at high altitude.
 
Some countries have therefore started to act. France passed a law banning domestic flights on routes where a train journey of less than two and a half hours is available, although exemptions reduced its scope considerably. Austria's national airline agreed to stop flying a route that a fast train could cover in three hours, as a condition of a government rescue during the pandemic. Several cities in Europe have introduced night train services, and some campaigners are calling for a tax on aviation fuel, which is exempt from the duties that apply to petrol and diesel.
 
Yet banning flights is not as simple as it sounds. The first obstacle is that, in many places, rail is not a realistic alternative. Trains may be slow, infrequent, or expensive, and where a line has to cross mountains or water, it may not exist at all. Tickets for international rail travel are often difficult to purchase, with different companies using different systems, and a flight can cost a fraction of the corresponding rail fare. Aviation benefits from decades of public investment in airports and from the absence of fuel taxation, while railways are expected to cover much of their own costs. Persuading people to take the train means addressing these imbalances, not merely forbidding the alternative.
 
Critics of bans also raise the question of fairness and of connectivity. Many short flights are feeders, carrying passengers from small cities to large hubs where they join long-distance services. If these flights disappear, travellers must reach the hub by other means, which may be impossible for those in remote areas. Island communities, in particular, often have no practical option except the aircraft. Business groups warn that reducing connections could damage regional economies.
 
Still, the potential for change is considerable. High-speed rail has already driven airlines out of some of the busiest corridors in the world: on the route between Paris and Lyon, or Madrid and Barcelona, trains now carry the large majority of travellers. Investment in new lines, simpler ticketing, and attractive night services could widen the range of journeys for which the train is the natural choice. Technology may help in the longer term, since electric and hydrogen aircraft are being developed for short routes, though they are unlikely to carry significant numbers of passengers for some time.
 
The debate over short flights is, in the end, a test of whether societies can make sustainable options not only virtuous but convenient. When the train is faster, cheaper, and easier, few people will need to be persuaded.
