Every night, you have a strange experience. You lie down, close your eyes, and, after a while, you find yourself in a world where houses have no walls, friends have different faces, and you can sometimes fly. Then you wake up and, in most cases, forget almost everything. Dreams have fascinated people for thousands of years, yet scientists are still debating what they are for.

We know a few things for certain. Most vivid dreaming happens during a stage of sleep in which the eyes move quickly beneath the eyelids, known as REM sleep. During this stage, the brain is nearly as active as when we are awake, but the body is mostly unable to move. This is probably a safety mechanism. If your muscles worked normally, you might jump out of bed whenever you dreamed that you were running away from a tiger.

Beyond these facts, several theories compete. One of the oldest comes from the Austrian doctor Sigmund Freud, who argued around 1900 that dreams are disguised expressions of our hidden desires. For many decades, this idea was extremely influential. Today, most researchers regard it as too difficult to test, and they prefer explanations based on the way the brain works.

One popular modern theory is that dreaming helps with memory. While we sleep, the brain seems to sort the information it collected during the day, strengthening the important parts and discarding the rest. Experiments show that people who sleep after learning something often remember it better than those who stay awake. Dreams may be a side effect of this sorting process, or they may even help it by replaying experiences in a mixed-up way.

Another idea is that dreams help us to deal with emotions. Studies have found that people who have had a stressful day often dream about the same worries, but in a less intense form. Some scientists believe that this is a kind of night-time therapy, which allows the brain to process feelings without the full stress of reality. This might explain why we feel better after "sleeping on" a problem.

A third theory suggests that dreams have no purpose at all. According to this view, the brain simply produces random signals during sleep, and the mind tries to turn them into a story, in the same way that we see faces in clouds. The stories feel meaningful only because we cannot help searching for meaning.

It is possible that all of these explanations contain some truth. What is clear is that sleep matters. People who do not sleep enough find it harder to learn, to control their mood, and to stay healthy, and dreaming seems to be part of the package.

So the next time you wake up with a strange story in your head, enjoy it. It may be a message from your hidden desires, or just the brain tidying up after a long day.
