Try to imagine a night without electric light. For most of history, when the sun went down, people used candles, fires, or oil lamps. These gave a weak light, and they were often dangerous. Today, we turn on the light with one finger. Who made this possible?

Many people say that the light bulb was invented by an American, Thomas Edison. But the true story is more complicated. Before Edison, other inventors in several countries had already made electric lights. One of them was an English scientist, Joseph Swan, who made a working bulb in the 1860s. The first bulbs did not last long, and they were too expensive.

Edison's great skill was to make the idea practical. In 1879, he and his team in New Jersey began to test hundreds of different materials for the thin line inside the bulb that gives out light. Some burned out in minutes. At last, they found that a thin piece of burnt thread could shine for many hours. It was a big step.

But a good bulb was not enough. Nobody had a way to bring electricity into people's homes. So Edison and his men built the first power station in New York in 1882. Soon, the first customers could use electric lights, and the idea began to spread across the city.

Light bulbs became cheaper and brighter, and soon millions of homes had electric light.

The changes in daily life were huge. People could work, study, and play after dark. Streets became safer, and shops could stay open late. Factories could run all day and all night.

The story of the light bulb shows that great inventions are rarely the work of one person. They come from many people, each adding a small step.
