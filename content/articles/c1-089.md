For as long as humans have lived on Earth, they have moved in response to changes in their surroundings. Droughts have driven farmers from their fields, floods have forced villages to relocate, and shifts in the climate have emptied whole regions. What is new today is the scale and speed of the changes that scientists expect. As the planet warms, many people will find that their homes become too hot, too dry, or too exposed to rising seas, and some will have no choice but to leave.
 
Estimates of how many people may be affected vary widely. A report by the World Bank suggested that, without strong action, well over a hundred million people could be displaced within their own countries by the middle of the century, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Latin America. Other estimates are higher or lower, depending on assumptions about emissions, adaptation, and population growth. All such figures must be treated with caution. People rarely decide to move for a single reason, and it is difficult to separate the influence of climate from that of poverty, conflict, and politics.
 
Some effects are already visible. Island nations in the Pacific, such as Kiribati and Tuvalu, face the loss of land as sea levels rise, and their governments have begun to discuss the long-term relocation of their people. In Bangladesh, where millions live on low-lying river deltas, floods and coastal erosion have pushed families toward the capital. In the Sahel, a belt of semi-arid land south of the Sahara, repeated droughts have reduced harvests and herds, and many young men have travelled to the cities or abroad in search of work. Wildfires and hurricanes have forced temporary evacuations in wealthy countries too, and some residents of the American coast have chosen not to rebuild.
 
It is important to note that most climate migration occurs within countries rather than between them. People generally prefer to stay close to home if they can, and moving long distances is expensive. In fact, the poorest are often the least able to move at all, trapped in places that are becoming uninhabitable. This has led some researchers to speak of "trapped populations" as a concern equal to that of migrants.
 
The legal situation is uncertain. The international convention on refugees, adopted after the Second World War, protects people who flee persecution, but it makes no mention of those who flee environmental disaster, and most governments are reluctant to extend its definition. A few have taken limited steps. New Zealand has considered a special visa for Pacific islanders, and regional agreements in Africa permit people to cross borders in times of crisis. Yet there is no comprehensive framework, and the political climate in many wealthy countries is not favourable to more immigration.
 
What can be done? The most important response is to reduce emissions, which will limit the scale of the problem. Alongside this, investment in adaptation, such as flood defences, drought-resistant crops, and better early warning systems, can allow people to remain where they are. Where movement is unavoidable, planned relocation, carried out in consultation with the communities concerned, is far less damaging than chaotic flight. Cities that expect new arrivals can prepare housing, jobs, and services in advance.
 
The question of responsibility also deserves attention. The countries that have contributed least to global warming are often the ones facing its worst effects, while the wealthy countries that emitted the most are better able to protect themselves. For this reason, many developing nations argue that rich countries have a moral duty to pay for adaptation and to share the burden of receiving displaced people. Negotiations over a special fund for losses and damage, agreed in principle at recent climate conferences, reflect this argument, though the money promised so far is small compared with the needs.
 
Climate migration is sometimes presented as a threat, but it may be better understood as a test of foresight and solidarity. Whether it becomes a humanitarian disaster or a managed transition will depend on choices made long before the first families pack their bags.
