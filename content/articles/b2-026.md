When we say that a meal is delicious, we usually think that the tongue is doing all the work. In fact, taste is a much more complicated experience, and it involves the nose, the eyes, the memory, and even the sounds around us.

The tongue is covered with thousands of tiny bumps, and inside them are the taste buds. Each of these contains cells that react to chemicals in our food. For a long time, people believed that there were four basic tastes: sweet, sour, salty, and bitter. Today, scientists add a fifth, called umami, which is the rich, savoury taste of meat, cheese, and mushrooms. Each taste has a purpose. Sweetness tells us that a food contains energy, saltiness helps us to find the minerals that the body needs, and bitterness warns us about poison, since many dangerous plants taste bitter.

However, the tongue can only recognise these five tastes. What we normally call flavour comes mostly from smell. When you chew, small amounts of the food's smell travel from the back of your mouth to your nose. This is why food seems to have no taste when you have a bad cold. Try holding your nose while you eat a piece of chocolate. You will notice only sweetness until you let go, and then the full flavour suddenly appears.

Our other senses matter, too. Experiments have shown that people judge the same food differently, depending on its colour and the plate it is served on. A strawberry dessert tastes sweeter on a white plate than on a black one. Even sound plays a part. Volunteers who heard a loud, crunchy noise while eating crisps believed that the crisps were fresher than those they ate in silence. Restaurants now use this knowledge to design the whole experience of a meal, not only the food.

Individual differences are also large. Some people are what scientists call supertasters. They have more taste buds than average, and they find strong bitter foods, such as coffee or green vegetables, very unpleasant. Others can hardly notice the same flavours. Part of this difference comes from our genes, but much of it comes from experience. Children learn what to like by watching their family, and they often need to try a new food ten times or more before they accept it.

As we grow older, our sense of taste becomes weaker, which is one reason why older people often add more salt or sugar to their food. This can be a problem for health, but it can be managed with herbs and spices.

Understanding taste is not only interesting but also useful. It helps doctors to treat patients who have lost their appetite, and it helps food companies to make healthier products that still taste good.
