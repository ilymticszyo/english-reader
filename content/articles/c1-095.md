When a person breaks a leg, friends send cards and colleagues offer to carry bags. When a person is struggling with severe depression, the response is often very different. Many people feel unable to speak about it, afraid of being judged as weak or unreliable, and some suffer in silence for years before seeking help. This reluctance has a name: stigma, a mark of shame attached to a condition or a group. Doctors and campaigners increasingly argue that stigma does as much harm as the illnesses themselves.
 
Mental illness is far more common than most people assume. According to international estimates, about one person in eight lives with a mental disorder, and in the course of a lifetime, a much larger share will experience a serious episode of anxiety or depression. These conditions are a leading cause of disability across the world, and they cost economies hundreds of billions each year in lost productivity. Despite this, mental health receives only a small proportion of health budgets in most countries, and in some of the poorest, almost none.
 
Stigma takes several forms. Public stigma consists of negative attitudes among the general population: the belief that people with mental illness are dangerous, unpredictable, or to blame for their condition. In fact, most people with such conditions are no more violent than anyone else, and are in greater danger of being harmed than of harming others. Self-stigma occurs when individuals absorb these attitudes and begin to feel ashamed of themselves. Structural stigma is built into policies and institutions, for example in discrimination in employment, or in the lower priority given to mental health services.
 
The effects are serious. People who fear judgment are less likely to seek treatment, and they tend to delay until their illness is severe. Those who do seek help may find it difficult to disclose their condition to employers or landlords. Relationships suffer, and isolation, which is itself a risk factor for illness, deepens. In the most tragic cases, the silence contributes to suicide.
 
How can attitudes be changed? Research suggests that contact is the most effective approach. People who have met someone with a mental illness, and heard their story as an equal, tend to hold more positive and less fearful views than those who have not. Campaigns in which well-known figures describe their own experiences have helped to open conversation, and several countries have run large programmes in schools and workplaces to improve understanding. Language matters as well. Casual expressions that treat illness as a joke, or describe individuals as "mad" or "crazy", reinforce negative attitudes.
 
Families also need support. Relatives often carry a great deal of the burden of care, and they too may feel ashamed or blamed. Information that explains the illness, and groups where they can share their experiences, can ease the sense of isolation. Recovery is possible for most people, and many return to full and productive lives, especially when help arrives early and without judgment.
 
Equally important are changes in services. Treatment must be affordable, available close to home, and respectful of the individual's wishes. Many experts support a shift from large institutions towards community-based care, in which people can remain among family and friends, and to early help in schools and primary care, before problems become entrenched.
 
The workplace is one setting in which change is both urgent and possible. Employers who offer flexible hours, confidential support, and training for managers in recognising the signs of distress often find that absences fall and that staff feel more loyal. A person who has recovered from an episode of illness is frequently as capable as any other employee, yet many fear that admitting past difficulties will damage their careers. Clear policies against discrimination, together with leaders who speak honestly about their own experiences, can help to make it safe to ask for support.
 
There have been real improvements. In many countries, people now speak far more openly about their mental health than a generation ago, and younger people in particular seem more willing to seek help. Yet there is much to do. A society that treats illness of the mind with the same seriousness and compassion as illness of the body would be healthier, more productive, and kinder.
