For many centuries, the usual way to learn a skilled trade was to become an apprentice. A young person would be placed with an experienced craftsman, often for several years, working alongside the master, gradually taking on more difficult tasks, and eventually earning the right to practise independently. The system was found across the world, from the guilds of medieval Europe to the workshops of Asia. In the modern age, it has often been regarded as old-fashioned, replaced by classrooms and examinations. Yet in recent years, many countries have rediscovered its merits.
 
The strongest argument in its favour is that it combines learning and earning. Apprentices are paid, usually at a modest rate, and they gain practical experience from the first day. By the end of their training, they have not only a qualification but also several years of real work behind them, and employers know exactly what they can do. In countries such as Germany and Switzerland, where apprenticeships are widespread, the transition from school to work is smoother, and youth unemployment is among the lowest in the world.
 
There is also a point about how people learn. Educational researchers have long observed that much knowledge is tacit, meaning that it cannot easily be written in a book or explained in a lecture. A carpenter knows by feel when a joint is right, and a cook knows by smell when a sauce is ready. Such knowledge is acquired by watching, trying, failing, and trying again, in the presence of someone more skilled. The educator Donald Schön described this as learning through reflection in action, and it is difficult to reproduce in a classroom.
 
Furthermore, apprenticeships can help to address the mismatch between education and the labour market. In many countries, employers complain that graduates lack the skills they need, while there are shortages in fields such as construction, engineering, and healthcare. Because apprentices are trained on actual equipment under the supervision of working professionals, their skills are likely to be up to date. And since firms invest in their apprentices, they have an interest in making sure the training is of good quality.
 
Yet the system has weaknesses. In some countries, the status of vocational education is low, and young people and their parents tend to regard university as the only respectable path. Apprenticeships may be seen as a second choice for those who did not do well at school. This perception is damaging, because it discourages talented people from entering valuable professions, and because it can create a divide between those who work with their heads and those who work with their hands.
 
There are also practical difficulties. Small companies may not have the time or the resources to train apprentices, and some employers may use them as cheap labour without teaching them anything. A narrow training in one firm's methods may leave workers poorly prepared when technology changes or the company closes. Good schemes therefore combine on-the-job practice with classroom teaching of general principles, and are supervised by independent bodies.
 
Cost is another consideration. A good apprenticeship is not cheap for an employer, since an experienced worker must spend time teaching instead of producing, and the apprentice makes errors. Governments therefore often offer financial support, or ask firms to share costs through a training levy. Where employers are confident that trained workers will stay, they are more willing to invest. Where workers move quickly between firms, companies fear that competitors will benefit from their investment, and they may prefer to hire people who are already trained. The design of the scheme, including the length of the contract and the rights of both sides, is therefore of great importance.
 
As technology changes the nature of work, the idea of learning on the job may become more important rather than less. Britain has recently expanded apprenticeships to include fields such as accounting, nursing, and software development, extending them beyond the traditional trades. If this approach succeeds, it may help to erase the old distinction between academic and practical education, and to restore the dignity of skilled work.
