Honey is a sweet food that people have enjoyed for thousands of years. But it is not made by people. It is made by bees, and the work is much harder than you might think.

It begins with flowers. A worker bee flies from flower to flower and drinks a sweet liquid called nectar. She stores it in a special part of her body. A bee may visit hundreds of flowers on one trip.

When she returns to the hive, she passes the nectar to another bee. That bee chews it for a while. This adds a substance from the bee's body, and it starts to change the nectar. Then the bee puts the liquid into a small wax room called a cell.

At this point, the nectar is still thin and watery. The bees need to remove most of the water. They stand near the cells and beat their wings very fast. The moving air dries the liquid. After a few days, it becomes thick and sticky.

When the honey is ready, the bees close each cell with a thin layer of wax. The honey can stay there for a long time. It is the bees' food for the winter, when there are no flowers.

A beekeeper takes some of the honey but leaves enough for the bees. A single bee makes only a small amount in her whole life, about one spoonful of honey for twelve bees' lifetimes.

The colour and taste of honey depend on the flowers. Honey from orange trees is light and mild. Honey from wild forests can be dark and strong. Next time you have honey on your toast, remember how many flowers it needed.
