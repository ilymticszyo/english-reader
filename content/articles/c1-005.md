For much of the twentieth century, many cities treated their rivers as little more than convenient drains. Factories discharged waste into them, engineers confined them in concrete channels, and entire stretches were covered over with roads or buried in underground pipes. The logic was straightforward: rivers flooded, smelled and obstructed development, so the sensible response seemed to be to control them as tightly as possible. In recent decades, however, a remarkable reversal has begun, as urban planners seek to bring these neglected waterways back to life.

The motivations are varied. In some places, the impetus was environmental. Polluted rivers had become dead zones where almost nothing could live, and tightening regulations on industrial discharge made recovery possible. In others, it was a matter of safety. Concrete channels carry rainwater away quickly, but they also concentrate it, which can result in catastrophic floods when storms exceed their capacity. Allowing a river to spread across a wider floodplain, with parks and wetlands absorbing the surplus, can be far more effective and considerably less expensive than building higher walls.

There is, in addition, a social argument. Residents who once turned their backs on the water increasingly want to walk, cycle or relax beside it, and a clean riverside can transform the character of an entire neighbourhood. Property values often rise, small businesses flourish, and communities that were previously separated by a barrier of concrete find themselves reconnected.

The technical challenges, however, should not be underestimated. Uncovering a river that has been buried for a century requires detailed knowledge of what lies underneath, from old foundations to contaminated soil. Restoring natural curves and banks demands space, and space in a dense city is rarely free. Moreover, a restored river needs to cope with modern demands: it must handle sudden surges of stormwater from roofs and roads, and it must do so without overwhelming the sewer system that shares its catchment area.

Perhaps the most delicate issue is fairness. When a neglected district is cleaned up and made attractive, housing costs frequently increase, and long-term residents may be forced out by the very improvements that were meant to benefit them. Planners have begun to acknowledge this risk, and some now combine restoration projects with policies to protect affordable housing. Whether such measures prove adequate remains to be seen.

Despite these complications, the trend is encouraging. Fish have returned to waters where they had been absent for generations, and birds nest in places that were once industrial wasteland. Equally important is the change in mentality. The idea that nature and the city are opposites, and that progress consists of overcoming the former for the sake of the latter, has gradually given way to a more nuanced view in which a healthy river is regarded as part of the urban infrastructure rather than an obstacle to it.

Finance is another obstacle that deserves mention. Restoration projects often require large upfront investments, while the benefits, such as reduced flood damage, improved health and increased tourism, accumulate slowly and are shared among many different people. Municipal budgets are rarely designed to capture such diffuse returns, and officials who must justify spending to sceptical voters may hesitate to commit to projects that will not be completed during their term in office. Some cities have addressed this problem by creating dedicated funds that combine public money with private contributions from developers who stand to benefit, while others have persuaded national governments to treat river restoration as part of climate adaptation. These arrangements are still experimental, but they suggest that the obstacles are institutional as much as technical.

The lesson, perhaps, is that cities flourish most when they work with the natural systems on which they were built, rather than against them.
