It is a normal day. You eat breakfast at eight o'clock, and at noon, your stomach starts to make strange noises. You feel hungry. But how does your body know that it needs food? The answer is more interesting than you might think.

Hunger is not only about the stomach. It starts in the brain. A small part of the brain controls our wish to eat. It receives messages from the rest of the body and decides whether we need more food.

One important message comes from the blood. After a meal, the sugar in your blood goes up. A few hours later, it goes down. When the level is low, the brain understands that the body needs more food, and it makes you feel hungry.

The stomach also sends messages. When it is empty, it sends a message to the brain that says, "Time to eat!" After you eat, the stomach fills up, and other chemicals tell the brain that you are full. This is why it can take about twenty minutes before you feel that you have had enough. If you eat too fast, you may eat more than you need.

Hunger is not always about the body. Our eyes and nose also play a part. The smell of fresh bread or the sight of a cake can make us feel hungry, even after a meal. Emotions are important, too. Some people eat when they are bored, sad, or nervous. This is not real hunger. It is a habit.

Sleep also changes hunger. When people do not sleep enough, they often feel hungrier, and they want sweet food. This is one reason why a good night's sleep is important for a healthy weight.

What can you do? Eat slowly, and listen to your body. Drink water, because thirst can feel like hunger. Choose food that makes you feel full for longer, such as vegetables, beans, and brown bread.

Hunger is a signal that our body is working well. If we understand it, we can make better choices.
