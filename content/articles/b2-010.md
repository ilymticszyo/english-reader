When I accepted a job in Germany three years ago, I imagined the move would be an adventure. I had studied the language for a year, I had read blogs about expat life, and I had a list of places I wanted to visit. What I had not imagined was how many small things would turn out to be difficult.

The first surprise was the paperwork. To rent a flat, I needed a bank account. To open a bank account, I needed an address. To register my address, I needed a rental contract. It felt like a puzzle with no starting point, and I spent my first two weeks sleeping in a friend's spare room while I tried to solve it. Eventually, a helpful clerk explained a way out, and I realised that the system was not impossible, only unfamiliar.

The second surprise was the language. My classroom German was fine when people spoke slowly and clearly. In real life, they spoke fast, used local words, and never waited for me to find the right grammar. In the supermarket, I could not understand the question at the checkout, and the whole queue watched me smile and nod like a fool. I felt much less intelligent than I was used to feeling.

Then there were the little cultural rules that no guidebook mentions. I learned that you should never cross the road on a red light, even when there are no cars. I discovered that shops close on Sundays, so you must plan your food in advance. I found out that my neighbours expected me to be quiet after ten o'clock and to separate my rubbish carefully. No one explained these things. I learned them by making mistakes, usually in front of other people.

Loneliness was the hardest part. At home, I had friends who understood my jokes and family who called me every week. Here, every conversation required effort. For the first six months, I sometimes sat in my flat on Saturday evenings feeling that I had made a terrible decision.

What helped was joining a local running club. The members were patient with my German, and they invited me to dinners and birthday parties. Slowly, the city turned from a place I lived in to a place I belonged to.

Looking back, I would not change my decision. The experience taught me patience, humility, and a new respect for anyone who lives in a language that is not their own. If you are thinking of moving abroad, my advice is simple: expect difficulties, ask for help early, and give yourself more time than you think you need.
