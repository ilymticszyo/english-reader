Most of us give little thought to insurance until we need it. We pay a regular sum for protection against a house fire, a car accident, or a serious illness, and we hope that we will never see a return on our money. Yet insurance is one of the most important inventions of modern economic life. It allows individuals and businesses to take risks that they could never bear alone, and it enables the vast investments on which prosperity depends. Behind this simple arrangement lies a surprisingly interesting history, and some difficult questions.
 
The basic principle is the pooling of risk. A single household cannot predict whether its home will burn down, and the loss would be ruinous. But among a hundred thousand households, the number of fires in a year can be forecast fairly accurately. If each household contributes a small premium, the fund is sufficient to compensate the unlucky few. The loss that would destroy one family is shared by many, and each is protected for a modest price.
 
The origins of the practice are ancient. Merchants in the Mediterranean and in China distributed their goods among several ships, so that the loss of one vessel would not ruin them. Later, the coffee houses of seventeenth-century London became meeting places for those willing to guarantee a ship's voyage in return for a fee, and one of them, run by Edward Lloyd, grew into the famous market that still bears his name. The Great Fire of London in 1666 gave a strong boost to fire insurance, and by the eighteenth century, companies were selling life insurance on the basis of the new science of probability.
 
Insurance, however, faces two problems that economists have studied closely. The first is moral hazard: people who are protected against a loss may take less care to avoid it. A driver with complete cover may be a little less cautious. Insurers respond with deductibles, requiring the customer to pay the first part of any claim, and with discounts for careful behaviour. The second is adverse selection: those who know that they are at high risk are more likely to buy cover than those at low risk. If the price is set according to the average risk, healthy people may decide that it is not worth buying, leaving a more expensive pool of customers and pushing the price higher. This is one reason why health insurance is so difficult to organise in a free market.
 
Technology is changing how insurers work. With data from vehicles, phones, and wearable devices, companies can assess an individual's behaviour with great precision, and offer lower premiums to those who drive safely or exercise regularly. Supporters say this is fairer, because the careful are not forced to subsidise the careless. Critics worry that, as insurers learn more about each person, the principle of sharing risk will dissolve: those who most need protection, because of illness or poverty or where they live, may find it unaffordable.
 
Climate change adds another strain. As storms, floods, and wildfires become more frequent and severe, insurers in some regions have raised their prices sharply or withdrawn altogether, leaving households exposed. Governments may have to step in, but that raises questions about who ought to bear the cost of living in dangerous places.
 
Public insurance deserves mention too. Many countries run national schemes for health, pensions, and unemployment, financed by compulsory contributions, which avoid the problem of adverse selection because everyone must take part. Supporters argue that some risks are so large, and so unevenly spread, that only the state can pool them fairly. Opponents reply that compulsory schemes reduce choice and can create heavy burdens on future taxpayers. The right balance between private and public provision is one of the oldest disputes in politics, and it is unlikely to be settled soon.
 
Insurance is, at heart, an expression of solidarity, and a way of turning uncertainty into something manageable. Its future will depend on whether we can keep that spirit alive in an age of ever more detailed information.
