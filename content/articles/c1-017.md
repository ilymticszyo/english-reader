Travel is often described as one of life's great pleasures, and for many communities, it is also a vital source of income. But when too many visitors converge on the same place, the qualities that attract them can be damaged. From the narrow streets of historic cities to remote beaches and mountain trails, a growing number of destinations are asking whether they have become victims of their own popularity.

Venice is the best-known example. With a permanent population of only a few tens of thousands, the city receives many millions of visitors a year, a large proportion of whom stay for only a few hours. Residents complain that housing has become unaffordable as apartments are converted into holiday rentals, that ordinary shops have been replaced by souvenir stalls, and that the crowded footpaths make daily life difficult. In 2024, the city began experimenting with a fee for day visitors during peak periods, a controversial step that raised questions about whether a living city should be treated like a museum with a ticket office.

The problem is not limited to cities. At Maya Bay in Thailand, made famous by a film, thousands of tourists a day arrived by boat, damaging the coral and driving away the sharks that once swam in the shallows. In 2018, the authorities closed the beach to give the ecosystem time to recover, and it did not reopen for several years. Machu Picchu in Peru, a fragile site of enormous cultural value, now operates a strict system of timed tickets and designated routes. Similar measures have been introduced on popular mountains, where litter and human waste had accumulated at high camps.

Defenders of tourism note that the industry supports hundreds of millions of jobs worldwide, and that for many developing regions, there are few alternatives. Revenue from visitors can pay for the protection of wildlife and the restoration of historic buildings. Where local people profit directly, they have a strong incentive to care for the environment. Banning visitors altogether, they argue, would penalise the poorest communities while doing little to solve the global problems of emissions and pollution.

There is a legitimate counterargument, though. Much of the income from mass tourism is not retained locally, since hotels, airlines, and tour operators are often owned by foreign companies. Meanwhile, the costs, such as pressure on water supplies, waste disposal, and rising prices, are borne by residents. Aviation, which carries most long-distance travellers, is also a growing source of greenhouse gases, and a single return flight can account for a significant share of one person's annual emissions.

What can responsible travellers do? Some choices are practical: travelling outside the peak season, staying longer in one place instead of rushing between many, choosing locally owned accommodation, and following the rules of protected areas. Others are matters of attitude. Visitors might ask whether they are seeking an experience or merely a photograph, and whether their presence adds anything to the community they enter. Destinations, for their part, can manage numbers through reservation systems, fees, and limits, while spreading visitors towards lesser-known areas.

Technology offers some assistance, but it also creates new pressures. Social media can make an obscure viewpoint or beach famous within weeks, sending visitors to places that have no facilities or experience in managing crowds. A single photograph posted by an influential account may increase traffic on a narrow path tenfold, and the people who live nearby have no way to prepare. Some local authorities have responded by asking visitors not to share exact locations, and a few have even removed signposts to discourage casual traffic. Such measures may appear unwelcoming, but they reflect a genuine desire to protect a fragile place from being loved to death.

Ultimately, the dilemma of tourism is a question of balance. The desire to see the world is natural and, at its best, promotes understanding between peoples. But places are not infinite resources, and treating them as such destroys the very thing that people travel to find. A more thoughtful kind of travel, slower and more respectful, may be the only way to ensure that these remarkable places are still there for those who come after us.
