In 1955, an American physician named Henry Beecher published a paper that would change the way medicine is tested. Drawing on a number of earlier studies, he claimed that about a third of patients felt better after receiving an inactive substance, such as a sugar pill, provided that they believed it to be a real treatment. The phenomenon, known as the placebo effect, had been noticed for centuries, but Beecher's paper made it impossible for researchers to ignore. Today, no serious drug can be approved without being compared against a placebo.

The reasoning behind this requirement is straightforward. If a patient improves after taking a new medicine, it is tempting to conclude that the medicine worked. But people recover for many reasons: the illness may have run its course, symptoms naturally rise and fall, and the attention of a caring doctor can itself be reassuring. Only by comparing a group that receives the real drug with one that receives a dummy can we separate the specific effect of the treatment from everything else. The best trials are also "blinded", meaning that neither the patients nor the doctors know who has received which, so that expectations cannot distort the results.

What is less obvious is that the placebo effect is not imaginary in the sense of being worthless. Brain scans have shown that when patients expect pain relief, the brain releases natural painkillers, and regions involved in the experience of pain become less active. Certain conditions respond particularly well, especially those in which the perception of symptoms plays a large role, such as pain, nausea, depression, and sleep problems. By contrast, there is little evidence that placebos can shrink a tumour or cure an infection.

The form of the treatment appears to matter, too. Studies suggest that large pills work better than small ones, that capsules appear to be more powerful than tablets, and that injections tend to produce a stronger effect than pills. Colour has an influence, and so does price: in one experiment, volunteers who were told that a painkiller was expensive reported more relief than those who were told that it was cheap, although both received an identical inactive substance. Perhaps most surprisingly, some researchers have found a benefit even when patients are told honestly that they are taking a placebo, as long as the treatment is presented in a warm and confident way.

These findings raise uncomfortable ethical questions. If a harmless pill can relieve suffering, should doctors prescribe it? In practice, many already do something similar, although not always openly; surveys have found that a considerable proportion of physicians have at some point given a treatment mainly for its psychological effect. Yet deception conflicts with the principle that patients are entitled to understand and consent to what is done to them. Moreover, it could encourage people to rely on remedies that have no real power, and delay the use of treatments that do.

There is also a commercial danger. The popularity of alternative therapies owes a great deal to the placebo effect, combined with the natural recovery that follows most minor illnesses. A person who tries a herbal remedy for a cold, and feels better a week later, will credit the remedy rather than time. Without careful trials, such mistaken beliefs spread easily, and unscrupulous sellers profit from them.

The most useful lesson, perhaps, is a humane one. The placebo effect suggests that the way a doctor listens, explains, and reassures is not merely a pleasant extra but an active part of healing. Modern medicine, with its pressure of time and its dependence on technology, risks losing sight of that fact.
