Every year, about a third of all the food produced in the world for human consumption is lost or thrown away. That is more than a billion tonnes of food, enough to feed hundreds of millions of hungry people, and it represents a staggering waste of the land, water, and energy used to produce it. If food waste were a country, it would rank among the largest sources of greenhouse gases, after only China and the United States. Yet the problem has attracted far less attention than its size deserves.

The causes differ markedly between rich and poor countries. In developing regions, most losses occur early in the chain, on the farm and in transport. Without refrigeration, good roads, or proper storage, fruit and vegetables spoil before they reach a market, and grain is eaten by pests or damaged by damp. In wealthy countries, the reverse is true. Most food is lost at the end of the chain, in shops, restaurants, and homes. A typical household in a rich country throws away a significant share of the food it buys, much of it still perfectly edible.

There are many reasons for this wasteful behaviour. Cheap food encourages carelessness: when a loaf of bread costs very little, it feels unimportant to throw away half of it. Shoppers are tempted by special offers to buy more than they need, and confusion about date labels leads many to discard food that is still safe. "Best before" dates, which refer to quality, are often mistaken for "use by" dates, which refer to safety. Supermarkets, for their part, reject fruit and vegetables that are the wrong shape or size, since they believe that customers will not buy them, and large portions in restaurants lead to leftover meals.

The environmental consequences are serious. When food rots in landfill, it produces methane, a powerful greenhouse gas. More importantly, all of the resources used to grow, process, transport, and cool the food are wasted too. Agriculture takes up about half of the world's habitable land, and a large part of this land is used to produce food that nobody eats. The waste also has a moral dimension, since close to a tenth of the world's population does not have enough to eat.

Solutions exist at every level. In poorer countries, simple improvements, such as hermetic storage bags, solar-powered cooling, and better roads, can greatly reduce losses on farms. In richer ones, retailers can sell imperfect produce at lower prices, and they can donate surplus food to charities rather than throwing it away. Several countries have passed laws on this. France, in 2016, was the first to prohibit supermarkets from destroying unsold food, and some others have followed. Clearer labelling can help consumers to understand dates, and restaurants can offer smaller portions or provide bags for leftovers.

Individuals can make a difference as well. Planning meals, writing a shopping list, storing food correctly, and using leftovers all help. Composting, which returns nutrients to the soil, is far better than sending waste to a landfill. Some households find that they save hundreds of pounds a year, simply by throwing away less.

Technology offers further possibilities. Apps connect restaurants and shops with customers who can buy surplus food cheaply at the end of the day, and sensors in packaging may soon indicate when a product has really spoiled, rather than relying on a printed date. Some companies make new products from material that would otherwise be thrown away, from beer made with stale bread to flour made from the pulp left after pressing juice.

The paradox of food waste is that it is both a global crisis and a matter of ordinary daily habit. It is also one of the few environmental problems whose solution does not require sacrifice. It asks only that we treat food as the valuable thing that it is.
