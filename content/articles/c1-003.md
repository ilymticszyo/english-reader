It is tempting to describe the invention of the movable-type printing press in fifteenth-century Europe as a sudden revolution, but historians increasingly emphasise how gradual and uneven its effects were. Printing did not instantly replace manuscripts, nor did it immediately make books affordable to ordinary people. What it did, over several generations, was to alter the way knowledge was produced, preserved and contested, and many of the habits we associate with modern intellectual life can be traced back to that transformation.

Before printing, every copy of a text had to be written by hand. This process was slow, costly and prone to error, since each scribe might introduce small mistakes that were then reproduced by later copyists. As a result, two manuscripts of the same work could differ significantly, and scholars had to travel considerable distances to compare versions. Print introduced a degree of standardisation that was previously unimaginable. When hundreds of identical copies circulated, readers in different cities could refer to the same page number, discuss the same wording and, crucially, notice the same errors.

This uniformity encouraged new forms of collaboration. Scientists could publish observations and invite others to verify or refute them, and the habit of citing earlier work became a basis for cumulative research. Mapmakers, anatomists and astronomers all benefited from the ability to reproduce accurate illustrations, which had always been difficult to copy by hand without distortion. It would be an exaggeration to claim that the scientific revolution depended entirely on print, but it is hard to imagine it developing as it did without it.

The press also changed the balance of power between institutions and individuals. Religious and political authorities had once been able to control the circulation of ideas by controlling the scribes. Printers, by contrast, were commercial entrepreneurs who responded to demand, and they could move their equipment across borders when a ruler became hostile. Pamphlets criticising established authority spread with remarkable speed, and the religious conflicts of the sixteenth century cannot be understood without reference to the cheap tracts that carried arguments to a wider public than ever before. Governments soon responded with licensing systems and censorship, which suggests that they understood the threat perfectly well.

Not every consequence was positive. The same technology that circulated careful scholarship also spread rumours, propaganda and fraudulent claims, and contemporaries complained of an overwhelming flood of books that nobody could possibly read. These anxieties will sound familiar to anyone who has worried about the quality of information online.

The economics of the trade deserve attention as well. Early printers were risky investors who had to buy expensive equipment and paper before knowing whether a book would sell. Many went bankrupt, and those who survived learned to favour texts with reliable demand, such as prayer books, grammars and almanacs. This commercial pressure shaped the contents of early libraries in ways that are easy to overlook: a work that could not attract enough buyers might survive only in a handful of manuscripts, or vanish altogether. In that sense, the market became a new kind of editor, deciding which voices would be heard widely and which would remain obscure, long before anyone had imagined the idea of an algorithm doing the same.

Perhaps that is the most instructive aspect of the story. Each major communication technology has provoked a similar cycle: enthusiasm about democratised knowledge, alarm about misinformation, and eventually the slow creation of new norms, institutions and skills for coping with both. The printing press did not make people wiser by itself; it made it possible for societies to argue about what wisdom means on an unprecedented scale. How well they have done so has always depended on the people involved rather than the machines.
