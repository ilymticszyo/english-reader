In the past, most people retired in their early sixties. Today, in many countries, they are working longer, whether by choice or by necessity. As a result, it is increasingly common for workplaces to include people from four different generations, from those in their early twenties to those approaching seventy. This brings opportunities, but also some tension.
 
Older workers have clear strengths. They have experience, and they often know how to handle difficult customers, solve problems, and avoid mistakes that younger colleagues have not yet met. They tend to be loyal, and studies suggest that they take fewer unplanned days off. Many are happy to teach others, and this passing on of knowledge can be extremely valuable to a company.
 
Younger workers, in turn, bring fresh ideas and comfort with new technology. They may question the way things have always been done, and they are often keen to learn quickly. Many of them also bring different expectations about work, such as more flexible hours and a greater emphasis on purpose, as well as pay.
 
Problems arise when each side relies on stereotypes. Older workers may be seen as slow, unwilling to change, or uncomfortable with technology. Younger ones may be described as impatient, or lacking commitment. Neither picture is fair. Research shows that differences within any age group are far greater than the differences between groups. A fifty-five-year-old may be a keen user of new software, and a twenty-five-year-old may prefer to work in traditional ways.
 
Nonetheless, misunderstandings can occur. Communication styles may differ, for example, with older colleagues preferring a phone call or a meeting while younger ones prefer a message. People may also disagree about how much feedback they expect, or about what a good work-life balance means. Without open discussion, these small differences can build into resentment.
 
Some organisations have found effective ways to help. Mixed teams, in which people of different ages work together on projects, can break down stereotypes. "Reverse mentoring" schemes pair young employees with senior ones, so that each teaches the other something, perhaps technology in one direction and judgment in the other. Managers can also avoid assuming anything about people because of their age, and ensure that training and promotion opportunities are open to everyone.
 
There is also a financial reason to get this right. With populations ageing and fewer young people entering the workforce in many countries, companies that cannot keep and use the talents of older employees will struggle. At the same time, unfair treatment because of age is against the law in many places.
 
In the end, a workplace that combines experience with energy is stronger than one that depends on a single group. The key is simple respect, and a willingness to listen to people whose life stories are different from our own.
