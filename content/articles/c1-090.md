The word "wilderness" carries a powerful appeal. It brings to mind vast forests, empty mountains, and open plains, places where nature is undisturbed and human beings are only visitors. In many countries, large areas have been set aside to protect such landscapes, and the idea of preserving untouched nature has inspired writers, artists, and conservationists for more than a century. Yet historians and ecologists have begun to question whether the wilderness we admire has ever existed in the form we imagine.
 
In the United States, the modern idea of wilderness emerged in the nineteenth century, when writers such as Henry David Thoreau and John Muir described wild places as sources of spiritual renewal, in contrast to the noise and corruption of the growing cities. Their influence led to the creation of the first national parks, and in 1964, Congress passed a law defining wilderness as an area "where the earth and its community of life are untrammelled by man, where man himself is a visitor who does not remain". The definition has since been widely imitated.
 
But a growing body of research suggests that many of these supposedly empty landscapes were in fact shaped by people. When Europeans arrived in North America, they encountered open woodlands and grasslands that appeared natural, yet which Native Americans had maintained for centuries through the deliberate use of fire, hunting, and planting. The same is true elsewhere. Large parts of the Amazon rainforest, once thought to be pristine, contain evidence of ancient settlements, soils enriched by human activity, and even orchards of useful trees. In Australia, Aboriginal burning practices over many thousands of years influenced the vegetation of an entire continent.
 
The implications are profound. The creation of national parks often involved the removal of the people who had lived there, on the assumption that their presence was incompatible with nature. In many cases, the result was injustice, as indigenous communities were evicted from their homelands, and sometimes ecological harm, since the removal of traditional management allowed forests to become overgrown and more vulnerable to catastrophic fires. Some scholars have therefore called the wilderness myth a cultural construction that conceals a history of displacement.
 
Does this mean that the idea of wilderness should be abandoned? Not necessarily. Even if no landscape is entirely free from human influence, there are clear differences in degree. Some places have been far less altered than others, and they shelter species and natural processes that are now rare. Scientists estimate that only a small fraction of the planet's land remains largely free of intensive human use, and that these remaining areas are critically important for biodiversity and for regulating the climate. To lose them would be a genuine loss, whatever we call them.
 
Even the language we use can be misleading. To call a landscape pristine suggests that it is at its best when people are absent, as if human beings were a kind of stain on nature. Many of the world's richest ecosystems, however, have long been home to people who depended on them and had every reason to look after them. Their knowledge of animals, plants, and seasons is a precious resource, and conservation that ignores it is likely to be poorer, as well as less just.
 
A more modest and perhaps more accurate view is that nature and people have always been entangled. Today, human influence reaches everywhere, through climate change, pollution carried by wind and water, and the spread of species around the world. Even the most remote places are affected. If wilderness is defined as a place beyond human influence, then none remains.
 
That recognition changes the task of conservation. Instead of trying to freeze landscapes as they supposedly were, many ecologists now argue for managing them wisely, in partnership with local and indigenous communities who know them best. The aim is not to keep people out, but to ensure that the relationship between people and the natural world is one of care rather than exploitation.
