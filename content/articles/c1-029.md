Older drivers often describe a change that they have noticed over the decades. In summer, a long journey used to leave the windscreen covered with the bodies of insects, so that stopping to clean it was a necessity. Today, the windscreen stays largely clear. This "windscreen phenomenon" is, of course, only an anecdote, and anecdotes can mislead. But in recent years, scientific studies have begun to suggest that the impression is not an illusion. In many parts of the world, insects are disappearing, and the consequences may be far more serious than most people realise.

The most influential evidence came from a German study published in 2017. Volunteers had trapped flying insects in nature reserves for nearly three decades, using the same methods in the same places. When researchers analysed the records, they found that the total weight of insects had fallen by more than seventy percent. Because the reserves were protected areas, the result was especially alarming. Subsequent reviews have been more cautious, noting that the data are patchy and that trends differ from place to place; some freshwater species, for example, have increased. Nevertheless, the general picture is of a significant decline in many regions, particularly among butterflies, bees, and beetles. Researchers also point out that most of the world's insect species have never been properly counted, which makes any firm conclusion difficult.

Several causes are likely to be at work, and they reinforce one another. The most important is probably the loss of habitat. Hedgerows, meadows, and wetlands have been replaced by large fields of a single crop, or by buildings and roads. Insects that need a particular plant to feed on, or a patch of undisturbed soil in which to lay eggs, find nothing suitable left. Intensive farming adds the burden of chemicals: modern insecticides are far more potent than their predecessors, and certain types have been shown to impair the navigation and reproduction of bees even in tiny doses. Artificial light at night confuses species that navigate by the moon, and a warming climate disrupts the timing of life cycles, so that insects may emerge before or after the flowers on which they depend.

Why should anyone other than entomologists care? The answer is that insects hold up a large part of the living world. About eighty percent of wild flowering plants rely on animals for pollination, and the majority of those animals are insects. So are many of the crops that people eat, including most fruits, nuts, and vegetables; some estimates value pollination at hundreds of billions of dollars a year. Insects also break down dead material and recycle nutrients, and they are the food on which countless birds, fish, bats, and amphibians depend. Populations of insect-eating birds have fallen sharply in Europe and North America, which is consistent with the loss of their prey. Some ecologists describe this as a slow-motion collapse of the food chain, in which each missing link makes the next one harder to sustain.

The difficulty, as with many environmental problems, is that the decline is gradual and largely invisible. No single catastrophe announces it, and because insects are small and often disliked, they rarely arouse sympathy. Yet the evidence suggests that the cost of ignoring them will be high.

Fortunately, solutions are not mysterious. Reducing the use of pesticides, preserving wild corners on farms, planting native flowers in cities, and turning off unnecessary lighting would all help. Some countries have begun to pay farmers to leave strips of land uncut, and a number of cities now allow their parks to grow a little wilder. These steps are modest, but they cost little. The greater task is a change in attitude: to recognise that a healthy world is not tidy, and that the creatures beneath our notice are quietly holding it together.
