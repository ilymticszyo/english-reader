Every morning, hundreds of millions of people drink coffee. For many of us it is a habit so ordinary that we never think about where it came from. Yet the history of this drink includes legends, religion, politics, and trade.

One popular story says that coffee was discovered in Ethiopia by a boy named Kaldi, who looked after goats. He noticed that his animals became unusually lively after eating the red fruit of a certain bush. He tried the fruit himself and felt the same energy. Historians doubt that the story is true, but it is certain that the coffee plant grew wild in the mountains of Ethiopia.

The first clear evidence of people drinking coffee comes from Yemen, around the year 1450. Religious men found that the drink helped them stay awake during long evenings of prayer. From there, it spread quickly to Mecca, Cairo, and Istanbul. In these cities, a new kind of public place appeared: the coffee house. People came to drink, play games, listen to music, and, above all, to talk. Some rulers disliked this and tried to close the coffee houses, fearing that people were discussing politics. They never succeeded for long.

Coffee reached Europe around the year 1600, carried by traders from Venice. At first, some people were suspicious of the dark drink, but it soon became fashionable. In London, coffee houses were nicknamed "penny universities", because for the price of a cup you could listen to educated men discuss science, business, and news. Several famous institutions, including an insurance market, are said to have begun in such places.

Meanwhile, the plant itself was moving around the world. The Dutch began growing it in their colonies in Asia, and the French planted it in the Caribbean. By the year 1700, much of the world's coffee came from large farms in the Americas, many of which depended on the forced labour of enslaved people. This dark side of the story is often forgotten when we enjoy our morning cup.

Today, coffee is one of the most traded products on Earth, and farmers in many countries depend on it for their living. Yet they often receive only a small part of the final price. In response, some companies now offer coffee labelled as fair trade, which promises a minimum payment to the grower.

From a boy's discovery to a global industry, coffee has changed how we work, meet, and think. The next time you hold a warm cup, remember that you are holding a piece of history.
