At almost any famous landmark today, a similar scene unfolds. Visitors arrive, turn their backs on the monument, extend their arms, and take a picture of themselves with it. A few moments later, they look at the screen, perhaps adjust the angle, and take another. Then they move on. Observers sometimes find this behaviour shallow, a sign that people care more about proving that they were there than about truly seeing the place. But the relationship between photography and memory is more complicated than such complaints suggest.

Psychologists have investigated what happens to our memories when we photograph an experience. In a much-discussed study at a university museum, participants were led around a series of artworks and asked either to photograph particular objects or simply to observe them. The next day, those who had taken photographs remembered fewer details about the objects and were less accurate at recalling what they looked like. The researchers called this the photo-taking impairment effect. A plausible explanation is that, once a camera has recorded the scene, we feel free to leave the task of remembering to the device. We "outsource" our memory, and our attention drifts.

However, the story has an interesting twist. When participants in a follow-up experiment were asked to zoom in on a specific part of an object, the impairment largely disappeared, and they remembered those details as well as, or even better than, the parts they had not photographed. This suggests that the problem is not the camera itself but the way we use it. If photographing makes us look more carefully, choosing a composition, noticing light and shape, it can actually deepen our engagement. If we simply point and press, we may hand the work to the machine.

Photographs also change what we remember in the long term. Looking at an old picture can trigger a flood of recollection, bringing back sounds, smells, and conversations that were not recorded. Yet it can also quietly replace the original memory. After seeing the same image dozens of times, we may find that what we recall is the photograph rather than the event. Details that were outside the frame, or that the photographer did not think to capture, gradually fade, while the posed smile, which may have been forced, becomes the permanent version of the day.

There is a further question about selection. We tend to photograph celebrations, holidays, and moments of success, and rarely the ordinary hours of boredom, worry, or disagreement. Social media encourages this tendency, since the pictures we share are chosen to be admired. Over time, the collection of images may present an overly cheerful account of our lives, and our memories, guided by these records, may become equally rosy. Earlier generations, who owned only a handful of expensive prints, were forced to be selective in a different way, and they often treasured the awkward, blurred pictures that survived.

None of this means that we should abandon our cameras. For many people, photographs are a precious link to relatives who have died, to places that have changed, or to periods of life that would otherwise be hard to reconstruct. A photograph can also be an act of attention and affection, as when a parent records a child's first steps. The challenge is to ensure that taking pictures supplements, rather than replaces, being present.

Cultural habits shape these questions as well. In some societies, family photographs are displayed on every wall, and elaborate albums are passed from generation to generation; in others, images are more often shared and quickly forgotten on screens. The shift from physical prints to digital files means that most of us now own thousands of pictures that we have never organised and rarely look at. A shoebox of old photographs, though messy, at least invited a rainy afternoon of browsing and conversation. A phone containing twenty thousand images may, paradoxically, offer fewer chances to remember, because nothing in the collection tells us which moments mattered most.

One practical approach is to take fewer pictures, and to choose them with intention. Another is to spend a minute simply looking before reaching for the phone, and to return to the photographs later, perhaps writing a few lines about what happened. Memory is not a storage device but an activity. If we want a picture to keep a moment alive, we have to keep returning to it with our own minds.
