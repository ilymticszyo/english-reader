In March 2018, researchers at a university in the United States published the results of a large study of the way news spreads on social media. They examined more than a hundred thousand stories that had been shared millions of times over a period of about ten years, and they found something disturbing. False stories travelled faster, further, and more widely than true ones. A false report was about seventy percent more likely to be shared than an accurate one, and it took true stories about six times as long to reach the same number of people.
 
The finding overturned a comfortable assumption, namely that the spread of misinformation is the work of automated accounts. The study found that machines spread true and false stories at about the same rate. It was human beings who made the difference. People, it seems, are drawn to stories that are new, surprising, and emotional, and false stories tend to have just those qualities. The truth is often less dramatic than an invention designed to attract attention.
 
Psychologists have identified several features of our minds that make us vulnerable. The first is confirmation bias, the tendency to accept information that supports what we already believe and to doubt what does not. A story that fits our view of the world feels true, and we rarely stop to check it. The second is the effect of repetition. The more often we encounter a claim, the more familiar it becomes, and familiarity is easily mistaken for truth. Even people who know that a statement is false are slightly more likely to believe it after seeing it several times.
 
Social factors are also important. We tend to trust friends and family more than strangers or institutions, so a story shared by someone we know carries extra weight. Sharing a dramatic claim can also be a way of signalling loyalty to a group or expressing anger at its enemies, regardless of whether the claim is accurate. In this sense, much of what we call misinformation is less an attempt to inform than an act of social identity.
 
The design of the platforms contributes. Algorithms that decide which content appears in our feeds are usually tuned to maximise engagement, meaning that posts which provoke strong reactions are shown to more people. Because outrage and fear generate clicks, the system tends to favour the sensational over the careful. The platforms have introduced measures such as fact-checking labels and the removal of the most harmful posts, but critics argue that these are too slow and too limited, while others warn that excessive control could threaten free expression.
 
What can be done? Research offers some encouraging findings. Simply prompting people to think about accuracy before they share a post has been shown to reduce the spread of false claims. So-called "pre-bunking", in which people are warned in advance about common manipulation techniques, such as emotional language or fake experts, appears to build resistance, much as a vaccine builds immunity. Teaching media literacy in schools, with practical exercises in checking sources and recognising bias, can also help, though its effects are modest on their own.
 
Individuals can adopt some simple habits. Pause before sharing something that provokes a strong emotional reaction. Check whether the story has been reported by reliable sources. Look at who is behind a claim and what they might gain. And be willing to say, "I was wrong," when a story turns out to be false.
 
No technique will eliminate falsehood, and it would be a mistake to imagine that earlier generations were free of rumour. But the speed and reach of today's networks make the problem more urgent, and a healthy public life depends on the ability to tell what is true. That ability, it seems, is not natural. It must be learned and practised.
