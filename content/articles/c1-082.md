In the decades after the Second World War, millions of families in Britain, the United States, and elsewhere moved to the suburbs. They traded cramped apartments for houses with gardens, and they exchanged the noise of the city for quiet streets and the convenience of the car. The suburb became the embodiment of a certain version of the good life: space for children to play, a place of one's own, and a sense of safety. Yet today, planners and critics increasingly question whether this dream can be sustained.
 
The attractions are real. Many people want a private garden, a garage, and a calm environment in which to raise children. Suburban homes are often more affordable per square metre than city apartments, and for much of the twentieth century, governments encouraged their construction through cheap loans, tax breaks, and the building of motorways. The result was an extraordinary expansion of urban areas, in which cities spread across the countryside at a rate far greater than that of their populations.
 
The costs, however, have become more evident with time. The first is dependence on the car. Because suburbs are built at low density, with houses far from shops, schools, and workplaces, residents often need to drive for almost every errand. Public transport is difficult to provide economically, since there are too few passengers within walking distance of any stop. The consequences include traffic congestion, high spending on fuel and vehicles, and a considerable share of carbon emissions. Children and the elderly, who cannot drive, may find themselves isolated.
 
The second cost is financial, though it is less visible. Roads, sewers, water pipes, and cables must be provided for every house, and spread-out neighbourhoods require far more of them per resident than compact ones. Some economists have calculated that many suburbs do not generate enough tax revenue to pay for the maintenance of their own infrastructure, so that they depend, in effect, on subsidy from denser areas. As the pipes and roads age, the bill falls due.
 
Third, there are environmental effects. Suburban development consumes farmland and wildlife habitat, and large areas covered with pavement increase flooding. Lawns and large houses use a great deal of water and energy. Yet it is worth noting that the picture is not simple. Some suburban residents are among the most committed environmental activists, and cities have their own pollution problems.
 
Some critics also describe a social cost. The architecture of the suburb, with its private yards and cars, can reduce casual contact between neighbours. The sociologist Robert Putnam linked longer commuting times with a decline in community involvement. But defenders reply that many suburbs have strong communities, with active schools, clubs, and churches, and that surveys often show high satisfaction among residents.
 
What, then, is the future of the suburb? Rather than abandoning them, many planners now propose to improve them. Ideas include allowing more varied housing, such as small apartments and houses built at the back of existing gardens; building shops and offices near railway stations; and adding safe routes for cycling and walking. Some older suburbs have already begun to change in this way, becoming more like small towns with their own centres.
 
Attitudes also differ from country to country. In parts of Europe and Asia, cities are denser, and the suburbs often come with good rail links, so that residents can reach the centre quickly without a car. In North America and Australia, by contrast, suburbs were designed around the car from the start, and a change of habit is much harder. The age of the residents matters too. Younger families are often willing to accept a longer journey in exchange for a bigger home, but older people who no longer drive may find the same house suddenly inconvenient, and many wish to remain in their neighbourhoods rather than move away.
 
The suburban dream is unlikely to disappear, because it answers to real desires. The task is to preserve what people value in it, space, quiet, and security, while reducing the burden it places on their budgets and on the planet.
