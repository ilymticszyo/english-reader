A garden can be a grand estate with fountains and long lawns, or it can be a window box with a few herbs. Either way, people who spend time growing things often say that it makes them feel better, and in recent years, researchers have begun to take such claims seriously. The idea that contact with plants and soil may protect our health is old, but the evidence for it has grown considerably. For a modern world in which most of us live in cities and spend our days in front of screens, the findings are of more than passing interest.
 
One of the best-known studies was published in 1984 by the environmental psychologist Roger Ulrich. He looked at patients who had recovered from gall-bladder surgery in a hospital in Pennsylvania. Some of the patients had rooms with a window looking out on a small group of trees, while others looked out on a brick wall. The patients with the view of the trees left the hospital sooner, asked for fewer strong painkillers, and received fewer negative comments in the nurses' notes. The study was small, but it has been cited ever since, and it helped to start a field now known as evidence-based design.
 
Later research has looked more closely at gardening itself, not just at views of nature. In a study from the Netherlands, published in 2011, volunteers were asked to carry out a stressful task and then either to garden for thirty minutes or to read indoors. Afterwards, the gardeners had lower levels of the stress hormone cortisol and reported a better mood, while the mood of the readers got worse. Large surveys have found that people who garden regularly tend to report greater satisfaction with life, and community gardens have been used with some success in the treatment of depression and loneliness.
 
Several explanations have been suggested. Gardening is a form of gentle physical exercise, which is known to improve mood and sleep. It takes place outdoors, which means exposure to daylight, and daylight helps to regulate our body clocks. It also demands a particular kind of attention, absorbing but not exhausting, which gives the mind a rest from worry. Some psychologists describe this as "soft fascination": the mind is held by the movement of leaves, the sound of water, and the slow change of the seasons, without the need for effort. Others point out that tending a plant offers a sense of purpose, and that watching something grow can be a quiet source of hope.
 
A more surprising idea concerns the soil itself. A study on mice, published in 2007, found that a common soil bacterium, Mycobacterium vaccae, increased the activity of a brain chemical linked to mood, and produced effects in the animals similar to those of an antidepressant. Some scientists suggest that this might partly explain why people feel good after working in the earth, although it is far from certain, and no one has shown the same effect in humans.
 
The benefits should not be exaggerated. A garden is not a replacement for medical treatment, and gardening can be difficult for those with limited mobility or no access to land. Even so, it is notable that interest in growing food has risen in many cities, and waiting lists for allotments, the small plots rented from local councils, are long. Schools, hospitals, and care homes are also creating gardens, and in some countries, doctors have begun to prescribe time in nature.
 
No special equipment is required. A pot of herbs on a kitchen shelf, a few seeds in a tray, or an hour a week on a shared plot is enough to begin. The reward may not be a prize-winning vegetable, but a calmer mind, and the quiet satisfaction of having helped something to grow.
