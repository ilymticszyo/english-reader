Online shopping has made life easier for millions of people. You choose a product, press a button, and a few days later, it arrives at your door. Many shops now offer free delivery, and customers have come to expect it. But nothing is truly free, and it is worth asking who pays for the service.

The first answer is the shop itself. A company that offers free delivery often raises the price of its goods slightly to cover the cost. Some also set a minimum spending amount, which encourages customers to buy more than they planned. A person who wants one small item may add a second one just to avoid paying for transport. In this way, the cost is hidden inside the final bill.

The second answer is the workers. Many parcels are carried by drivers who work for small contractors rather than for the large companies themselves. They are often paid for each parcel, not for each hour, so they must hurry to earn a reasonable income. Some drivers report that they work twelve hours a day, and that they have little time to eat or rest. When the system depends on speed and low prices, the pressure falls on those at the bottom.

The third answer is the environment. Delivery vans fill the streets of cities, add to traffic, and produce pollution. When people can return anything they do not like, the problem gets worse. A large share of returned clothes and shoes is never sold again, and some is simply thrown away. Every extra trip uses fuel and creates waste.

Customers pay as well, even when they do not notice. The habit of ordering everything online can harm small local shops, which cannot compete with the prices and convenience of big companies. When these shops close, streets become less lively, and neighbourhoods lose places where people meet.

Of course, there are benefits. Delivery helps people who cannot easily leave home, such as the elderly or those with a disability. It saves time for busy families. And some studies suggest that one van carrying many parcels can produce less pollution than many separate shopping trips by car.

What can be done? Some shops now offer slower delivery at no cost and charge for fast service, which reduces the number of rushed journeys. Others encourage customers to collect parcels from a nearby point, so that the driver can make fewer stops. Customers can also try to order several items at once and think carefully before sending something back.

In the end, the price on the screen is only part of the story. If we want fair wages, cleaner streets, and healthy local shops, we may have to accept that good service has a real cost. Being aware of that cost is the first step toward a fairer system.
