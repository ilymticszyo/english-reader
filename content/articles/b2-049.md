When the first satellite was launched in 1957, space seemed endless. Today, it is becoming crowded. Thousands of working satellites now circle the Earth, but they are outnumbered by objects that no longer serve any purpose: dead satellites, pieces of rockets, and fragments created by collisions. This "space junk" threatens the technology that modern life depends on.

How much of it is there? Agencies that monitor space can track about thirty thousand objects larger than a tennis ball. But there are probably hundreds of thousands of pieces about the size of a marble, and millions that are smaller than that. This may not sound dangerous, but speed changes everything. Objects in orbit travel at about twenty-eight thousand kilometres an hour, ten times faster than a bullet. At this speed, even a tiny piece of paint can damage a window, and a fragment the size of a coin can destroy a satellite.

The most serious danger is a chain reaction. When two objects collide, they break into hundreds of new pieces, which can hit other objects and create even more debris. Scientists call this idea the Kessler effect, after the researcher who described it in the 1970s. In 2009, two satellites, one active and one dead, collided over Siberia, and the crash produced thousands of pieces that remain in orbit. In 2007 and 2021, tests of weapons that destroy satellites made the problem worse.

Why does it matter? Satellites carry out many tasks that we take for granted. They support the navigation system in our phones, weather forecasts, television signals, and the global banking system. They also help to observe climate change and to guide rescue workers after disasters. If orbits near the Earth became too full of debris, we could lose some of these services, and launching new satellites might become too risky.

The International Space Station already has to move out of the way from time to time to avoid larger pieces, and astronauts have occasionally taken shelter in an escape capsule when a threat was discovered too late.

What can be done? The first step is to avoid creating more junk. New rules require companies to bring satellites back into the atmosphere, where they burn up, within a few years after the end of their mission. Better designs prevent rockets from exploding after use. Operators also share data to avoid collisions.

Removing the debris that is already there is harder. Engineers are testing many ideas, including robot arms to capture dead satellites, nets, and even lasers that could push small pieces out of orbit. A few companies have begun trials, but the technology is expensive, and it is not clear who should pay. There is also the problem of law, since a dead satellite still belongs to the country that launched it, and no other nation can simply take it away.

Space is a shared resource, and, as with the oceans and the air, it needs shared rules. If we act now, we can keep the sky open for the generations that follow.
