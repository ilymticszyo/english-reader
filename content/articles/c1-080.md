Human beings are not well equipped to think about long periods of time. Our own lives last a few decades, and our memories reach back little further than a few generations. Even the pyramids, which seem impossibly ancient, are only about four and a half thousand years old. Yet the history of the Earth stretches over four and a half billion years, a span so vast that the phrase "deep time", coined by the writer John McPhee, has become the standard way to describe it.
 
For most of history, people had no idea of such scales. In the seventeenth century, a respected archbishop calculated from the Bible that the world had been created in the year 4004 BC. The idea that the planet might be far older emerged only gradually, as naturalists began to look closely at rocks. In the late eighteenth century, the Scottish geologist James Hutton observed that layers of rock were slowly formed from the wear of mountains and the settling of sand, and that the same patient processes were still at work. "We find no vestige of a beginning," he wrote, "no prospect of an end."
 
This was a radical thought. It meant that the Earth was shaped not by sudden catastrophes but by small changes accumulating over immense periods. Charles Lyell developed the idea in the nineteenth century, and it provided the setting for Darwin's theory of evolution, which required enormous time for species to change. Today, scientists use the decay of radioactive atoms to date rocks with remarkable precision, and the figure of four and a half billion years is firmly established.
 
Attempts to grasp such numbers often rely on analogies. If the history of the Earth were compressed into a single year, the first simple living things would appear in spring, while the dinosaurs would arrive in late December and disappear a few days before the end of the month. Humans would arrive in the last minutes of the last day, and all of recorded history would take up the final seconds. Such images are helpful, though they can only give a hint of the true scale.
 
Deep time has practical as well as philosophical significance. Climate scientists study ancient ice and sediments to understand how the planet responded to past changes in carbon dioxide. The record shows that the climate has varied widely, but also that the present rate of change is unusually rapid. Geologists estimate how long it takes for oil to form, or for a mountain to rise, and these figures illuminate the finite nature of the resources we use. And those who plan the storage of nuclear waste must think about hazards that will last for many thousands of years, longer than any human institution has ever endured.
 
That challenge raises an unusual ethical question. How can we make decisions for people who will live ten thousand years from now? Some have proposed ways to warn future generations, using symbols and monuments that might remain understandable for millennia, although experts doubt whether any language or sign will survive so long.
 
The same patience is needed in understanding living things. Species that seem fixed to us are, over deep time, only passing forms. Whole groups of animals have risen and vanished, and the continents themselves have drifted, joined, and broken apart, carrying their plants and animals with them. A mountain range that now seems permanent was once a sea floor, and it will one day be worn flat. To geologists, nothing on the surface of the Earth is truly solid; everything is slowly in motion. This view does not make our own concerns trivial, but it does give them a context. The problems of one lifetime are real, yet they are small beside the processes that shaped the ground beneath our feet.
 
Thinking in deep time has a humbling effect. It places human history within an immense story and shows that our species is a recent arrival. Yet it can also be a source of wonder. We are the product of billions of years of change, and we are able, uniquely, to understand it.
