In 2018, a Chinese scientist announced that he had created the first genetically edited babies. Using a technique that allows researchers to cut and change DNA with remarkable precision, he had altered the genes of twin girls in an attempt to make them resistant to HIV. The response from the scientific community was swift and almost uniformly hostile. The experiment was condemned as reckless, medically unnecessary, and ethically indefensible, and the researcher was later sentenced to prison. Yet the episode did not end the debate. It merely made it more urgent, for the technology is real, and it is improving fast.

The technique, known as CRISPR, was developed in the early 2010s and quickly transformed biology. Before it, changing the genes of an organism was slow, expensive, and imprecise. Now it can be done in a few days, in an ordinary laboratory, by a student. Researchers have used it to create crops that resist disease, mosquitoes that cannot carry malaria, and animals that serve as models of human illness. In medicine, it has already produced treatments for certain blood disorders, in which the patient's own cells are edited and returned to the body.

Most people accept this kind of work, which is called somatic editing, because the changes affect only the treated person and are not passed on to children. The ethical debate becomes much sharper when the edit is made to an embryo, an egg, or a sperm cell. Such "germline" changes would be inherited by all future generations, and the person affected cannot give consent. Mistakes would not be limited to one patient; they could remain in the human gene pool.

Supporters of germline editing argue that it could end terrible suffering. Thousands of serious genetic diseases, such as cystic fibrosis, are caused by a single error in a gene, and editing could remove that error before a child is born. If we have the means to prevent suffering, they say, it would be wrong to refuse to use them. Many parents who carry such diseases already use screening of embryos to avoid passing them on, and editing could offer an option for couples for whom screening is not enough.

Critics respond with several concerns. The first is safety. Editing can cause unintended changes elsewhere in the genome, and their effects might not be apparent for years or even generations. The second is the difficulty of drawing a line. Once we edit to prevent disease, what is to stop parents from choosing height, intelligence, or appearance? The distinction between treatment and enhancement is not always clear, and a market for "designer babies" might be hard to resist. The third concern is justice. If such technology is expensive, only the wealthy will benefit, and it could deepen social inequality in a literal biological form. Disability rights groups add a further point: that the drive to eliminate certain conditions sends a message that the lives of people with them are less valuable.

International opinion is divided but cautious. Many countries ban the use of edited embryos to create pregnancies, and scientific bodies have called for a pause until the safety and the ethical questions are better understood. At the same time, some argue that a complete ban is unrealistic and that clear rules, together with open public discussion, would be wiser than prohibition that merely pushes the work underground.

The central lesson of this debate may be that scientists alone cannot decide. Technical skill does not give anyone special authority on the question of what kind of future we want. These are choices that concern everyone, and they will require careful, informed, and patient public debate while the technology is still young. Several countries have already set up national commissions, and international meetings have brought together scientists, ethicists, and patient groups to discuss shared standards.
