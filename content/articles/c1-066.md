We are used to thinking of memory as a kind of archive, a store in which the events of our lives are kept and from which they can be retrieved on demand. The metaphor is comforting, but it is largely wrong. Research over several decades has shown that remembering is not the playing back of a recording but an act of reconstruction. Each time we recall an event, the brain assembles it afresh from fragments, filling gaps with expectation and inference. As a result, memories are not only incomplete; they can be changed, sometimes substantially, by the very act of remembering.
 
The evidence is striking. In a famous series of experiments, psychologists showed volunteers films of traffic accidents and then asked them to estimate how fast the cars had been going. Those who were asked how fast the cars were going when they "smashed" into each other gave higher figures than those who were asked about cars that "hit". A week later, the first group was more likely to recall seeing broken glass, although there had been none. Other studies have persuaded people that they were once lost in a shopping centre as children, an event that never occurred, and many came to remember it in detail.
 
These findings have serious consequences for the law. Eyewitness testimony has long been treated as powerful evidence, yet analyses of cases in which innocent people were later cleared by new evidence show that mistaken identification played a role in the majority of them. Witnesses are usually sincere, and juries find sincerity persuasive. But sincerity is not accuracy, and confidence, as research shows, is a poor guide to whether a memory is right. Many legal systems have therefore reformed the ways in which police conduct identity parades and question witnesses.
 
If memories can be changed, can they also be deliberately removed? Recent experiments suggest that this is not pure fantasy. When a memory is recalled, it enters a temporary unstable state before being stored again, and researchers have found that certain drugs, given during this window, can weaken the emotional force of a frightening memory in animals, and in some limited human studies. The hope is to help those who suffer from post-traumatic stress, whose lives are dominated by memories that they cannot escape.
 
Yet the prospect raises ethical questions. Our memories, including painful ones, form part of our identity, and some philosophers argue that to erase them is to alter who we are. A soldier who has witnessed horror may carry a burden, but also knowledge that gives him understanding. Others point out that the memory of wrongdoing serves a moral purpose, as when societies preserve the memory of atrocities to prevent their repetition. Forgetting, in these cases, may be a form of injustice towards the victims. There are also practical concerns about misuse: a technology able to reduce unwelcome memories could, in the wrong hands, be used to manipulate witnesses or to silence protest.
 
Against these anxieties, it should be remembered that forgetting is a normal and useful function. A person who remembered everything, as in a famous case of a man unable to forget any detail, would be overwhelmed by trivia and unable to think in general terms. Selective forgetting allows us to focus on what matters, to recover from loss, and to forgive. Some researchers even argue that the main function of memory is not to preserve the past but to help us imagine and prepare for the future, which would explain why it is so flexible.
 
The practical lessons are modest but useful. We should hold our memories more humbly, accepting that vivid recollections can still be mistaken. In disputes, particularly those involving strong emotions, it is wise to look for written records. And we should be careful about asking leading questions, of children above all, since suggestion can easily become memory.
 
The study of memory ultimately teaches both caution and compassion. We are all, in part, the authors of our own pasts, and the stories we tell about ourselves are built as much by the present as by what happened.
