We usually think of the internet as something very modern, but its origins go back more than half a century. It was not created by one person or one company, and it was not designed to be the world of videos, shopping, and social media that we know today. It grew from a series of experiments and small decisions, many of which seemed minor at the time.

The story begins in the 1960s, during the Cold War. The American government wanted a communication system that could survive an attack. If a network depended on one central point, the destruction of that point would cut everything off. So researchers developed an idea called "packet switching", in which messages are broken into small pieces, sent separately by whatever route is available, and put together again at the other end. In 1969, a network funded by the American military connected four universities. This was the beginning of ARPANET, the ancestor of the internet.

During the 1970s, the network grew, and engineers faced a problem. Different networks used different rules, and computers could not easily talk to each other. Two researchers, Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn, designed a common set of rules, known as TCP/IP, which allowed separate networks to connect. On the first of January 1983, ARPANET adopted these rules, and many people regard this date as the birthday of the internet.

Meanwhile, ordinary people began to use the network for something unexpected. Email, invented in the early 1970s, quickly became its most popular use. The researchers had built the network to share computers and data, but they discovered that people mostly wanted to communicate with each other. This pattern would repeat many times: the real value of the internet has often come from uses that its creators did not predict.

For a long time, the internet remained a tool for scientists and technicians. It was difficult to use and not very attractive. The big change came in 1989, when a British scientist working in Switzerland, Tim Berners-Lee, proposed a system of linked documents that anyone could read using simple software. He called it the World Wide Web. In 1993, the organisation where he worked announced that the technology would be free for everyone to use, and this decision allowed it to spread very quickly. The first web browsers, which made it easy to move from page to page, followed soon after.

The rest is familiar. In the 1990s, millions of people connected from home, using telephone lines. Companies were created and sometimes disappeared, shopping and news moved online, and in the 2000s, fast connections and smartphones put the internet in every pocket. Today, more than five billion people use it.

The early designers believed that an open, shared network would bring people together. Many of those hopes have come true, but others have not. Questions about privacy, false information, and the power of large companies suggest that the story of the internet is far from over.
