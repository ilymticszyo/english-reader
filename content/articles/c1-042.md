In every period of crisis, a parallel explanation seems to emerge. During the great plague of the Middle Ages, some Europeans blamed the poisoning of wells; after the death of an American president in 1963, a huge number of alternative accounts appeared; and during the recent pandemic, a flood of claims suggested that the virus had been invented, or that vaccines contained hidden devices. Conspiracy theories, the belief that powerful groups are secretly plotting to harm the public, are hardly new. But the internet has given them a very wide reach, and researchers are trying to understand why they exert such a powerful appeal.
 
It would be a mistake, to begin with, to assume that believing in conspiracies is simply a sign of a lack of intelligence or of madness. Real conspiracies do exist: governments have lied about military actions, companies have hidden evidence of the harm that their products cause, and secret programmes have, at times, been revealed by brave insiders. A healthy doubt about powerful institutions is, in moderation, a useful trait. The difficulty arises when doubt hardens into a belief that is closed to evidence, and when absence of proof is interpreted as proof of a successful cover-up.
 
Psychologists have identified several needs that conspiracy theories seem to satisfy. The first is the desire to understand. Faced with a frightening and confusing event, people find it hard to accept that it might have been caused by chance, mistakes, or the accidental results of many decisions. A conspiracy offers a clear story with identifiable villains, and in doing so replaces uncertainty with a kind of order. The second is the need for control. Studies have found that people who are made to feel helpless in laboratory experiments become more willing to see patterns and plots in random events, as if the belief that someone is in charge, even an evil someone, were preferable to the sense that nobody is.
 
The third is a need to belong and to feel special. Believers often describe themselves as people who see what others are too naive to notice, and the communities that form around such beliefs can offer friendship, status, and purpose to those who feel excluded from the majority. This helps to explain why conspiracy thinking tends to be more common among those who feel ignored or treated unfairly by the political system, and why attempts to laugh at believers usually make them hold their views more firmly.
 
Social media amplifies these tendencies. Recommendation systems, designed to increase engagement, tend to promote emotionally charged content, and a user who watches one video about a hidden plot may soon be shown a series of increasingly extreme ones. Online groups create closed circles in which every member confirms the views of the others, and corrections from outside are dismissed as part of the plot. Moreover, since conspiracy theories are often compelling stories, they spread faster than the dull explanations that contradict them.
 
Can anything be done? Direct argument rarely works, because it threatens the identity of the believer. More promising approaches have been suggested by recent research. One is "prebunking": warning people in advance about the techniques used to manipulate them, such as the use of fake experts or emotional language, which seems to build a degree of resistance. Another is to engage with individuals respectfully, to ask them how they came to their view, and to acknowledge the legitimate concerns that often lie behind it. Studies of conversations with an artificial intelligence, which was patient and well informed, found that some believers modestly reduced their confidence, apparently because the exchange was free of judgment.
 
Ultimately, the best protection may be a healthy social environment. When people trust their institutions, because those institutions are open, honest, and responsible, they are less likely to look for hidden explanations. And when they feel that they have a voice in public life, they have less need to find meaning in a plot.
