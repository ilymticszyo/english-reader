Every time we use a phone, search the internet, or pay by card, we leave a trail of data. Where we go, what we buy, whom we contact, and what we read are recorded by companies whose names we may never learn. Most of us accept this arrangement without much thought. We tick boxes to accept terms we have not read, and in exchange, we receive free services, personalised recommendations, and the convenience of having our needs anticipated. But a growing number of people ask whether the price we are paying is too high.
 
The business model is simple. Many online services are free because the real product is the user's attention and information. By collecting data about behaviour, companies can build detailed profiles and sell advertisers the ability to reach very specific audiences. The more they know, the more precisely they can target their messages, and the more valuable their services become. Data is sometimes called the new oil, though a better comparison might be to a resource that is extracted from ordinary people, often without their full understanding.
 
Supporters of this system argue that the exchange is fair and benefits everyone. Users get excellent services at no cost, and personalised advertising is less irritating than random advertising. Data can also be used for good purposes: to improve traffic flow, to detect the spread of diseases, and to develop better medical treatments. Many people, when asked, say that they have nothing to hide and do not mind being tracked.
 
Critics offer several objections. The first is that consent is largely an illusion. Privacy policies are long, technical, and written in a way that discourages reading, and refusing them often means being excluded from services that are essential to modern life. Few people can realistically opt out of using a phone or a bank account. The second is that data can be misused. Information collected for one purpose may be sold, stolen in a security breach, or used to make decisions about people, such as the price of insurance or the offer of a loan, without their knowledge. Patterns discovered in data can reveal sensitive facts, such as health conditions, political views, or sexual orientation, that individuals never chose to disclose.
 
A third objection is about power. When companies and governments know a great deal about us, while we know little about them, the balance shifts. Personalised content can be used to influence opinions, as the controversy over political advertising has shown. And the knowledge that one is being watched can change behaviour, making people more cautious about what they search for or say. The argument that those with nothing to hide have nothing to fear misses the point: privacy is not about hiding wrongdoing, but about having a protected space in which to think and experiment freely.
 
Regulation has begun to respond. The European Union introduced a major law in 2018 that gives people the right to see, correct, and delete the data held about them, and requires companies to explain how they use it. Other jurisdictions have followed with similar measures. Technology can help, too: browsers and phones increasingly offer tools to limit tracking, and some services are designed to collect as little as possible.
 
There is, finally, the matter of what happens to data over time. Information that seems harmless when it is collected may acquire a different meaning in the future, when new tools can combine it with other sources, or when a new government or owner takes control of it. A company that promises to protect personal information may be bought, or may fail, and its records can be sold. Once data has been shared, it is almost impossible to recall.
 
Individuals can also take modest steps, by reviewing privacy settings, using strong passwords, and being selective about what they share. But these actions cannot substitute for the larger change that is needed: a shift in the default assumption from "collect everything" to "collect only what is necessary". The convenience of the digital world is real, but it should not come at the cost of our freedom to be left alone.
