In the winter of 1952, Harold Pryce was sixty-one years old, and he knew that this would be his last season at the lighthouse. The government had decided to replace the keepers with an automatic light. After thirty-four years of climbing the same stone steps, he would soon be a man with no work and no view of the sea.

The lighthouse stood on a rock a mile from the coast, and in bad weather, nobody could reach it. Harold lived there with two younger men, Tom and Elias, in turns of several weeks. Their routine rarely changed. They cleaned the great lamp, polished its glass until it shone, and wrote the weather in a thick book. When night came, they lit the light and watched the ships go by, each one a small moving star.

That winter, the storms were stronger than anyone could remember. In January, waves climbed the walls, and the whole building trembled like a ship. Harold sat by the stove, telling stories that the others had heard many times. He spoke of the winter when ice covered the windows, and of the night he saw a ship burning far away. Tom and Elias pretended to be bored, but they listened carefully, and Harold understood that these evenings would soon be gone.

One night in February, the radio brought a message. A fishing boat was in trouble, and nobody knew exactly where it was. Harold went to the top of the tower, and through the rain, he saw a very small light, flickering to the south. He told the others to turn the great lamp so that it shone in that direction, again and again, as strongly as possible. For three hours, they stood in the cold and sent the same signal across the water.

At dawn, a boat came slowly out of the mist. Five men were on board, tired and half frozen, but alive. They had lost their engine in the storm, and the light had shown them the way to the rocks, where they could wait for rescue. One of them, an old fisherman, climbed the steps and held Harold's hand for a long time without speaking.

In the spring, the men came to install the machine. It was efficient, quiet, and needed no one. Harold packed his bag, shook hands with his colleagues, and stood for a moment at the top of the tower. He thought of the boat, and of all the nights the light had shone without anyone knowing who was watching.

When the little ship took him back to land, he did not look behind him. He did not need to. He knew that the light would continue, and that for thirty-four years, he had been part of it.
