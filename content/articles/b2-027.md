Every few years, someone announces that libraries are dying. Books can be bought cheaply, and almost any piece of information is available on a phone. Some local governments, facing tight budgets, have closed libraries or reduced their opening hours. But in many cities, the opposite is happening: people are using libraries in greater numbers, and for more reasons than ever before.

The first reason is that a library is one of the few public places where you do not have to spend money. You do not need to buy a coffee to sit down, and nobody asks you to leave. For students who have no quiet space at home, a library may be the only place where they can do their homework. For people who are looking for work, it offers free computers, internet access, and help with writing a CV. For newcomers to a country, it often provides language classes and a safe place to meet others.

The second reason is that libraries have changed. Many now lend not only books but also tools, musical instruments, and even seeds for the garden. Others have rooms where people can use 3D printers or record music. Librarians, too, have a new role. Rather than only looking after books, they teach people how to search for reliable information and to recognise false news, which is a skill that the internet makes more necessary, not less.

Third, libraries bring people of different ages and backgrounds together. In an age when many of us spend our days looking at screens, a library is a rare place where a retired teacher, a teenager, and a young parent with a baby can sit in the same room. Researchers who study communities say that such shared spaces are important for trust between neighbours. When they disappear, the community often becomes more divided.

Critics argue that the money would be better spent elsewhere. This is a fair point, since budgets are limited and there are many urgent needs. But the cost of a library is small compared with the benefits it gives. One study in a large city found that, for every pound spent on its libraries, the community received several pounds in value, through better skills, more jobs, and improved health.

Of course, libraries will have to continue changing, and not every town needs a grand building. A small branch in a school or a shopping centre may be enough. The important thing is the idea behind it: that knowledge should be open to everyone, whatever their income.

A city without a library may save money in the short term. In the long term, it loses something that is much harder to replace.
