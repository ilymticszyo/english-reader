A century ago, most people owned few clothes and mended them until they wore out. Today, the average shopper in a wealthy country buys many more garments each year than their grandparents did, and keeps each of them for a much shorter time. A shirt that costs less than a sandwich can be worn a few times and then thrown away without a pang of guilt. This is the world of "fast fashion", in which designs move from the catwalk to the shop floor in a few weeks, and new collections arrive almost every week. It offers consumers variety and low prices, but there is growing awareness of the costs that do not appear on the label.
 
The environmental costs are considerable. Making clothes requires large quantities of water and energy. Cotton is a thirsty crop, and the dyeing and finishing of fabric pollutes rivers in many producing regions. Most modern clothing is made from synthetic fibres derived from oil, which release tiny plastic particles every time they are washed, and which do not rot when discarded. The industry is estimated to be responsible for a few percent of global carbon emissions, a figure that could rise if production continues to expand. Meanwhile, huge quantities of unwanted clothing end up in landfill or are burned, while others are shipped to poorer countries, where they overwhelm local markets and pile up on beaches and in dumps.
 
The human costs are equally troubling. Much of the world's clothing is made in countries where wages are low and regulation is weak. In 2013, the collapse of a factory building in Bangladesh killed more than a thousand workers, most of them young women, and drew attention to the conditions under which clothes for Western shops were being made. Since then, some improvements in safety have been achieved, but many workers remain poorly paid, work long hours, and have little power to complain. Companies argue that their orders provide jobs and help countries to develop, which is true, but critics reply that the benefits are shared very unequally.
 
Why, then, does the system persist? One reason is that it is skilfully designed to encourage consumption. Constant novelty makes last month's purchases seem old, and social media, with its stream of images, increases the pressure to wear something new for every occasion. Low prices lower the barrier to impulse buying, and clothing has come to be seen as disposable. At the same time, consumers face real difficulties if they want to choose differently. Labels reveal little about how and where clothes were made, and higher prices do not always guarantee better conditions or quality.
 
Efforts at reform come from several directions. Some governments are considering laws that would require companies to monitor their supply chains, to report their environmental impact, or to pay for the collection and recycling of used clothing. Technology may help, since researchers are developing methods to recycle fibres from old garments into new ones, although at present only a tiny share of clothing is recycled in this way. Some companies have introduced collections made from recycled materials, repair services, or schemes to take back used items, but critics say that such initiatives are modest in comparison with the volume of new clothes that these same companies sell.
 
Consumers have a part to play, though it would be unfair to place the whole burden on them. Buying fewer items of better quality, repairing what we own, borrowing or renting for special occasions, and buying second-hand are all simple ways to reduce harm. The market for used clothing has grown rapidly, helped by online platforms, and it shows that a change in attitude is possible. For many younger people, wearing second-hand clothes has lost its stigma and is now a matter of pride.
 
The deeper issue is a question of values. If clothing is cheap because its true costs are borne by workers and the environment, then low prices are an illusion. A more sustainable industry would probably make fewer clothes, charge more for them, and expect people to keep them longer. Many shoppers might find that they enjoy their wardrobes more as a result.
 
There is, in other words, a trade-off between abundance and responsibility. Whether societies are prepared to accept it will be one of the tests of our willingness to count the full cost of what we consume.
