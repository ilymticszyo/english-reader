For five hundred years, the cold waters off the coast of Newfoundland were among the richest fishing grounds in the world. When the explorer John Cabot arrived in 1497, he reported that the sea was so thick with cod that fish could be caught by lowering baskets into the water. European fishermen soon followed in large numbers, and for centuries, the cod sustained communities on both sides of the Atlantic. In the early 1990s, however, the fish suddenly vanished, and the tragedy that followed has become one of the best-known warnings about the dangers of overfishing.
 
The decline did not happen overnight. In the middle of the twentieth century, the industry was transformed by technology. Large factory ships, equipped with sonar to locate shoals and huge nets to scoop them up, began to arrive from Europe and the Soviet Union. These vessels could catch and process fish around the clock, and they took far more than traditional boats. Catches rose to record levels in the 1960s, and then began to fall. Local fishermen noticed that the fish were becoming smaller and harder to find, but scientists and officials, under pressure from an industry that provided thousands of jobs, were reluctant to reduce quotas.
 
The government's own estimates turned out to be too optimistic. Surveys of the fish population contained errors, and the assumption that the stock was recovering was mistaken. In 1992, the Canadian government finally declared a moratorium, a complete ban on cod fishing, in the hope that the stock would recover in a few years. Some thirty thousand people lost their jobs in a single day, in what has been described as the largest industrial layoff in Canadian history. Whole villages were left without a source of income, and many young people moved away.
 
What is striking is that the cod have still not fully recovered. More than thirty years later, stocks in many areas remain a fraction of their former size, although there are signs of improvement in some regions. Scientists have suggested several reasons. Fishing removed the largest and oldest females, which produce the most eggs, and the remaining fish matured at a younger age and smaller size. The ecosystem itself may also have changed. With fewer cod to eat them, populations of smaller fish and shellfish, such as shrimp and crab, have grown, and these may now compete with young cod for food or even eat their eggs. Changes in water temperature may also play a part.
 
The story is not unique. Around the world, a large proportion of fish stocks are fished at or beyond their sustainable limits, and the collapse of cod is only one example. It reflects a problem that economists call the "tragedy of the commons": when a resource belongs to no one in particular, each user has an incentive to take as much as possible before others do, and the result is exhaustion for all.
 
There are, however, success stories. Where governments have set scientifically based quotas, enforced them, and protected breeding areas, some stocks have rebounded. Marine reserves, where fishing is banned, often allow fish to grow larger and to spill over into neighbouring waters. Certification schemes help consumers to choose seafood from well-managed sources. In the North Sea, careful management has allowed several fish stocks to recover.
 
Local knowledge was another casualty. Inshore fishermen had warned for years that the fish were disappearing from their traditional grounds, but their observations were dismissed as anecdotes, while the official figures, which looked more scientific, told a more comfortable story. Researchers now argue that the voices of those who work on the water should be taken more seriously, and that management should combine scientific surveys with the experience of fishing communities. Humility about what we do not know is as important as the quotas themselves.
 
The lesson of the cod is that nature does not always give warning before it fails, and that the cost of caution is almost always smaller than the cost of collapse. Fishing communities are now among the strongest supporters of sustainable management, because they have seen what happens without it.
