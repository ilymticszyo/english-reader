There is an old Italian saying that translators are traitors. The two words, traduttore and traditore, differ by only a few letters, and the joke contains a real problem: no matter how careful the translator is, something is always lost when a text moves from one language to another. Far from being a mechanical exchange of equivalent words, translation is a series of difficult decisions, and each decision reflects the judgement, culture, and sometimes the politics of the person who makes it.

Consider the simplest case, the individual word. Many languages divide the world in different ways. English has a single word for "blue", whereas Russian distinguishes between a light blue and a dark blue as two separate colours, and speakers appear to notice the difference more quickly. Some terms have no direct equivalent at all. The Portuguese word saudade describes a deep, gentle longing for something absent, and translators typically need a whole phrase to approximate it. Even apparently ordinary words carry associations that vary from one culture to another. A dog may be a loyal companion in one tradition and an unclean animal in another, and the translator must decide whether to preserve the original image or to adapt it to the reader.

Grammar creates further difficulties. Languages differ in whether they require speakers to indicate the gender of a noun, the formality of a relationship, or the source of their knowledge. Translating from Turkish into English, for example, a translator must often decide whether a pronoun refers to a man or a woman, even though the original does not specify. Translating in the opposite direction, the subtlety of English politeness may disappear in a language with rigid levels of respect. These choices may seem small, but they can alter the meaning of a sentence and the impression a character gives.

History offers striking examples of how much depends on such decisions. Religious texts have been translated again and again, and each version has reflected the beliefs of its time. Scholars have sometimes been punished for translating sacred writings into the language of ordinary people, because the new version threatened the authority of those who controlled interpretation. In the twentieth century, errors in diplomatic translation have occasionally been blamed for deepening a political crisis, though historians often argue about whether the mistake was truly decisive.

Literary translators face a particular dilemma. Should they remain close to the original, even if the result sounds strange, or should they produce a smooth, natural text that reads as though it had been written in the new language? The first approach respects the author's style but may feel awkward; the second is easier to enjoy but risks hiding the foreignness that made the work distinctive. Many translators describe their craft as a continual compromise between faithfulness and readability.

Teachers of foreign languages have long understood this problem from the other direction. A student who relies on a dictionary soon discovers that the first meaning listed is rarely the right one, and that idioms cannot be decoded word by word. The English phrase "it is raining cats and dogs" makes little sense when translated literally, and the learner must grasp that the meaning lives in the whole expression. Good translators therefore need more than vocabulary. They need a deep familiarity with both cultures, an ear for tone, and the humility to admit that some jokes, rhymes, and puns simply cannot cross the border intact. Often the best solution is not a perfect equivalent but a creative substitute that produces a similar effect on the new audience.

Machine translation has improved dramatically, and for everyday purposes, such as understanding a menu or an email, it is now remarkably useful. Yet it too makes choices, usually based on patterns in the vast quantities of text on which it was trained. When those patterns contain stereotypes, the translation can repeat them, quietly assigning professions to genders, for instance. Understanding that translation is an act of interpretation, rather than a transparent window, helps us to read translated works with more humility and curiosity. Behind every sentence we read in a foreign book, there is a person who made a decision on our behalf.
