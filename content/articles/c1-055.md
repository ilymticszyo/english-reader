Look at a map of Africa, and you will notice something unusual. Many of the borders are astonishingly straight, running for hundreds of kilometres along lines of latitude and longitude, or in perfect angles that no river, mountain, or human settlement would produce. They look drawn by a ruler, and, in a sense, they were. In 1884 and 1885, representatives of European powers met in Berlin to agree on the rules for dividing the continent among themselves. No African ruler was invited to attend. The lines they drew would shape the political life of hundreds of millions of people for more than a century.

The motives behind the division were practical and self-interested. European states competed for raw materials, markets, and prestige, and they wished to avoid fighting each other over territory. Their solution was to draw boundaries on paper, often with little knowledge of the geography or the people who lived there. As a result, ethnic groups were split between two or more colonies, while others, who had little in common or were long-standing rivals, were placed within the same borders. The Somali people, for example, found themselves divided between five different territories.

When the colonies became independent, mostly in the twenty years after 1950, African leaders faced a dilemma. They could have chosen to redraw the borders to reflect ethnic or historical lines, but they feared that this would lead to endless disputes and violence. In 1964, the newly formed Organisation of African Unity decided that existing borders should be respected. This principle prevented many wars, and compared with other regions, the number of conflicts between African states has been relatively small. But it also meant that the artificial divisions were inherited by the new nations.

The consequences are still debated. Some scholars argue that ethnically mixed states have experienced more internal conflict, as groups compete for control of the state, while others claim that the problem is not diversity itself but the way in which colonial rulers deliberately favoured some groups over others. In several territories, administrators gave positions of power to a minority and encouraged divisions between communities, a strategy of "divide and rule" that left lasting bitterness after independence. The terrible violence in Rwanda in 1994 is often connected with such a history, though its causes were complex.

Africa is far from the only region where colonial borders still matter. In the Middle East, boundaries drawn by Britain and France after the First World War created states that combined peoples with different religions and loyalties and left others, such as the Kurds, without a country of their own. In South Asia, the partition of British India in 1947 produced two states, and the hasty drawing of the line led to mass migration and violence in which hundreds of thousands, and perhaps more, died. Many disputes in these regions can be traced back to decisions made by outsiders, often in a few weeks.

It would be an exaggeration, however, to blame all present troubles on the past. Many borders in Europe, too, were drawn by war and agreement, and few are "natural". Political choices made since independence, including corruption, weak institutions, and outside interference during the Cold War, have also played a part. Historians caution against simple explanations, and note that people in the former colonies are not merely the victims of their history.

Nevertheless, the story of the lines on the map reminds us that political arrangements, once made, are difficult to undo. Borders appear permanent and natural, but they are human inventions, and many have lasted far longer than those who created them could have imagined. Even the meaning of a border has changed: for many people who live near one, it is less a line on the ground than a daily experience of checks, markets, and family ties that cross it.
