In 1969, a man walked on the Moon, and the whole world watched. Half a century later, the sight of rockets climbing into the sky still moves people, and space has acquired new excitement: private companies are launching their own rockets, several countries plan to return to the Moon, and ambitious plans exist to send humans to Mars. At the same time, many people ask a pointed question. In a world with so many problems on the ground, from poverty to climate change, is it right to spend huge sums on exploring space?
 
Supporters of space programmes begin by pointing to their practical benefits. Satellites are now central to modern life. They provide weather forecasts that save lives, enable navigation systems on which transport depends, support global communications, and monitor the climate, forests, and oceans with a precision that would be impossible from the ground. Much of the technology developed for space has found uses elsewhere, from medical imaging to water purification, and the demands of spaceflight have driven advances in materials, computing, and electronics. Defenders also note that, compared with other forms of public spending, the cost is modest: the budget of the American space agency is a small fraction of that of the military.
 
A second argument concerns knowledge. Space missions have transformed our understanding of the universe, from the discovery of planets orbiting other stars to the detailed study of the origins of the solar system. Telescopes in orbit have revealed galaxies billions of years old, and probes sent to distant planets have sent back images that have shaped how we see our place in the cosmos. Knowledge of this kind, supporters say, is valuable in itself, in the same way as art or music, and a society that stops asking such questions is poorer for it. There is also an inspirational effect, as young people who are excited by space often go on to study science and engineering.
 
A third argument is about the long-term survival of humanity. Some scientists and entrepreneurs argue that our species should not depend on a single planet, since a disaster such as an asteroid impact could end human civilisation. Settling other worlds would provide a form of insurance. Critics find this unconvincing, pointing out that Mars is cold, airless, and drenched in radiation, and that it will remain far less hospitable than the worst place on Earth for a very long time. They argue that the same resources would be better spent on protecting the planet we have.
 
Critics also raise the question of priorities. Even if the benefits are real, they say, the same funds could save more lives and reduce more suffering if applied to health, education, or climate adaptation. Human spaceflight is especially costly and risky, and many scientists argue that robots can achieve most scientific goals at a fraction of the price, without endangering astronauts. Furthermore, the growing commercialisation of space raises new concerns, including the accumulation of debris in orbit, which threatens satellites, and the question of who has the right to claim resources on other worlds.
 
The choice is often presented as an opposition between space and Earth, but this may be a false one. Budgets are not fixed in the way the argument assumes, and spending on space often supports spending on Earth, since many of the satellites that study climate change are launched by space agencies. In practice, societies spend money on a great many things that are not strictly essential, and the real question is whether exploration is a worthwhile part of that mixture.
 
There is no scientific formula for answering this. It depends on what we value: security and welfare today, or curiosity and ambition for the future. But a civilisation that can afford to do both, and chooses to do neither, may be missing something important about what it means to be human.
