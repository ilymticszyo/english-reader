It is easy to forget how strange money is. A banknote is a piece of coloured paper, and the number in a bank account is merely a record on a computer, yet people will work for years to obtain them and will give up goods and services in return. Money works because everyone believes that others will accept it, and this shared belief is among the most remarkable inventions in human history. How did it come about, and what does its history tell us about the nature of trust?
 
A popular story, repeated in many textbooks, says that money began as a solution to the problems of barter. If a farmer who had grain wanted shoes, but the shoemaker wanted eggs, trade was difficult; so people began to use a common commodity that everyone would accept, such as cattle, salt, or shells. Anthropologists, however, have found little evidence for a world of pure barter. Early societies seem to have relied more on credit and obligation, in which people kept informal accounts of what they owed to each other. Money may have developed less from the exchange of goods than from the need to measure debts, including payments to rulers and temples.
 
Metal coins appeared in the kingdom of Lydia, in what is now Turkey, around the seventh century BC, and spread rapidly through the Greek world. Their value was guaranteed by the stamp of the ruler, and their metal was valuable in itself. This made them convenient but also vulnerable: rulers who needed to pay for wars discovered that they could reduce the amount of silver in the coins while keeping the face value, a practice known as debasement. The Roman Empire used it so often that its coins eventually contained little silver, and the resulting rise in prices is thought to have contributed to its troubles.
 
In China, the first paper money appeared about a thousand years ago, when merchants who were tired of carrying heavy strings of copper coins began to leave them with deposit shops in exchange for paper receipts. The government soon took over the system. When Marco Polo described it to Europeans, they found the idea hard to believe. In Europe, paper money developed more slowly, through the notes issued by goldsmiths and banks, which promised to pay the holder in gold on demand. For centuries, paper money was backed by a precious metal, and the supply was supposedly limited by the amount of gold in the vaults.
 
This link was gradually broken. Governments found that the gold standard restricted their ability to respond to crises, and in the twentieth century, they abandoned it one after another. Today's currencies are "fiat" money, meaning that they have value only because the government declares them to be legal tender and because people trust that they will keep their worth. The system has delivered flexibility, but it also means that its stability depends on the discipline of central banks. History offers many examples of what happens when this discipline fails: in Germany in the 1920s and Zimbabwe in the 2000s, prices rose so quickly that wages had to be paid daily and savings became worthless.
 
The latest chapter concerns digital money. Most money today exists only as electronic entries, and cash is used less and less in some countries. Cryptocurrencies, which appeared in the last two decades, propose to replace trust in institutions with trust in mathematics, but their prices have been so unstable that they work poorly as a means of payment, and they have mostly been used for speculation. Central banks, meanwhile, are examining digital versions of national currencies.
 
Across all these forms, one thing remains constant. Money is a social agreement, and it works only as long as people believe in it. Coins, notes, and numbers on screens are simply different technologies for sustaining that belief.
 
Seen in this light, the history of money is also a history of trust, in rulers, in banks, in governments, and in each other. When trust is strong, money allows strangers to cooperate on a vast scale; when it breaks down, the consequences can be disastrous.
