Human beings have always dreamed of defeating old age. Ancient myths describe fountains of youth and potions of immortality, and every generation has produced sellers of remedies that promise to restore vigour. For centuries, such hopes belonged to fantasy. Today, however, the study of ageing has become a serious scientific field, and some researchers believe that the process may be slowed, or even partly reversed, within the lifetimes of people who are alive now.
 
First, it is worth asking why we age at all. The question is less obvious than it appears. Natural selection favours traits that help organisms to survive and reproduce, and it has little power to remove problems that appear only after reproduction is complete. One influential theory suggests that ageing is the accumulation of such late-acting flaws: genes that are beneficial in youth but harmful in old age are passed on because their harmful effects arrive too late to be selected against. Another proposes that bodies simply wear out, as damage to cells builds up faster than it can be repaired.
 
Modern research has identified a number of biological changes that accompany ageing. Cells accumulate damage to their genetic material. The protective caps at the ends of chromosomes, called telomeres, become shorter each time a cell divides, until the cell can no longer divide. Some cells, instead of dying, enter a state called senescence, in which they stop dividing but remain alive and release substances that inflame surrounding tissue. Proteins become damaged, and the tiny structures that supply cells with energy become less efficient. Each of these changes has been proposed as a target for treatment.
 
Experiments with animals have given reason for hope. Restricting calories without causing malnutrition extends the lives of worms, flies, mice, and some monkeys, and researchers have begun to identify the chemical pathways involved. Certain drugs, including one first discovered in soil from Easter Island, also lengthen the lives of mice. Other studies have shown that removing senescent cells makes older mice healthier and more active. Several companies, funded by wealthy investors, are now testing similar approaches in people, although the results so far are modest.
 
Yet there are reasons for caution. Humans are not mice, and effects seen in the laboratory often fail to appear in people. Ageing is not a single disease but a vast web of interacting processes, and interfering with one may have unpredictable consequences elsewhere. Moreover, many of the largest improvements in life expectancy have come not from medical miracles but from better sanitation, nutrition, and the prevention of infectious disease. Average life expectancy in wealthy countries has risen by about two or three years each decade over the past century, though the rise has slowed in recent years.
 
A different view holds that the goal should not be longer life but longer health. Many people spend their final years in poor health, suffering from conditions such as heart disease, arthritis, and dementia. If treatments could delay the onset of these diseases, even without extending the maximum lifespan, the benefit would be immense. Researchers speak of extending the "healthspan", the period during which people remain well and independent.
 
Such advances would raise difficult social questions. If effective treatments were expensive, would they be available only to the rich, creating a gap in life expectancy wider than any that exists today? How would societies manage a population in which large numbers of people lived and worked into their nineties? What would happen to pensions, to careers, and to the balance between generations? These questions are no longer purely theoretical.
 
Meanwhile, the known ways of ageing well remain surprisingly ordinary: regular exercise, a healthy diet, good sleep, not smoking, and strong social relationships. They are not glamorous, but they are the best evidence-based advice we have.
