For forty years, Margaret Hill had opened her bookshop at nine o'clock sharp. The shop stood at the end of Harbour Road, where the street turned towards the sea, and it was the last independent business in a row of buildings that had once held a baker, a tailor, and a small hardware store. One by one, they had closed. Now the other windows were covered with paper and the only sound came from the gulls.

On a grey Tuesday in March, a young man came in and asked whether she had any books about boats. Margaret pointed to a shelf at the back, and he spent almost an hour reading. When he left, he bought nothing. She was not surprised, because it had happened many times before. Most visitors these days came to look, to take photographs, or to ask for directions to the ferry.

That evening, she sat at the counter and added up the month's sales. The total was lower than the rent. She had known this for a long time, but seeing the numbers on paper made it harder to ignore. Her daughter, who lived in the city, kept telling her to sell the building to a developer who wanted to turn the row into flats. "You have worked enough," she said on the phone. "Nobody would blame you."

The next morning, the young man returned. His name was Omar, and he explained that he was a designer who had recently moved into a rented room nearby. He had never been inside a bookshop that felt so peaceful. "Would you mind," he asked carefully, "if I brought my laptop and worked here sometimes?" Margaret hesitated, and then she put a chair by the window.

Within a month, others followed. A retired fisherman started coming on Fridays to read the newspaper. Two students borrowed the corner for a study group. Nobody spent much money, but the shop was no longer silent, and people began to ask whether she might sell coffee.

Margaret did not become rich, and she knew that the street would never return to what it had been. Yet when her daughter called again, she said she was not ready to sell. "Not yet," she told her. "There are still people who need somewhere to sit."

The next day, she opened at nine o'clock sharp, and for the first time in years there was a small queue at the door.
