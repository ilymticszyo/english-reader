In many countries, the usual path from school leads straight to university. But a growing number of young people decide to wait. They take a "gap year", a period of twelve months between school and further study, which they spend working, travelling, or volunteering. The idea began in Britain in the 1960s, and it has now spread around the world. Is it a smart investment or an expensive delay?

Supporters point to personal growth. A young person who spends a year working abroad or helping in a community project has to solve problems alone, deal with people from different backgrounds, and manage money. These experiences can build independence and confidence, and they often help students to understand what they truly want to study. Many universities report that students who have taken a gap year are more motivated, and some research suggests that they achieve slightly better results.

It can also help to prevent a very common problem. Some students go directly to university because it is expected of them, and then discover that they have chosen the wrong subject. They may drop out after a year, having spent a lot of money. A gap year gives them time to think, and, if they work, to save towards the costs of study.

Critics, however, raise several concerns. The first is cost. A year of travelling can be very expensive, and the students who benefit most are often those whose families can pay for it. This means that the gap year may increase the advantages that already exist. Another worry is that some young people lose the habit of studying, and find it difficult to return to the classroom. A few never do, although studies suggest that most gap-year students do go on to university.

Others question the value of some activities. Short volunteer projects in poor communities, for instance, are sometimes more useful to the visitor than to the people they claim to help. A programme that sends untrained teenagers to teach or build for a few weeks may not leave anything lasting behind. Such projects need to be chosen carefully, and the best ones are those that last long enough to make a real difference.

So how can students get the most from the experience? Experts recommend planning ahead. Decide what you want to achieve: earning money, learning a language, or trying a possible career. Arrange a place at university before you leave, if that is possible, so that you can enjoy the year without worry. Keep a mixture of activities, and include some paid work, as it teaches practical skills that employers value.

There is no right answer for everyone. For students who are exhausted after years of exams or unsure of their goals, a year away may be the best decision they ever make. For others, who know exactly what they want, going straight ahead may be better.

What matters most is not the gap itself, but how it is used. A year of purpose can be worth more than a year of routine.
