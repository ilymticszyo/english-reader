Ask people to describe a smell from their childhood, and many will talk about food: a grandmother's soup, bread in the oven, spices on a festival day. Food is more than a way to feed our bodies. It is one of the strongest ways in which a culture keeps its memory alive.

Take the case of migrants. When people move to a new country, they bring very little with them, but they often bring their recipes. In their new homes, these dishes become a link to the place that they have left. A woman who has not seen her village for thirty years may cook the same meal that her mother cooked, and, for an hour, the kitchen becomes a different country. For the children who grow up there, these meals are often their first and most lasting lesson in who they are and where their family comes from.

Recipes also carry history. The food that people eat today often contains traces of old trade routes, wars, and discoveries. Tomatoes, now essential to Italian cooking, came from the Americas only about five hundred years ago. Chillies, which are central to the food of India and Thailand, arrived in Asia in the same way. A single dish can tell the story of a journey that crossed an ocean.

Traditional cooking is also a form of knowledge that is passed down without books. Many older cooks do not use written recipes, and their measurements are "a handful" or "until it looks right". This kind of knowledge can only be learned by watching and by doing, and when a cook dies without teaching anyone, an entire tradition can disappear. For this reason, some communities and researchers have begun to record the methods of elderly cooks on video, and the United Nations has recognised some traditional food cultures as part of the world's cultural heritage.

At the same time, food is never fixed. It changes as people move, as ingredients become available or disappear, and as new generations make their own choices. Some people fear that globalisation will make all food the same, and it is true that the same fast-food companies now operate in almost every country. But there are signs of the opposite as well. Many cities have more varied food than ever before, and young cooks are mixing old methods with new flavours, to create dishes that belong to more than one tradition.

This raises an interesting question: who owns a dish? Arguments often arise when a famous chef presents a traditional meal in a new way, or when a restaurant claims to have invented something that families have made for centuries. Perhaps the fairest answer is that a recipe belongs to the people who keep it alive.

So the next time you sit down to a family meal, remember that you are not only eating. You are taking part in a conversation that is hundreds of years old.
